## Chapter 9: Gunpowder, Bureaucracy, and Mass Mobilization

At Tophane, on the European shore of Istanbul, the work of war was arranged indoors before it reached a battlefield. The arsenal stood beside the water, so guns, timber, charcoal, and metal could arrive by vessel; roads and workshops connected it to the capital’s foundries and powder stores. During the reign of Selim II, as the Ottoman fleet prepared to recover Tunis from Spanish control, clerks recorded the materials required for the expedition: bronze pieces, iron shot, powder, carriages, tools, and the men assigned to cast, repair, move, and fire them. The inventory described violence before it occurred, in the form the state needed to make it possible.

The Ottoman conquest of Tunis in 1574 is often remembered through its commanders and its result. Sinan Pasha’s fleet crossed from the eastern Mediterranean, and the Spanish garrison at La Goleta, the fortress guarding the approach to Tunis, was reduced after bombardment and assault. The city returned to Ottoman control, four years after Don Juan of Austria had seized it for Philip II. Yet the expedition’s decisive object was neither the soldier’s sword nor the admiral’s courage. It was the artillery train: the guns, powder, shot, vessels, crews, and written orders that brought force to the fortress.

An Ottoman artillery inventory gives the campaign a different shape. It counts pieces by size and use, separates serviceable guns from those needing repair, and places each object inside a chain of custody. A cannon appears not as an isolated invention but as a numbered burden. Its weight determines the cart or vessel required to carry it; its bore determines the shot cast for it; its location determines who must guard it and which official must account for its loss. Powder appears in measures, and powder consumed at one battery must be replaced from a magazine supplied through other offices. The list turns metal into an administrative relationship. This was the work of the Tophane corps, named for the cannon foundry and arsenal in Istanbul. Artisans cast bronze guns, moulded shot, prepared carriages, and maintained the equipment that allowed artillery to operate beyond the capital. The corps included specialists whose status differed from that of ordinary infantrymen, and its routines depended on skilled labour accumulated over generations. When a barrel split, a carriage failed, or a shipment of powder arrived damp, the problem belonged at once to a workshop, a treasury, a transport officer, and a commander in the field. The inventory preserved those dependencies by making the gun answerable to offices and men.

A fortress could be battered only when the state supplied the artilleryman who knew the piece, the powder-maker who prepared the charge, the animal that hauled the carriage, and the clerk who released the stores. At La Goleta, the guns contributed to an Ottoman victory that depended on the fortress’s strength, the Spanish defenders’ resistance, naval control, infantry assaults, and sustained provisioning. Gunpowder enlarged the work before it enlarged the result.

Selim III confronted the same problem more than two centuries later. His reform programme, the Nizam-i Cedid, followed the Ottoman defeat by Russia and Austria in the wars ending in 1792. The sultan and his advisers sought an army drilled with firearms, supported by regular pay, equipped from central magazines, and protected from the irregular forces whose privileges made reform dangerous. Ahmed Cevdet Pasha, writing in the nineteenth century, presented the reform record as a sequence of decrees, appointments, revenues, barracks, and resistance. His account shows a ruler trying to make soldiers, money, buildings, and obedience fit together.

The new troops trained at Levend Farm, north of the Golden Horn, under European-influenced regulations. Their uniforms distinguished them from older corps, and their exercises required repeated firing and coordinated movement. A musket was useful only when a state could provide flints, cartridges, cleaning tools, replacement barrels, and wages while recruits learned to use it. The Nizam-i Cedid therefore required a separate treasury, the Irad-i Cedid, because existing revenues were already claimed by older military and administrative interests. That treasury exposed the political cost of technical improvement. The sultan’s government had to identify taxable resources, assign collectors, supervise expenditure, and prevent provincial officials from absorbing the funds. Reform created offices that threatened other offices. The Janissaries, whose corporate power rested partly on control of military service and urban influence, saw the new force as a challenge. In 1807 opposition in Istanbul ended the reform, and Selim was deposed.

Ahmed Cevdet’s account preserves the sequence hidden by the language of military revolution. A decision to reform generated a fund; the fund required assessment; assessment required officials; officials needed records; records exposed evasion; enforcement provoked resistance. The army existed only if each link held. When the political coalition broke, uniforms and drill books could not preserve it.

The 1574 inventory and the Nizam-i Cedid record belong to different centuries, yet both ask who stores the powder, pays the crew, counts the barrels, and compels a village, workshop, or provincial governor to deliver what a campaign requires. At Tophane, the answers were embedded in an imperial arsenal. Under Selim III, they were being rebuilt against established interests. In each case, the gun’s range depended on paperwork reaching beyond the place where the gun stood.

The reach could begin with a tax collected far from the capital. In France, the taille was assessed on those legally liable for the principal direct tax, connecting the royal treasury to parishes, local officials, and the intendants who increasingly reported to the crown. A village supported the frontier by sending money, grain, horses, carts, or a young man placed under military obligation. Officials gathered and redirected these contributions until they became campaigning power.

The sequence was practical. A royal order fixed a levy or demanded supplies; local officers apportioned the burden; collectors converted claims into coin or provisions; treasury officials paid contractors and regiments; magazines stored the powder and food; wagons and river boats carried them to the army. Between the parish and the firing line lay months of accounting. The taille made war repeatable because it could be demanded again.

The Dutch Republic used another route. Its provincial governments borrowed against future tax income through public debt, and Amsterdam’s financial market allowed the States of Holland to raise money before taxes had been collected. Bondholders accepted interest because the provincial authorities possessed credible revenue sources and political institutions gave creditors reason to expect payment. A warship or regiment could be funded in the present by pledging the future.

Public credit altered tempo as well as scale. The Dutch could hire troops, maintain fortifications, and equip fleets without waiting for a single harvest or extraordinary collection. Interest became a military expense, while excise duties, customs charges, and provincial taxes secured the creditors’ confidence. The system did not remove coercion from the countryside; it made coercion durable and less visible at the moment when the army was assembled.

Mughal campaigning rested on a different structure. Under the mansabdari system, a mansab ranked an officer and specified obligations in cavalry and pay. A jagir assigned revenue from particular territories in lieu of a simple salary, while imperial officials inspected horses and recorded the strength a commander was expected to maintain. An officer’s military standing thus depended on a chain joining the emperor’s order to a district’s land revenue and a cavalryman’s mount.

The Mughal state did not need to imitate France or Holland to mobilize at scale. Its armies moved through negotiated extraction, cash payments, service obligations, local intermediaries, and the resources of fertile regions such as the Punjab and the Gangetic plain. Revenue was assessed, assigned, collected, and transformed into men, animals, powder, tents, food, and transport. Where the conversion broke, an army arrived late, unpaid, or hungry.

Gunpowder sat inside these systems rather than replacing them. Powder spoiled in damp storage; shot had to match a gun’s bore; bridges and roads determined whether heavy artillery could follow an advancing force. A treasury could purchase weapons and still fail to make them useful. A register could name ten thousand cavalry and conceal that many horses were unfit. Campaigning power was a chain of deliveries, and every delivery gave an official an opportunity to extract, delay, falsify, or refuse. That chain made firearms valuable to governments able to standardize obedience, but it did not make every government equally strong. The Ottoman arsenal supported an expedition across the Mediterranean, while Selim III could not force a durable settlement with the Janissaries. Dutch credit financed prolonged war, but depended on provincial consent and commercial taxation. Mughal ranks organized immense forces, while court politics and jagirdar assignments could disrupt the expected supply of troops. The weapon travelled widely; the administrative solution did not.

Michael Roberts’s account of a seventeenth-century Military Revolution placed changes in drill, tactics, fortification, and army size at the centre of European history. Geoffrey Parker enlarged the argument by emphasizing the trace italienne, siege warfare, and the financial demands of larger forces. The debate is most useful when it follows the records rather than treating transformation as sudden and uniform. Cannon did not erase cavalry, muskets did not instantly produce disciplined infantry, and fortresses became harder to take even as their construction grew more expensive.

The siege of Nagashima in Japan shows why altered coercive scale should not be confused with a new motive. Oda Nobunaga attacked the fortified Ikko-ikki strongholds in the delta of the Kiso, Nagara, and Ibi Rivers during the 1570s. The defenders included religious communities, armed peasants, and warriors resisting Nobunaga’s consolidation of power. Waterways, embankments, stockades, and islands made movement difficult. Firearms gave Nobunaga’s forces greater striking power, but the siege still depended on encirclement, boats, food denial, and the destruction of people who could no longer be treated as a negotiating community.

After earlier campaigns failed, Nobunaga tightened the attack in 1574. His men built positions, cut routes, and used fire against the compounds. When defenders surrendered or were trapped, the conquering army narrowed the distinction between combatant and population. The burning of Nagashima’s strongholds killed large numbers, including monks and families associated with the movement. The arquebus allowed Nobunaga’s established intention toward the Ikko-ikki to be applied across a fortified landscape with greater regularity.

English Civil War records make the same point from another direction. In 1642 and 1643, royalist and parliamentary forces complained about the shortage, price, and movement of powder. Committees in Cheshire and Yorkshire sought saltpetre, lead, match, and firearms; merchants carried supplies through ports; local officials requisitioned transport; arsenals and magazines became strategic objects. A regiment without powder might possess colours, officers, and courage while remaining unable to hold a position.

The parliamentary ordinance of 1645 that created the New Model Army did not simply place better guns in soldiers’ hands. It concentrated recruitment, pay, command, and supply under a national structure. The army’s success at Naseby in June 1645 depended on cavalry and infantry tactics, but those tactics assumed regular ammunition, drilled formations, and officers able to direct men after smoke and noise had broken the battlefield into fragments. Powder was the condition under which command could persist.

The Military Revolution debate is most persuasive when it follows invoices and rosters rather than celebrating technical novelty. Firearms changed the cost of defence, the size of supply systems, and the punishment a government could inflict over distance. They changed motives less reliably. Nobunaga’s enemies were not made more dangerous by the matchlock; the English Parliament did not become more principled because it formed a disciplined army; Selim III’s reformers did not discover a new appetite for violence.

The dependable change lay in repetition. A bow required training and physical strength, while a firearm could be issued in standardized form to men whose skill was uneven, provided officers imposed drill and kept ammunition available. A cannon could breach a wall that resisted ladders, provided engineers placed it, gunners served it, and powder reached it. These conditions lowered barriers to organized harm. They did not determine whom rulers would classify as rebel, heretic, subject, enemy, or expendable.

A government able to count households, borrow money, collect taxes, store powder, and move troops could make coercion routine. That reach might defend a frontier, suppress a revolt, or massacre a besieged population. Technology widened the field of possible action; decrees and commanders selected the target. The Ottoman arsenal, the French levy, the Dutch bond, the Mughal rank, and the Nagashima siege belong in one argument because each joins a weapon to an institution without becoming the same story.

By 1793, the French National Convention gave that process a new vocabulary. Its decree of 23 August declared that the French people were placed in permanent requisition for the defence of the republic. Young men were to fight, married men to forge weapons and carry provisions, women to make clothing and tend the wounded, children to prepare lint, and older men to sustain public courage and speak of hatred for kings. The decree sorted a population by age, sex, skill, and political duty as it called for soldiers.

In Paris the words became law through committees and ministries; in the departments they became lists, quotas, requisitions, workshops, and arrests for refusal. The levée en masse joined military service to national belonging. A person’s body, labour, household goods, and speech could all be drawn into the emergency. Gunpowder warfare had developed within fiscal states; revolutionary mobilisation declared the entire nation a military resource.

The older systems had prepared the machinery. The taille taught officials to locate taxable persons. Dutch credit showed how future revenue could sustain present war. Mughal service ranks connected imperial command to local extraction. The Convention added an ideological claim that made compliance a test of citizenship. A musket in the hands of a conscript was the endpoint of a process involving a census, a decree, a district officer, a workshop, a requisitioned horse, and a political definition of treason.

The decree’s violence began before anyone fired. It compelled communities to surrender men and goods and made resistance punishable as disloyalty to the nation. Its scale came from the administrative form through which the claim travelled. The Convention’s words reached villages that could not have been reached by personal command from Paris because commissioners, municipal councils, and military agents carried them into local records.

Across the century and a half from Nagashima to the French levée, firearms did not produce a single moral direction. They strengthened those who could join metal to money, money to records, and records to compulsory service. The resulting armies could defend a state, conquer one, or turn its inhabitants into a category of supply. At Magdeburg, the chain ran through gates, magazines, batteries, and the crowded streets of a city that had become an object in a war of confessions and princes.

In the German lands, armies lived from the countryside even when rulers claimed to maintain them through taxation. Villages were ordered to provide quarters, grain, cattle, carts, and fodder; soldiers took what officials failed to deliver; harvests were trampled or abandoned; people fled toward woods, fortified towns, and territories believed safer. The famine of the 1630s was intensified by harvest failures and disease, but war turned shortage into displacement by deciding whose fields could be stripped and whose stores protected.

Magdeburg’s destruction on 20 May 1631 entered the records through military reports, clerical accounts, municipal registers, and later histories such as the *Theatrum Europaeum*. The city had resisted imperial authority and hoped for Swedish assistance. Tilly’s army, after the walls fell, killed inhabitants, seized property, and allowed fire to move through the streets. The dead were not only the consequence of artillery breaching a fortification. They were also the result of a command that treated political refusal as a problem to be crushed and gave soldiers access to the city’s houses.

German depopulation records preserve the slower aftermath. In parts of Brandenburg, Württemberg, Pomerania, and the Palatinate, parish books show missing households, deserted farms, sudden gaps in baptisms, and years in which burials rose while marriages fell. Numbers vary sharply because clerks disappeared, boundaries changed, and later writers converted local losses into broad regional estimates. The entries nevertheless reveal people moving under pressure: a family absent from one parish, a field leased to a stranger, a village unable to restore its tax quota.

When famine came, the damaged tax base became another pressure on survival. Armies demanded provisions from districts already short of seed grain; landlords raised claims on tenants who had lost animals; towns restricted entry to protect their stores. A person leaving Magdeburg or a burned village did not enter an empty landscape but a jurisdiction deciding whether to admit, feed, tax, or expel them.

The first shots came before dawn on 20 May 1631, while Dietrich von Falkenberg was still in the commandant’s house near the market. Tilly’s batteries had fired through the night, and the stones of the streets trembled under them. At the Kröcken Gate, where the Elbe entered the city through the eastern wall, musketeers waited behind the parapet with their matches glowing red in the dark. Beyond the ditch, Imperial and Catholic League infantry gathered in the smoke.

Falkenberg had been sent to Magdeburg by Gustavus Adolphus to organise its defence. He wore a breastplate beneath his coat and carried a sword, though the city’s councillors had argued that the walls could not be held against Tilly’s army without Swedish relief. The Swedish king was not there. His army remained far to the west, leaving Magdeburg with its burghers, its garrison, and a few hundred soldiers who had come in under Falkenberg’s command.

The alarm bells began at the cathedral and ran outward through the streets. Women pulled children from beds. Men carried powder kegs towards the gates and drove carts against doors and narrow passages. In the parish of St. Ulrich, a baker left dough rising in a trough and took a pike from the wall. The registers would later show houses by the hundreds without inhabitants, but that morning their inhabitants were still inside them, fastening shutters and searching for water.

At the Kröcken Gate the first Imperial troops crossed the ditch. Falkenberg rode towards the breach with a small body of defenders. He had reached the rampart and was directing men towards the smoke when a musket ball struck him. He fell near the gate, and the men around him carried his body into a house. By his own account, Otto von Guericke, then a councillor of Magdeburg, saw the assault spread along the walls while soldiers forced their way into the suburbs.

The defenders fired from windows and roofs. Tilly’s men broke into the city behind the storming parties, and the fighting moved from the ramparts to the streets. The powder magazine at the New Market exploded, throwing beams and tiles across the houses. Fire rose in several quarters at once. Wind drove it through timber-framed buildings faster than people could draw water from the wells.

Count Tilly entered after the gates had been opened. His chaplain later wrote that the city was filled with cries, smoke, and flame. Soldiers dragged people from cellars; others took silver vessels, clothing, and food from houses already burning. At the cathedral, where many inhabitants had crowded for shelter, men were killed in the nave and among the tombs. The convent of Our Lady became a refuge until soldiers forced their way inside.

By afternoon the streets were impassable. The cathedral alone remained standing above the smoke, while Magdeburg burned from the Elbe to the western walls and thousands of its inhabitants lay dead.
