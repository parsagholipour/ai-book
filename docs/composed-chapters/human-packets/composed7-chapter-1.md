## Chapter 1: What Do We Mean by Human Aggression?

At a prehistoric burial site, a healed fracture and a lethal wound can lie in the same soil, separated by centuries or by a single season. They invite different questions: what happened to these people, and what can anyone responsibly infer from the traces they left behind? A broken bone that knitted under the skin records pain, care, and survival, but not the quarrel, accident, raid, or punishment that caused it. A point lodged in a rib records a weapon meeting a body; it does not, by itself, tell us whether the hand belonged to an enemy, a neighbour, a guard, or a relative. The mark is physical. Its meaning has to be recovered from history.

The reports of the Chicago Commission on Race Relations begin from a world in which such meanings were contested in the open. During the summer of 1919, Chicago’s South Side drew Black migrants from the American South into a city whose industries needed their labour and whose neighbourhoods, beaches, transport lines, police districts, and political wards were divided by race. On Sunday, July 27, Eugene Williams, a seventeen-year-old Black boy, drifted across the line that white bathers treated as their boundary at the city’s Twenty-Ninth Street beach. The Commission’s account places his death at the centre of what followed. Williams was struck by a stone, disappeared beneath the water, and drowned. Black witnesses and bystanders pointed to the white man who had thrown the stone and asked the police to arrest him. The officer did not do so. That refusal became an event of its own. Word travelled from the beach into streets already tense with hostility, and groups of Black and white residents confronted one another near the shoreline and along the routes leading back toward the South Side. The testimony does not turn the afternoon into a single eruption with one cause. It records accusations, rumours, assaults, gunfire, and beatings, but also follows men who entered streets in groups because a solitary person was vulnerable there. Witnesses described particular attacks, carried the injured, watched police officers intervene, or became caught inside the circulation of stories that made retaliation seem immediate. A street could change character between one corner and the next.

Men attacked people identified by colour. White crowds moved into Black neighbourhoods, while Black crowds defended themselves or struck back where they believed white attackers were approaching. Streetcars became sites of inspection and danger. Passengers were pulled from vehicles, beaten, or shot at; drivers and conductors faced the immediate question of whether to continue along a route that had become unsafe. The city’s infrastructure remained in place, but the riot gave it another use. Tracks carried cars into contested districts, and streets connecting homes to factories also carried armed groups towards their targets.

The word “riot” flattens these encounters when it stands alone. The Commission’s report supplies a more difficult picture, distinguishing attacks committed by groups from conflicts between individuals and describing firearms alongside stones and clubs. Black stockyard workers, white labourers, policemen, soldiers, tenants, shopkeepers, and passers-by appear in testimony as people occupying different positions within the same emergency. Some carried weapons. Some hid. Some attempted to rescue an injured person. Others used the confusion to settle a grievance or punish someone who had become a racial symbol rather than a known individual. The testimony keeps returning to the question of how a person became a target: through a known act, a rumour, an appearance, or the group to which others assigned him.

The governor called in the Illinois National Guard. Soldiers entered the city as the violence continued, and their presence altered the streets before it ended the causes that had made them dangerous. The report records the official count of thirty-eight deaths and more than five hundred injuries, with Black and white Chicagoans among the dead. It also records damage to homes, businesses, and livelihoods, as well as the displacement of residents whose neighbourhoods had become exposed to attack. Those numbers establish the episode’s scale, but they do not make every wound equivalent. A fatal assault at the beach, an armed defence of a block, an attack on a passing streetcar, and the deployment of troops belonged to different kinds of action even when they occurred within the same week.

Who could stand on a beach, enter a street, board a car, call the police, or expect an officer to treat a complaint as legitimate? The report’s testimony repeatedly returns to those questions. A stone thrown into the water did not create Chicago’s racial boundaries. Housing practices, the labour market, policing, and political habits had already placed people inside them. Williams’s killing made those boundaries visible at a particular spot, and the police response helped determine whose account would count as an actionable fact. By August 3, the Guard had helped restore a measure of order. The Commission did not treat that restoration as a moral resolution. Its investigation asked how a city that claimed to be governed by law had permitted racial violence to spread, connecting the immediate confrontations to segregation, employment, housing, and the unequal distribution of protection.

Chicago in 1919 gives us a first distinction. Aggression may be felt and expressed by individuals, but the scale and direction of violence depend on the streets people may enter, the groups to which they are assigned, the weapons available, and the officials who decide when a grievance becomes a crime.

Anger is not the same as aggression. Anger names an emotional state, often produced by perceived insult, threat, frustration, or unfairness; aggression names conduct directed towards harm, control, or intimidation. The difference is plain in the Chicago testimony. Some people were enraged and did not attack. Others joined a group, carried a weapon, blocked a route, or beat a stranger whose only identifying feature was his race. Interpersonal violence includes physical injury, but it also includes threats, stalking, isolation, and the use of money or dependence to maintain control. A history concerned with aggression must attend to more than the visible blow while asking who acted, against whom, under what permission, and with what expected consequence.

The Roman material gives this problem another shape because it preserves a vocabulary for sorting injuries according to their object, setting, and remedy. The Twelve Tables survive only through later quotations and paraphrases, yet their fragments show that early Roman law did not treat every harmful act as one undifferentiated form of violence. Table VIII, in the traditional arrangement, speaks of injury in terms that join the damaged body to a rule of retaliation. If a person broke another’s limb, the injured party could accept the talio, the like-for-like penalty, unless the parties agreed on compensation. A broken bone was not simply a private sorrow. It was an injury that could call forth a socially recognised response. The same table assigns different payments to different harms. The traditional text gives three hundred asses for the broken bone of a free person and one hundred and fifty for that of a slave. For lesser injury, it gives twenty-five asses. These figures expose the legal order’s assumptions about status, but they also show an attempt to move from unbounded retaliation towards a scale of valuation. The law places bodily damage within a schedule that can be counted. It does not remove anger from the injured person’s world; it gives the injury a form that another party can assess. That conversion appears in the fragment concerning a tooth knocked out of another person’s mouth. The table’s language is terse, and its later transmission makes exact reconstruction difficult, but the surrounding provisions show concern with iniuria, an affront or injury to the person, as well as with wounds that could be measured more materially. Roman law was not asking only how much blood had been spilled. It was asking whether a body had been damaged, whether an insult had been offered, whether property had been taken, and whether the parties stood as free persons or slaves. A tooth, a limb, and a lesser injury entered the legal record through different valuations.

The early law’s treatment of retaliation is often read as evidence of a primitive violence waiting to be civilised. That reading misses the legal work performed by the rule. Talio could authorise a severe response, yet it also confined the response to a stated relation between injury and penalty. The injured party did not possess an unlimited licence to continue the quarrel. Compensation could substitute for retaliation, and the settlement could be spoken, witnessed, and remembered as a transaction. The rule did not make Roman society gentle. It gave a private injury a recognised route towards settlement and made the limits of retaliation a matter for communal judgment.

Roman jurists later developed more elaborate distinctions around force. In the Digest, they discuss vis publica and vis privata, public and private violence, in relation to the use of force, the seizure of property, and resistance to lawful or unlawful acts. The surviving commentary does not praise every force exercised by officials, nor does it treat every private use of force as identical. The category of public force assumes that state officers may compel obedience in circumstances recognised by law, while also implying that force becomes wrongful when an office or procedure is exceeded. Private force identifies coercion undertaken without that public standing, even when the person using it claims to be defending a right.

The jurist therefore asks more than whether someone was hurt. Who used force? In whose name? Towards what end? Under which rule? A magistrate’s order, a soldier’s action, a creditor’s seizure, a household head’s command, and a robber’s assault could all impose themselves upon a body, but Roman law placed them in different moral and legal compartments. The compartments were unequal and often harsh. They did not protect everyone in the same way. Their existence shows that Roman writers understood violence as organised by status and office rather than as a simple measure of temperament.

The Twelve Tables and the later juristic commentary belong to different moments in Roman legal history, and they should not be made to speak as one unchanged voice. The tables are remembered through later authors and fragments. The jurists wrote within an imperial legal culture in which public offices, military power, slavery, and imperial command had expanded. A rule about a broken limb and a commentary on public force answer related questions without belonging to the same institutional world. The distance between them is evidence of change: Roman society repeatedly revised the relation between injury, compensation, retaliation, and coercion.

The Roman distinction between private injury and public force also helps explain why a state may suppress one form of violence while authorising another. A person who struck a neighbour without legal standing could face a penalty, while a magistrate, soldier, or officer could use force under a recognised command. Chicago’s National Guard offers a modern instance of the same structural problem, though its racial setting, constitutional language, and political circumstances were different. Troops could restrain street attacks while the city’s police and housing practices had helped produce the unequal conditions in which those attacks occurred. The intervention restored public order without erasing the institutions that had distributed protection unevenly.

The words used for aggression must remain attached to these differences. Interpersonal aggression concerns conduct between persons, often within relationships of dependence or control. Raiding describes an organised incursion for capture, plunder, livestock, slaves, or destruction, but it does not by itself tell us whether the raiders belonged to a state, a lineage, a military company, or a temporary alliance. Warfare involves organised armed conflict between political communities or armed groups; its legal names and ritual boundaries vary widely. Policing is the use of authorised force to regulate conduct and secure public order, though the authorisation may be contested and the target may be selected through racial, class, religious, or colonial categories. Atrocity names severe violence directed against civilians or other vulnerable populations, often with a political or identity-based purpose, and should not be used as a loose synonym for all killing. These are working distinctions, not five levels on a ladder. A raid can become warfare. Warfare can include policing of occupied populations. Policing can become atrocity when officials use public power to terrorise or destroy a marked group. An interpersonal assault may occur during a war and acquire a political meaning that it would not have had in a private quarrel. The same weapon can appear in a robbery or a battle; its presence does not settle the category. The category depends on organisation, authority, target, purpose, and the evidence by which those features can be established.

Comparison becomes difficult when sources measure unlike things. A Roman law table records the terms under which an injury could be compensated. The Chicago Commission records testimony collected after a week of racial violence. A cemetery may preserve wounds without preserving the dispute that caused them. A military chronicle may count victories and captives while ignoring the people who fled. The absence of a number in one source cannot be placed beside the presence of a number in another as though both were measurements made by the same instrument. Each record was produced for a different reason and gives shape to the violence it preserves.

Cross-cultural comparison does not require a single scale. Historians can compare how a community defined an injury, who was authorised to use force, or how a government responded when private violence threatened public order. They can trace how a new weapon changed the distance between attacker and victim, how a road or river changed the reach of a force, or how a religious and political identity turned neighbours into enemies. Such comparisons become persuasive when they preserve the arrangements that made the violence possible. The Chicago testimony should not be detached from segregated housing and racial policing in order to serve as a generic example of crowd violence; Roman legal language should not be detached from status and office in order to serve as a generic measure of restraint.

The scale of a death toll is one fact, not the meaning of an event. Chicago’s thirty-eight recorded deaths tell us something important about the riot, but they do not rank the city against a Roman campaign, a village raid, or an episode of colonial conquest. A law prescribing twenty-five asses for a lesser injury tells us that Roman jurists categorised bodily harm and compensation; it does not reveal how often such injuries occurred, how many went unreported, or how consistently a poor claimant could obtain payment. The number belongs inside the source that produced it.

Economic pressure enters these histories through concrete needs and unequal choices. Competition for work brought Black migrants to Chicago’s stockyards and factories, while employers and white workers contested the terms of that labour. Scarcity, debt, land, livestock, and access to trade routes have often made violence tempting or profitable. Yet pressure does not move directly from an empty granary or a low wage to a wound. Political leaders identify enemies, courts define property, police select whose safety deserves protection, and communities teach people which losses require revenge. Material hardship gains destructive force through ideas about belonging and through offices that can enforce those ideas.

Technology changes the reach of aggression without supplying its purpose. A stone at the Twenty-Ninth Street beach required proximity and a crowd. A revolver extended danger beyond the reach of the hand. A streetcar carried strangers through a contested neighbourhood and made the vehicle itself a moving boundary. Roman roads, writing, accounting, and military equipment allowed commands and supplies to travel farther than an individual could walk, but none of these tools independently decided whom Romans would conquer or punish. Tools alter speed, distance, endurance, and organisation. People and institutions select targets.

The historian who begins with human nature may find confirmation everywhere because every society contains quarrels, injuries, threats, and killing. The historian who begins with progress may find a decline in violence whenever private revenge is replaced by courts, even when courts and armies enlarge the state’s ability to coerce. The evidence supports neither a timeless human substance nor an inevitable movement towards peace. It shows people acting within settings that differ in law, resources, technology, status, belief, and political choice. Those settings have to be reconstructed before societies can be compared.

A comparison should begin with the question a source is able to answer. What did the Chicago Commission collect from witnesses, and how did its investigators organise those accounts? What injury did the Roman table name, what compensation did it prescribe, and what relation between private harm and public force did later jurists construct? When the questions remain close to the documents, differences become more revealing than a general verdict that one society was violent and another restrained.

The archive is full of such decisions. Someone copied the Twelve Tables into a later discussion. Someone asked Chicago residents to testify, selected questions, preserved statements, and assembled them into a report intended to influence public policy. A magistrate’s record might preserve a charge because the case entered a court; a victim’s fear might remain in a household and leave no written trace. Archaeology can show a healed fracture, a burned layer, a mass grave, a weapon, or a defensive wall. It can establish that bodies were injured or that a settlement was destroyed. It rarely supplies the exact words spoken before an attack, the identities assigned to enemies, or the reasons a person chose to flee rather than fight. This unevenness cannot be translated neatly into peace. A community with no written record may have experienced less organised violence, or it may have used forms of aggression that left little durable evidence. A state with extensive archives may appear more violent because it counted, classified, and preserved its punishments. The Roman jurist’s page and the Chicago investigator’s testimony are signs not only of injury but of record-making. A society’s ways of naming harm affect what later generations can see.

Archaeological inference has a corresponding limit. A concentration of weapons may indicate conflict, trade, ritual display, or accumulation at a place of authority. Defensive walls may have guarded against armies, raiders, animals, floods, or imagined enemies. A burial with wounds can reveal violence without revealing whether the dead were attackers, defenders, captives, or victims of an internal dispute. The inference grows firmer when several kinds of evidence converge, but that convergence has to be demonstrated rather than assumed.

The question remains open: when the records grow thin, are we seeing less violence, a different violence, or merely weaker preservation?
