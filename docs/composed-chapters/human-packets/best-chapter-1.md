## Chapter 1: What Do We Mean by Human Aggression?

> “A Khan is no sooner overcome than they cut off his [ 10 ] head; his children are treated in the same manner, and all his subjects belong to the conqueror.”
>
> — Montesquieu, *The Spirit of Laws (1758)/Book XVIII*

The five hundred and one jurors were already seated when Socrates came into the court beside the Royal Stoa in Athens. It was the spring of 399 BCE, in the archonship of Laches, and the morning light fell across the packed earth before the stoa, where men stood in knots beneath the colonnade waiting to hear the case. Socrates was seventy years old. He wore no lawyer’s robe and had brought no written speech. His feet were bare. The dikasts held their voting discs in readiness, bronze for acquittal and another colour for conviction, though the urns stood empty for now.

Meletus rose first. He was a young poet, narrow-bearded, with straight hair falling toward his cheeks. Anytus, a wealthy leather manufacturer and influential democrat, sat among the accusers; Lycon, who spoke for the rhetoricians, stood near him. The indictment had been entered in the name of Meletus: Socrates did not acknowledge the gods of the city, introduced new divine beings, and corrupted the young.

The clerk read the charge aloud.

Socrates turned toward the jurors. He had been accused before, he said, by men who had spoken against him for years without ever appearing in court. Those earlier accusers had told the Athenians that Socrates was a clever man who studied things beneath the earth and above the heavens, made the weaker argument stronger, and taught others to do the same. He could not name them all. Some were in the audience, smiling.

Aristophanes had put him in a basket in the *Clouds* twenty-four years earlier, hanging above the stage and measuring the length of a flea’s leap. The play had been a comedy, but Socrates said that its picture of him had remained in the minds of the jurors. He asked them to listen not to a polished speech but to whatever words came to him.

Meletus answered his questions badly. Socrates asked whether Meletus believed that all Athenians improved the young except Socrates alone. Meletus said that was so. Socrates asked whether the same held for horses, or whether only a few trainers improved them while most people spoiled them. The jurors murmured. Meletus said that Socrates corrupted the young deliberately.

Then Socrates asked whether, if he did so deliberately, he would not make his companions worse and thereby expose himself to harm from them. No one, he said, willingly chooses to live among people who may injure him. If the corruption was involuntary, Meletus should have instructed him privately rather than bringing him to court.

The questions moved on to the gods. Meletus accused Socrates of believing in no gods at all and also of believing in divine things. Socrates drew the contradiction out in front of the jurors: a man could not believe in the activities of horses without believing in horses, or in music without believing in musicians. Meletus grew angry. The clerk called for order.

Outside the court, the Agora was opening. Donkeys passed toward the market with panniers of olives. From the direction of the Acropolis came the smell of smoke from morning sacrifices. Inside, Socrates spoke of a voice that had opposed him since childhood, never commanding him what to do but turning him back from certain acts. It had not stopped him from entering the court. He told the jurors that he had served Athens as a soldier at Potidaea, Amphipolis, and Delium. At Potidaea, he said, he had remained at his post through the cold and had walked barefoot on the ice. When the generals ordered him forward, he went; when they ordered him back, he came back. In public life he had once resisted the illegal collective trial of the generals after the battle at Arginusae, when the assembly wanted to judge them all at once. Later, under the Thirty, he had refused to arrest Leon of Salamis, though the order had been issued and death might have followed refusal. He would now face the court without begging or bringing his sons into court to pull at the jurors’ pity. The city had laws and procedures, and he had come to answer under them. If they acquitted him on the condition that he stop examining himself and others, he said, he would obey neither the condition nor the command. He would continue asking men who claimed wisdom to account for it.

A murmur passed through the benches. Some jurors leaned forward; others turned away. Socrates said that a life without examination was not worth living.

The clerk called the jurors to the urns. One by one they rose, crossed the court, and dropped their discs. Bronze struck bronze. The count was made: two hundred and eighty votes for conviction, two hundred and twenty-one for acquittal.

Socrates returned to the centre. Under Athenian procedure, the accusers had proposed the penalty. Meletus asked for death. Socrates, asked to name his own punishment, first said that a man who had spent his life benefiting the city deserved public meals in the Prytaneum. Then he proposed a fine of thirty minae, with Plato, Crito, Critobulus, and Apollodorus standing surety.

The jurors voted again.

The presiding archon announced the sentence of death. Socrates turned from the benches toward the doorway, where the sun had climbed above the roofs of the Agora. The court officers led him away.

The charge had been framed around a man’s teaching and the city’s gods. Its machinery was more exact: an indictment entered by one accuser, speeches heard by a body of dikasts, discs dropped into urns, and a penalty fixed by a second vote. Athens could leave aside the universal meaning of Socrates’ conduct and decide whether this man, in this city, under this procedure, could be harmed by public authority.

In London, a century and a quarter after the death of John Hayes, the same vocabulary of guilt gathered around a different scene: a household in turmoil, a body carried away in pieces, neighbours speaking of what they had heard, and officers turning suspicion into a public case. The Old Bailey proceedings for the murder of Hayes in 1722 preserve the event through the language of examination. They do not begin with a theory of violence. They begin with names, occupations, rooms, streets, and questions put to witnesses.

The proceedings make the household visible by following the people who moved through it. A servant had seen or heard something. A neighbour had noticed an absence. Someone had carried an object from one place to another. These details did not arrive in the record as a complete account. Each witness possessed only a portion of the event, and each portion had to be placed beside the others. The court asked where a person had been, what had happened next, and why an ordinary action had acquired a criminal meaning.

Hayes’s case reached the Old Bailey because death had crossed the threshold between private trouble and public jurisdiction. What had happened indoors was tested in a public room before judges and a jury. The witnesses were not simply asked whether they disliked Hayes or whether his companions were bad people. They were asked what they saw, when they saw it, what they had touched, and how the body or the blood had been treated. A household dispute became a sequence. That sequence could extend the violence beyond the blow itself. A blow struck in anger might have lasted an instant and left only the testimony of the person who delivered it and the person who received it. Concealment required further movement. A bundle carried through London could be seen by someone outside the household. An absence could become suspicious when another person remembered the last time Hayes had been present. A statement repeated to a neighbour might reach a constable or a magistrate. The prosecution assigned each movement a place in the legal story, turning minutes of violence into hours and days of evidence.

London supplied the witnesses who made that extension possible. The city was crowded, but its crowding did not make people anonymous in every circumstance. Lodgers knew the habits of other lodgers. Publicans remembered who had entered together. Servants carried information between rooms. A parish knew who belonged to a household and who had recently disappeared. The watch moved through the streets after dark with lanterns and staves, and constables could seize a suspect. A magistrate’s clerk could write down the accusation. The Old Bailey could preserve the resulting testimony in print. The same streets contained several authorities. A household claimed power over servants and dependants. Husbands claimed authority over wives, though that authority was contested in practice and unevenly enforced. Men who quarrelled in alleys or taverns could call on friends, relatives, or neighbours, allowing an insult to travel beyond the first encounter. A feud could continue after one assault because the people involved remained tied by residence, work, debt, or marriage. The watch gave some of these relations a route into a record.

The officers who pursued such cases belonged to the same world as the people they arrested. They were not an impersonal force standing outside London’s violence. Their authority depended on warrants, parish cooperation, the power of the magistrate, and the willingness of witnesses to speak. Once a case entered the Old Bailey, however, the Crown could gather separate recollections and define the injury as an offence against public order. Punishment no longer answered to the victim’s household alone.

Printed proceedings gave the episode a second life. The trial became more than an encounter between the accused and the Crown. It showed readers how murder might hide inside ordinary relations and how the law claimed the right to expose it. Its moral language could be severe, but its practical work was exact. It located a body, a weapon, a journey, and a conversation. The record preserved the steps by which an injury became a felony.

Hayes’s case therefore sits at an important edge. Its immediate setting was interpersonal: people who knew one another, a household, a dispute, and the bodily vulnerability of one person before another. Its afterlife was institutional. The court sorted intention from accident, participation from knowledge, and concealment from grief. It decided which injury counted as a public crime and which people would be treated as authorised speakers about it.

Anger and aggression belong to different parts of that sequence. Anger is an inward condition, rising from insult, fear, frustration, or the belief that one has been wronged. Aggression is conduct directed toward harm or intimidation. A person may tremble with anger and leave without striking. Another may threaten calmly because the threat will secure obedience. Hayes’s case entered the record through conduct, not through any reliable measurement of the accused person’s feeling.

Aggression itself is broader than interpersonal violence. A threat, a calculated act of intimidation, or a psychological pressure can be aggressive even when no bodily force is used. Interpersonal violence concerns physical force or the use of power between individuals or small groups. It includes violence within a family, sexual violence, assault, and the control of a dependent person. The harm may be bodily, but the purpose can be domination as much as injury.

A husband’s confinement of a wife, a master’s beating of a servant, or a creditor’s threat against a debtor belongs to this field even when no battlefield is involved. The relationship supplies the means of coercion. One person knows where another sleeps, depends on another for wages, or controls access to movement and shelter. The act is personal, but its conditions are social. A household, a contract, and a law of status can place force within reach before anger appears.

Raiding changes the scale and the expected purpose. A raiding party crosses a boundary to seize animals, goods, or captives, or to damage a rival community while avoiding the burdens of a full campaign. The intended victims are chosen as members of a group. A raid can grow from a feud. It requires preparation and information about routes, settlements, and the likely response of those being attacked.

The men who ride out may know their targets personally. Their knowledge does not make the act interpersonal in the narrower sense. The raid converts a relationship between particular enemies into an operation against a community’s property or dependants. Its return journey must be organised, and its success depends on the group’s ability to assemble, move, seize, and withdraw. Resources and opportunity shape the violence more decisively than the anger of any one rider.

Warfare adds duration, command, and collective risk. Numbers alone do not make a war. Participants must be assembled for a campaign, supplied, directed, and recognised as fighting on behalf of a political community or claimant. A battle between the armies of two kingdoms belongs here. So may a prolonged conflict among chiefly societies, if fighters are mobilised through established obligations and the violence is understood by those involved as a contest between groups.

The Old Bailey could punish a murderer; it could not by itself describe a raid across a frontier. A war captain, a village elder, and a constable might all order violence, but their powers were not interchangeable. The captain directed fighters toward an enemy. The elder could summon dependants or negotiate compensation. The constable acted through a warrant and the penalties attached to law. Each office joined force to a different social arrangement.

Coercive law is force attached to a rule. Its distinctive feature is not that it is always unjust or always violent, but that obedience is secured through an authorised capacity to harm. The threat may concern imprisonment or the seizure of property; the punishment may be corporal or lethal. What gives it a separate character is the office behind the act. A constable, judge, or ruler harms under institutional authority, which claims that the harm is permitted because a rule has been broken.

In the *Laws of Manu*, compiled in India over several centuries and probably reaching its present form between about the second century BCE and the third century CE, the ruler’s punishment appears as a necessary instrument of order. Book VII places the king amid duties of protection, taxation, judicial action, and war, making rule inseparable from the power to punish. The text joins protection to hierarchy: the authority that promises security also decides whose conduct requires correction and what injury correction may involve.

A law can restrain interpersonal violence while establishing a hierarchy of permitted injury. The same polity that prosecutes a killer may permit a judge to order death, a ruler to seize goods, or a master to discipline someone beneath him in the social order. Status enters the definition of harm. An act directed against a Brahmin, a servant, a woman, an enemy, or a ruler could carry different consequences because the law did not imagine all bodies as equally situated.

Organised atrocity is more than a collection of murders. It is sustained or coordinated violence against civilians or a defined population, carried out through offices and armed groups that divide the work. Genocide, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity belong to this category. The scale is important, but the process is decisive. People must be identified, marked, expelled, confined, deprived, or killed through decisions that link leaders to the people who carry out those decisions.

A massacre can occur in war without becoming an organised atrocity in the fuller sense. An atrocity may proceed through starvation, forced removal, or imprisonment before mass killing begins. The necessary question is how separate acts become a programme. Who names the population? Who issues the order? Who transports the victims? Who records the property? Those questions lead away from the temperature of individual anger and toward the offices that make repeated harm possible.

Lawrence Keeley’s *War Before Civilization* takes up a related problem from a different archive. Its evidence is scattered among archaeological sites, ethnographic descriptions, skeletal remains, fortifications, weapons, and accounts of societies without states. Keeley’s purpose was to challenge the comfortable picture of pre-state life as largely peaceful. He assembled cases in which arrow wounds, embedded points, cranial injuries, burned settlements, and defensive works indicated recurrent conflict.

The book’s argument depends on comparison. A village of several hundred cannot be compared to a modern country by counting corpses alone. Keeley writes that “the percentage of war deaths in tribal societies is often much higher than in modern states,” directing attention away from absolute totals and toward the proportion of a population exposed to lethal conflict. A smaller society can suffer a greater demographic burden even when its dead would disappear beneath the totals of a twentieth-century war.

The calculation begins with an interpretive decision. An archaeologist must decide whether a mark on bone came from a weapon, an accident, a ritual treatment, or a later disturbance. A stone point lodged in a vertebra carries a different implication from a healed fracture on an arm. A wall may have defended a settlement, marked a boundary, or served a ceremonial purpose. No single object announces the social event that produced it.

Keeley’s evidence gains force from convergence. A wound becomes more persuasive when it appears beside a weapon type suited to the injury, a settlement with defensive works, and an ethnographic description of recurrent fighting. A burned layer may indicate attack when it occurs across houses and is associated with hurried abandonment. A single charred beam cannot carry that conclusion. The historian must bring the parts of the archive into relation without pretending that they speak in one voice. His tables and examples also show the danger of treating visibility as frequency. A massacre site can preserve the dead more clearly than a pattern of intimidation, hostage-taking, or domestic assault. A fortified settlement reveals fear or preparation, but not the number of attacks that never occurred. Ethnographic observers may describe warfare in detail while ignoring violence within families. Court records often illuminate a household killing while leaving routine threats unrecorded.

The archive does not distribute evidence according to the importance of the violence. It preserves what institutions, environments, and observers were equipped to notice. Bone records the force that reaches the skeleton. A fortification records labour spent on defence. A court proceeding records an injury that someone managed to carry into a legal process. Each source has a field of vision, and each field is shaped by the society that produced it.

Keeley’s method remains valuable because it asks the reader to compare kinds of evidence rather than wait for a perfect archive. His conclusion about the lethality of tribal warfare rests on rates, reported practices, and archaeological traces read together. The inference is strongest when the body, the weapon, the settlement, and the historical description point toward the same kind of event. It weakens when one category is made to stand for the whole society.

The result is not a universal scale on which every society can be assigned a single aggression score. A high proportion of war deaths describes one feature of a social world. It does not measure every threat, beating, feud, expulsion, or legal punishment. Nor does the absence of a wound prove the absence of coercion. The historian must ask what the source could have recorded, who had the power to create that record, and which violence remained outside its reach.

Iceland’s medieval sagas make violence look different because they preserve disputes as stories of named families. In *Njáls saga*, written down centuries after the events it recounts, insults, lawsuits, arson, ambushes, and settlements move through a world where a killing can demand compensation without ending the relationship between the families involved. The saga gives a reader names, reputations, spoken challenges, legal manoeuvres, and remembered injuries. It makes violence legible as a chain carried by people who remain answerable to one another.

The mechanism can be followed closely. An insult produces a claim. The claim brings supporters to an assembly. A death requires payment or another death, while the families continue to meet through law, trade, and marriage. A settlement can interrupt the chain without erasing the original injury. The capacity to mobilise household resources and legal expertise determines whether a grievance becomes a killing, a payment, or a prolonged contest.

Aztec tribute records reverse the emphasis. In the *Matrícula de Tributos*, towns appear as units assigned quantities of cotton cloth, feathers, shields, cacao, and other goods. The page does not narrate the quarrel of two households. It presents a conquered population as an obligation measured for collection. Campaign, submission, transport, and storage stand behind the painted signs and quantities, even when the page records only the amount due. That difference in record changes the violence we can see. Icelandic texts preserve reputations and acts of revenge because the saga’s social world turns on personal memory and legal relationship. Aztec records make extraction visible because imperial rule required a schedule: a town owed so much, an official received it, and failure could invite renewed force. One archive makes conflict look like a chain of encounters; the other makes it look like an administrative flow. Neither society possessed only the form shown by its surviving records. Icelandic feud required assemblies, legal expertise, and household resources. Aztec tribute required armies, local rulers, roads, storehouses, and officials. The page follows the institution that made the society governable, and the violence appears in its shape. The surviving record does not reveal an unchanging human aggression beneath history; it shows who could organise harm, whom that organisation reached, and what the society required violence to accomplish.
