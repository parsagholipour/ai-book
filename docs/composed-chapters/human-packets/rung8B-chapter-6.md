## Chapter 6: Frontiers, Faiths, and Political Fragmentation

In the Welsh Marches, a cattle raid tested more than a household’s ability to guard its herd. It tested whether a lord could gather men quickly, whether a castle could shelter villagers, whether a neighbouring ruler would compensate the injured party, and whether stolen cattle could cross a border before pursuit began. In the twelfth century, the Marcher lord’s power rested less on a continuous boundary than on movement and response. The cattle were wealth, food, breeding stock, and proof that one household’s claim had been broken. The same connection appeared farther east and south. A pastoral ruler who demanded sheep, horses, or silver from settled communities might spare their fields because he needed them productive the following year. A caravan route could invite attack because merchants carried goods between jurisdictions that could not easily police one another. Al-Bakri’s account of Sijilmasa in the eleventh century describes a North African trading centre whose prosperity depended on movement across the Sahara and whose rulers stood inside arguments about taxation, piety, and commercial trust. The authority that claimed to defend the market also claimed the right to take from it.

Historians who treat frontier violence as an eruption from outside settled society miss this bargaining structure. Raiding destroyed, but it also announced a price. Captives were future payments; livestock were mobile assessments; burned crops warned communities that obedience might still prevent a second attack. The Mongol demand for tribute and the Viking collection of danegeld differed in scale and political setting, yet both turned the threat of renewed attack into a recurring transfer of goods. Destruction was credible because exchange followed it.

Frontier violence was organised through unequal access to movement, refuge, and negotiation despite its connection to exchange. A fragmented landscape gave many groups room to evade one lord and seek another, while giving every lord an incentive to make departure costly. Merchants, shepherds, captives, monks, and town councils could become sources of revenue or instruments against a rival. The border endured through transactions that violence repeatedly interrupted and rulers repeatedly restored.

Bari stood at the edge of several such worlds. It was the last major Byzantine stronghold in southern Italy, a port facing the Adriatic, and a city whose loyalties had been claimed by Constantinople, local Lombard rulers, and Norman adventurers who had entered Italian politics as soldiers for hire. On 15 April 1071, after a siege lasting nearly three years, the city surrendered to Robert Guiscard. The date appears in the *Annales Barenses*, whose terse notice records the change of ruler without supplying the speeches that might make it seem inevitable.

The siege began in 1068, when Robert’s brother Roger was active in Sicily and the Byzantine government was trying to preserve its remaining positions in Apulia. Bari’s walls were strong, its harbour valuable, and its garrison connected to the imperial court by sea. The Normans could defeat local forces, but holding the peninsula required more than mounted aggression. They needed ships, supplies, siege works, and an argument that their conquest could be accepted by the people whose taxes would sustain it.

Byzantine rule in southern Italy had already been weakened by distance and divided command. Constantine X Doukas had died in 1067; Romanos IV Diogenes was occupied with the eastern frontier; and the local katepan, or governor, could not assume that Constantinople would send relief when it was needed. In 1069 an imperial force reached Italy, but the effort did not break the siege. Bari’s isolation became a Norman weapon. Each season that passed reduced the defenders’ choices.

A wall enclosed a garrison that could fight from towers and gates, while the inhabitants required grain, oil, water, and access to the sea. The Norman blockade attacked the relationship between Bari and the empire rather than merely the city’s stones. A siege tower could be burned and rebuilt; a convoy that never arrived changed the calculation of every household inside. The *Chronicon Casauriense* and southern Italian annals preserve the broad sequence of pressure, negotiation, and surrender, while later Norman histories make Robert appear as the natural master of a city he had first made expensive to defend.

When the surrender came, Bari endured: its churches remained, its merchants continued to reckon debts, and its harbour acquired a new political overseer. Norman victory was not a replacement of Byzantine inhabitants by Norman settlers. Robert needed officials who knew local revenues, priests who could conduct services, and port workers who could keep ships moving. Conquest altered the chain through which property and obedience travelled.

The political dispute surrounding the city can be seen in the titles Robert used and in the ceremonies expected of a ruler. The Normans were Latin Christians, but their legitimacy in southern Italy was not secured by religious difference alone. Byzantium was also Christian, its emperors still claimed Roman authority, and the Greek rite remained established in Apulia. Robert’s seizure of Bari challenged an imperial order as well as extending Latin lordship. The city’s inhabitants were compelled to live through a contest between two Christian political systems.

The papacy had its own interest in that contest. At Melfi in 1059, Pope Nicholas II recognised Robert as duke of Apulia, Calabria, and—by the language of the grant—future conqueror of Sicily, though the force of the agreement depended on military facts that no parchment could settle. Bari in 1071 gave the grant a visible result. A Norman duke who controlled the last Byzantine capital in Italy could present himself as the defender of Latin Christendom, while the pope gained a counterweight to Constantinople and a partner in the reshaping of southern Italy.

Papal recognition did not supply Robert with grain or crews. Those came from lands he controlled and from alliances he could enforce. Men cut timber, drove animals, hauled stones, dug trenches, and guarded roads. The city was captured by a ruler whose political future depended on turning scattered military victories into dependable revenue.

Bari’s harbour linked Apulia to Epirus, Dalmatia, and the imperial capital. Its fall weakened Byzantine communication in the central Mediterranean, but it did not end Byzantine interest in Italy. The empire retained influence through churches, merchants, and diplomacy, while Norman rulers continued to face a sea frontier that could carry enemies as readily as provisions. In 1081, Robert’s son Bohemond would fight Byzantine forces in the Balkans, and in 1085 Robert himself would die at Cephalonia during an expedition against Emperor Alexios I Komnenos. The capture of Bari opened a route into imperial politics.

Robert used a siege, but the siege depended on mobile men and flexible alliances. Byzantium possessed an imperial administration, but the imperial claim weakened where ships, money, and local cooperation failed. The Normans were not yet a settled state in the later sense; they became one by taking towns whose tax systems and markets could convert armed presence into durable rule.

A document as short as the *Annales Barenses* entry can carry a large institutional history. Its date fixes the moment when Bari changed hands. Its silence about ordinary fear does not empty the event of social meaning; the change of ruler required thousands of people to continue farming, trading, praying, and paying under a different command. The city’s walls had been built to preserve an imperial possession. In 1071 they became the foundation of a Norman one.

On the Marches, a raid began with animals rather than walls. A party crossed at dawn, often after watching a valley’s roads and learning where the herd was left overnight. Men drove cattle, sheep, or horses toward a known ford, while others delayed pursuit by setting fire to a byre, cutting a bridge, or threatening the household that raised the alarm. The stolen herd slowed the raiders and gave the injured lord time to understand the scale of his loss.

The next step was sorting. Some animals were kept; others were slaughtered for the raiders’ use; the best beasts might be reserved for sale or exchange. Welsh law codes and Marcher records reveal a world in which livestock had recognisable values and ownership could be argued through witnesses, brands, kinship, and prior possession. The *Book of Llandaff*, although chiefly concerned with ecclesiastical lands and rights, preserves the kind of disputes in which cattle, boundaries, and named men were joined. A herd belonged to a household, a monastery, or a lord whose honour was measured by the ability to recover it.

If recovery was possible, the injured side assembled a counter-raid. If it was not, the stolen animals became evidence of weakness. A lord might demand compensation from the raiders’ patron, threaten villages that sheltered them, or negotiate a payment to prevent a second incursion. One seizure could become regular tribute. The party that first took cattle might return each year for a smaller payment, presenting the reduction as mercy while the settled community understood it as a tax backed by remembered fire.

Captives made the calculation more exact. A person taken in a raid could be sold, exchanged, or held until relatives or a lord produced payment. Medieval ransom was not confined to kings and knights, though the records preserve elite cases best. After the capture of Richard I in 1192, Emperor Henry VI demanded a sum so large that English sheriffs assessed land, churches, and household goods to raise it. That royal episode belongs to another scale, but its logic appeared in frontier conflicts where a priest, merchant, landholder, or fighting man carried a value beyond the immediate labour of his body.

A captive had to be kept alive, guarded, and made credible as a bargaining object. The captor sent word through kin, clergy, merchants, or a neutral lord. The message named a sum or a form of service and often set a deadline. Relatives gathered movable wealth, borrowed against land, or persuaded a patron to intervene. In the Welsh Marches, a hostage might be exchanged for cattle or guarantees, while a knight could be released after pledges were given by men whose standing made default dangerous. Physical possession had become political leverage.

Seasonal movement increased that leverage. Pastoral communities required grass, wells, salt licks, winter shelter, and markets at particular times. A ruler who controlled a pass in April could demand animals before allowing a herd toward summer pasture; one who held a river crossing in October could collect again when the herd returned. The payment was called tribute when it flowed upward, rent when a settled landlord claimed it, and protection when a war band offered to prevent someone else from taking it. The same sheep could pass through several political vocabularies before reaching a market. This was why frontier rulers often preferred a productive neighbour to an empty one. Killing every household removed future tribute, while burning every field made the next campaign harder to supply. Mongol commanders who demanded submission from towns could destroy those that resisted, but they also preserved artisans, tax registers, and routes when incorporation promised more than annihilation. Viking armies that accepted danegeld did not cease to be dangerous; payment worked because communities believed refusal would bring another attack. The bargain rested on the attacker’s capacity to punish and the victim’s expectation that payment might purchase time.

Pastoral tribute could be organised without a permanent bureaucracy. A chief’s men counted animals at a seasonal gathering, received hostages, marked obligations through oral memory, and returned when the next payment was due. In the Sahel, caravan routes and grazing corridors joined rulers to merchants and herders through tolls enforced at wells or gates. Al-Bakri’s description of Sijilmasa places trade, taxation, and religious authority in the same urban setting: the market generated wealth, the ruler claimed a share, and the language of legitimate rule shaped whether that claim seemed like order or extortion.

The sequence was concrete. Movement exposed property; armed men seized it; captives and animals slowed the return journey; messengers translated possession into a demand; relatives, merchants, or lords raised payment; and a new obligation was fixed for the next season. Farmers planted under a heavier lord, shepherds took a longer route to water, a monastery supplied a hostage, or a town paid for the right to keep its gate open. Aggression was one way of regulating who could move, who could sell, and who had to pay.

At Clermont, in November 1095, Pope Urban II addressed an assembly of bishops, abbots, nobles, and ordinary hearers gathered in the open air outside the city. The setting is secure; the exact words are not. No written transcript made at the moment survives, and the accounts composed afterward differ in emphasis, sequence, and language. Fulcher of Chartres, who was present at Clermont, wrote his version later in his *Historia Hierosolymitana*, presenting Urban as calling western Christians to aid their eastern brethren and recover Jerusalem.

Fulcher’s account begins with suffering. He describes churches polluted, altars destroyed, Christians subjected to injury, and pilgrims prevented from reaching holy places. The speech turns these injuries into a summons directed at men who had been fighting one another at home. Their swords were not to disappear; they were to be redirected. The same warrior who had raided a neighbour or contested a lord’s inheritance could now be enrolled in a campaign whose destination was distant, whose authority came from the pope, and whose reward was spiritual.

Urban’s reported words move from accusation to promise. Fulcher says that those who went, whether by land or sea and whether at their own expense or by borrowing, would receive remission of sins from God if they died on the journey. The promise attached forgiveness to a defined expedition, a sacred destination, and obedience to a religious command. A fighter’s previous acts were placed within a new account. The papal office could transform armed pilgrimage into a penitential act. That transformation required witnesses and messengers. Urban preached; bishops repeated; local priests explained; nobles recruited; clerks recorded vows. Fulcher’s narrative describes the crowd responding with a cry, “Deus vult,” meaning “God wills it.” Whether every listener shouted those words at Clermont cannot be established from the surviving evidence, but the phrase became a compact political declaration. It made a contingent decision sound like a verdict already given.

Other accounts show how remembrance altered the sermon. Robert the Monk, writing around the beginning of the twelfth century, gives Urban a more vivid appeal to western warriors, including descriptions of eastern lands as rich and fertile and of the Holy Sepulchre as wrongfully held. Guibert of Nogent reworked the speech in a learned Latin history, while Baldric of Dol placed greater weight on Christian unity and the disgrace of fighting fellow Christians. These authors shaped an event for readers who already knew that an army had departed and that Jerusalem had fallen.

Fulcher’s version is especially revealing because he was close enough to the expedition to describe its leaders and later consequences, yet wrote after the campaign had acquired a providential meaning. His Urban speaks to knights whose violence is treated as a social problem and a military resource. The pope’s command does not abolish the frontier culture of honour, booty, and lordship visible in Bari or the Welsh Marches. It gives those practices a new theatre and places their legitimacy under a clerical office.

The remission of sins was more than inward consolation. It was an administrative and moral instrument. A vow bound the participant to a journey; the cross sewn onto a cloak made the vow visible; churchmen could preach the terms; families could explain why land or money had been committed; and leaders could demand discipline from men who believed the expedition carried supernatural significance. Urban’s promise joined private salvation to collective force.

Fulcher says that Urban urged the rich and poor, horsemen and foot soldiers, to go, though the costs of travel ensured that the crusading host was never socially uniform. The army included princes such as Raymond of Saint-Gilles, Godfrey of Bouillon, and Bohemond of Taranto, as well as servants, camp followers, artisans, and people who left their homes with little prospect of return. The sermon’s broad address did not produce equal power among them. Nobles commanded contingents; clergy interpreted vows; merchants supplied credit; poorer pilgrims endured the road and the siege.

The route tested the promise. Constantinople, Antioch, and Jerusalem were cities with gates, markets, rulers, languages, and populations that had to feed or resist the western armies. At Antioch, starvation, faction, and negotiation shaped the campaign as much as devotion did. When Jerusalem was reached in June 1099, the crusaders constructed siege towers and sought timber, while defenders repaired walls and gathered weapons. Religious authorization had become an apparatus of marching, provisioning, and attack.

The surviving versions expose the power of retrospective certainty. Fulcher wrote that Urban’s appeal was heard by a multitude and that men marked themselves with the sign of the cross. This scene became the moral beginning of the First Crusade, even though the campaign’s participants pursued differing aims. A pope sought the recovery of eastern Christianity and the extension of Roman leadership; nobles sought land and status; penitents sought remission; merchants sought viable routes and profit. These purposes could coexist because the sermon provided a common language in which military action, spiritual danger, and material opportunity reinforced one another.

The words attributed to Urban mattered because they travelled through offices. A spoken appeal at Clermont became a vow, a badge, a letter, a levy, and eventually a settlement. Faith told particular men when violence was permitted, against whom, and under whose command.

By the morning of 13 April 1204, Constantinople had already endured two days of attack. The crusading army had entered the city after the flight of Emperor Alexios V Doukas, while Venetian ships and soldiers pressed through the sea walls and the landward defences weakened. Nicetas Choniates, a high-ranking Byzantine official and historian, watched the collapse of the government he had served. His account of the sack is written in grief and anger, but its details are too numerous to reduce to a complaint about lost treasure.

Choniates describes soldiers breaking into houses, dragging away goods, and violating churches. Sacred vessels were taken from Hagia Sophia; an altar was dismantled and used for transport; a prostitute was seated on the patriarchal throne and sang obscenely, in his account, before the soldiers. The scene joins physical desecration to the seizure of offices. A church was a place whose hierarchy and ritual order declared what kind of city Constantinople was. To profane it was to show that the city’s old guardians had been displaced.

The attackers had arrived under the banner of the Fourth Crusade, ostensibly to recover Jerusalem after earlier failures. Debt and contract had redirected them. In 1202 the crusaders could not pay the Venetians for the fleet constructed to carry them east, and the doge Enrico Dandolo accepted a diversion against Zara, a Christian city that Venice claimed. The siege and capture of Zara established that a crusading army could be turned against a Christian population when creditors, commanders, and expedition leaders agreed that payment and strategy required it.

Constantinople offered more than Zara. The young Alexios Angelos had promised money, military aid, and church union if the crusaders restored him and his father Isaac II to the imperial throne. The promise exceeded the resources available to fulfil it. After Alexios became emperor as Alexios IV, the city’s taxes rose, local hostility sharpened, and the promised payments remained incomplete. In January 1204, a revolt brought Alexios V to power. The expedition’s leaders now treated the city as a debtor and enemy.

The change was expressed in a partition agreement known as the *partitio Romaniae*, concluded among the Venetians and the crusading leaders before the final conquest. It assigned churches, islands, ports, revenues, and imperial offices in advance. Its clauses show that the sack was not simply the uncontrolled release of soldiers after a breach. Men who had not yet taken the city were already deciding who would receive its provinces and which objects would be removed. The future empire was divided on parchment before its inhabitants knew which gate would fail.

For the people inside Constantinople, the attack moved through streets and thresholds. Families hid in houses, monasteries, and churches. Some fled toward the countryside or the Asian shore; others sought protection from soldiers, neighbours, or clerics. Choniates writes of women and children exposed to assault and of men stripped of possessions. The Latin army needed food, animals, quarters, and transport, so civilian homes became stores, barracks, and sources of payment. The sack reached beyond palaces because an army occupying a city had to live from it.

The Venetians pursued objects that could be carried and displayed. Horses from the Hippodrome, probably cast in antiquity and later placed above the façade of San Marco, were shipped to Venice. Enamelled panels, reliquaries, marble columns, and other Byzantine works entered Venetian churches and public spaces. The four bronze horses became a clear sign of transfer: an object associated with imperial ceremony in Constantinople was made to signify Venetian possession in the Adriatic world.

A Venetian viewer could see imperial horses above San Marco without seeing the streets from which they had been taken. In Constantinople, the absence of such objects was part of the defeat. The city had been stripped not only of wealth that could be melted or spent, but of materials that made its past visible. The conquerors converted Byzantine history into portable evidence of their own success.

Some treasures were melted. Others were divided among churches, rulers, soldiers, and creditors. Baldwin of Flanders became Latin emperor; Boniface of Montferrat received Thessalonica; Venice secured ports and commercial privileges. The settlement did not create a unified Latin command. It distributed authority among competing communities, leaving Byzantine resistance to reorganise in Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond. The empire that returned to Constantinople in 1261 under Michael VIII Palaiologos inherited a city diminished in population, resources, and political reach.

Nicetas Choniates fled first to Selymbria and then found refuge at the court of Theodore Laskaris in Nicaea. His movement traced the political aftermath. A state official became an exile; an imperial capital became the prize of foreign creditors and soldiers; a new Byzantine centre formed across the Bosporus. His history gave the catastrophe a vocabulary of sacrilege, but the partition agreement supplies its administrative grammar.

The civilians of Constantinople experienced those two languages together. A woman whose house was entered by soldiers faced theft and possible assault; a monk saw sacred vessels carried away; a merchant lost stock and access to clients; a child watched armed foreigners occupy a street where imperial officials had once enforced the law. Their suffering came from an army whose leaders had assigned territory, whose creditors demanded payment, and whose religious identity allowed the victims to be treated as enemies despite their shared baptism.

On the third morning, the city’s new rulers began turning conquest into possession. Guards were placed at gates, stores were counted, and messages went out to claim the districts and revenues allotted to particular men. The cries described by Choniates belonged to the moment when command was changing hands; the inventories, charters, and shiploads that followed belonged to the longer moment when violence became property. In Constantinople the scale was imperial, but the sequence remained legible: seize, divide, transport, and require the survivors to live inside the settlement.

Wat Tyler’s horse had pushed through the wet grass of Smithfield by the time the king came down from Clerkenwell on Saturday, 15 June 1381. Behind Tyler stood men from Kent, Essex, and the streets south of the river, packed between the wall of St Bartholomew’s Priory and the open ground where horses were bought and slaughtered. Their bows were unstrung in the rain. Some carried swords; more carried bills, axes, clubs, or nothing at all.

Richard was fourteen years old. He rode beneath a canopy, with Sir John Newton and other knights around him, and the mayor of London, William Walworth, close at his bridle. The king’s household had crossed the bridge from the Tower, where the rebels had slept and eaten for two nights. The city gates were open behind them.

Tyler came forward alone. He wore a short sword at his side and a dagger in his hand. The chronicler says that he greeted the king and asked him to confirm the liberties granted at Mile End the day before: that villeinage should end, that rents should be fixed at four pence an acre, and that the rebels should have the king’s pardon. He spoke as a man accustomed to being heard. The king answered that what had been granted would stand.

Tyler turned toward the king’s officers and began again. He pointed out the chancellor, Simon Sudbury, archbishop of Canterbury, and Robert Hales, treasurer of England, whom the commons had hunted from the Tower. Their deaths had not satisfied the men in the field. Tyler demanded that the king make the realm answerable to the people who laboured in it. As he spoke, he handled the dagger, lifting it and lowering it, and the men behind him shifted nearer.

Walworth saw the movement. He was wearing the red and white of the city, and his sword was at his belt. He stepped between Tyler and the king and struck him with the point or edge, the accounts differ, cutting him in the neck. Tyler reeled in the saddle. The Londoners shouted. Some drew their bows, and the king’s company closed around him.

Then Richard rode toward them.

“Traitors, what do you mean?” he called, according to the chronicle, and told them that Wat Tyler was a traitor and that they should have no leader but the king. He had them stand still while Tyler was carried into St Bartholomew’s Hospital. The rebels remained in the field with their weapons in their hands. Walworth returned almost at once, his sword dark with blood.

The king had Tyler brought out. He spoke to the men of Kent and Essex as though the promise were still whole. He granted them their pardon and said that they might go home in peace. Walworth, who had been made a knight there in the field, sent letters through the city ordering the gates watched and the commons disarmed. By evening the banners had begun to move away from Smithfield, and Richard’s officers were riding after them with the king’s writs to seize the rebels.
