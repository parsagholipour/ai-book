## Chapter 8: States, Slavery, and the Atlantic World

In Charleston, a sale began with a name in a newspaper and ended with a person assigned to a row. On 2 March 1769, the *South-Carolina Gazette* advertised enslaved Africans for sale at Ryan’s Tavern, describing men, women, and children by age, sex, skill, and supposed health. Other advertisements named the seller, the ship that had brought the people to Charleston, the hour of the auction, and the place where purchasers might inspect them. A buyer could ask a trader to separate a mother from her child, bargain over a carpenter’s claimed ability, and have the price entered in an account book beside horses, tools, or barrels of rice. Charleston’s Exchange and commercial wharves connected the sale to Atlantic credit. The auction was local; the debt, insurance, shipping, and expected crop revenue could reach London, Liverpool, or Amsterdam.

The person being sold entered the plantation with a legal identity already narrowed by statute. South Carolina’s 1740 Negro Act described enslaved people as property and restricted movement, assembly, literacy, and the possession of weapons. It authorised severe punishment for resistance and set conditions under which white people might search for, confine, or kill enslaved people. A sale transferred ownership without transferring legal personhood. The purchaser acquired the right to command labour and claim its products, to sell the person again, and to summon courts, patrols, and sheriffs when private discipline seemed insufficient.

A new arrival might be taken from Charleston to a rice estate along the Cooper River, or southward towards the plantations of St Helena Island and the Combahee. The journey was part of the process. The trader’s receipt recorded the price; the overseer’s book recorded the allocation; the owner’s ledger recorded the value. At the plantation, an enslaved worker was assigned a task according to age, strength, experience, and the crop calendar. A woman who had cultivated rice in West Africa might be placed in a lowland field. A man described as a carpenter might be kept near the repair sheds, while a child was attached to a household or sent to carry water. The judgement belonged to the manager, who turned skills acquired elsewhere into assets of the purchaser.

The day did not begin with one command. Before sunrise, a bell or horn called workers from their quarters. The driver assembled the gang, counted the people, and led them to the field. Under a task system, workers had to complete a specified amount of hoeing, ditching, planting, or picking. Under a gang system, they worked together at a pace set by the driver or overseer. The crop fixed the timetable. Sugar cane demanded cutting and hauling before boiling; the sequence could continue through the night. Rice required dikes, trunks, canals, and flooded fields to be maintained. Tobacco required repeated cultivation and careful handling.

The gang joined people who did not necessarily share language, kinship, or place of origin. An estate might contain Africans born in Senegambia, the Bight of Benin, the Congo, or Angola, people born in South Carolina, and children whose connection to Africa existed through a parent’s memory. The plantation officer made this varied population into a labour unit by assigning places, measuring progress, and imposing penalties. A task was complete when the overseer said it was complete. He also decided whether slowness meant disobedience, illness meant deception, or refusal meant rebellion.

Thomas Jefferson’s account of Virginia plantations, written in *Notes on the State of Virginia* in 1781–82, called the “whole commerce between master and slave” a perpetual exercise of the most boisterous passions. Jefferson was a slaveholder defending a social order he considered dangerous even while maintaining it. His sentence identifies the contradiction without removing it: the plantation required close personal supervision while representing the people supervised as property. Law made that personal command portable, saleable, and enforceable beyond the owner’s immediate presence.

An overseer’s ledger gave the command a daily grammar. Entries might record the number of hands in the field, the work expected, the work completed, people absent through illness, and punishments administered. On some estates, a separate column recorded lashes: “Jack, 10”; “Mingo, 20”; “Sambo, 15.” The entries preserve no voice from the person beaten, but they show that punishment was counted and made comparable. The whip was part of a reporting chain. The overseer answered to the planter, the planter calculated output, and the ledger converted bodily injury into information about labour.

Reports travelled upward. A manager wrote to an owner that workers had fallen behind, that a particular person was “insolent,” or that the crop required greater exertion. The owner might answer from Charleston or London with an instruction to increase the work, replace an overseer, purchase more workers, or sell those judged troublesome. Distance did not remove control; it required more writing. Jamaican estate records preserved lists of births, deaths, purchases, punishments, and provisions. South Carolina records are less uniform, but estate inventories, correspondence, sale notices, and court proceedings show the same reliance on written accounts.

The workday had several clocks. Natural light marked the beginning of field labour. The crop’s urgency marked its pace. The account book compared output with provisions, mortality, debt, and the price of enslaved people. The law set patrol hours, authorised searches, and prescribed penalties. When these measures converged, a person could be forced to work from before dawn until after dark while the plantation remained within its formal rules.

Rules made violence predictable by defining its lawful use. The 1740 South Carolina act required enslaved people to carry passes when away from their owners and empowered white patrols to enter quarters, search for weapons, and apprehend people found without authorisation. It prescribed punishments for striking a white person, running away, gathering without permission, and teaching enslaved people to read. It also protected white people who killed an enslaved person while suppressing resistance. A patrol therefore extended the plantation along the roads. The state lent the owner eyes, horses, weapons, and a claim to lawful force.

Virginia’s statutes widened the same circle. A 1705 law declared that servants imported into the colony who were not Christians in their countries of origin would be slaves. Later statutes defined children’s status through the condition of their mothers. In 1723, Virginia restricted the movement and meetings of enslaved people and limited their access to arms, placing enforcement in the hands of counties and magistrates. The plantation’s violence could be private in execution and public in authorisation. The master held the whip, but the assembly defined its place in the social order.

An accusation of theft or absence might end in a sale. The accused person could be confined, whipped, or sent to jail before being transported to another owner. Standards applied to free citizens left the testimony of enslaved people generally restricted or excluded in cases involving white defendants, while white testimony could activate a search or prosecution. Suspicion thus acquired the force of discipline through an unequal court system.

Resistance took forms the ledger rarely named accurately. A worker could slow the hoe, damage a tool, conceal food, leave the field, feign illness, preserve a language or ritual, or flee towards the swamps. In the Lowcountry, rivers, marshes, and islands offered both danger and concealment. Runaway notices in the *South-Carolina Gazette* described scars, clothing, speech, skills, and suspected destinations. They tried to convert a missing person into a recoverable asset. The notice asked readers to identify someone whom the owner claimed as property and offered payment for capture.

A failed escape could be followed by sale. The person returned to the plantation might be whipped, fitted with an iron collar, jailed, or sold away. Sale separated a person from family, familiar work, and the local knowledge that made survival possible. On plantations where generations lived together, that threat reached people who had not resisted, giving the owner power over future relationships as well as present labour.

The plantation’s economic calculation appeared in insurance and credit. An inventory might value an enslaved man according to age and occupation, an enslaved woman according to age and presumed capacity to bear children, and a child according to expected future value. Someone sick, elderly, pregnant, or injured could be entered at a lower price while remaining within the same property category. Calculation did not replace violence; it directed decisions about food, medical attention, working hours, and punishment towards the preservation of an asset that could be mortgaged and sold.

Reproduction became an economic process through law. If an enslaved woman gave birth, the child belonged to the owner of the mother. The rule later known as *partus sequitur ventrem* made a child’s condition follow the mother’s condition. In Virginia, the principle was enacted in 1662. It allowed planters to increase their labour force without importing every new worker from Africa, while sexual coercion by white men could enrich the owner of the mother without altering the child’s status.

The documents use ordinary words for extraordinary coercion. A person is “delivered,” “transferred,” “purchased,” “returned,” or “disposed of.” A plantation is “stocked.” A family becomes a “lot.” Punishment is “inflicted.” Such language belongs to the legal and commercial system that made violence compatible with ownership. The whip disciplined the gang, the patrol secured the roads, the court enforced the code, and the ledger preserved the result.

By the 1740s, sugar and slavery had made Barbados both warning and model for the English Atlantic. Enslaved Africans outnumbered white inhabitants there by nearly ten to one by the 1670s. In Bristol, merchants financed voyages, insured cargoes, and invested proceeds in houses, warehouses, and manufactures. A list compiled in 1749 assigned 8,560 enslaved people a combined value of £149,400. Human beings entered the same arithmetic as ships and commodities, linking a Carolina field to the wealth of a merchant, insurer, or manufacturer who never saw it.

The Atlantic system became violent through assemblies that declared people property, courts that defended the declaration, patrols that enforced it, traders who supplied bodies, and owners who measured the return. The field gang was the daily form of a transoceanic decision.

In 1788, the Liverpool slave ship *Brookes* carried enslaved Africans from the coast of West Africa towards Jamaica. The vessel became famous after an image of its human cargo was published by the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade and reused in abolitionist pamphlets. The engraving showed rows of bodies fitted into the lower deck. Each person lay on a narrow shelf. Male and female spaces were separated, and the available height was so small that sitting upright was impossible.

The image was based on the ship’s plans and dimensions, not on a scene drawn at sea. Its force came from measurement. Bodies were arranged according to length, width, and permitted space. A reader in London could inspect the hold as a plan. The black marks represented units of occupancy, packed beneath beams and partitions that made confinement architectural.

Parliament’s Dolben Act of 1788 required slave ships to carry no more than one enslaved person for each ton up to a specified limit, with a lower ratio permitted for larger vessels. It imposed financial penalties when the number exceeded the law, required the master to carry certificates, and provided for inspection. The statute set a legal maximum for the number of people who could be confined and transported as cargo.

The act moved between humanitarian concern and administrative control. It referred to the health and lives of enslaved people, then treated their suffering through tonnage, certificates, inspection, and forfeiture. The ship became governable through a ratio. The *Brookes* diagram showed what that ratio meant when rendered as bodies.

Published in 1789, the diagram turned a plan prepared for regulation into evidence against the trade. Viewers could see that the ship’s design made bodily contact compulsory and left little room for rest. The image showed the hold as a decision about whose physical pain could be accepted as a cost of commerce.

A ship carrying fewer people could still carry them in chains, sell them on arrival, and profit from their forced labour. The Dolben Act regulated the vessel without challenging the property claim that filled it. Parliament counted the harm while preserving the trade it supervised.

On the night of 21 August 1791, enslaved people gathered at Bois Caïman in the northern plain of Saint-Domingue. Later accounts associated the meeting with Dutty Boukman, described as a religious leader and coachman, and with a ceremony joining spiritual authority to a plan for revolt. The best-known narrative was recorded after the uprising and shaped by Haitian and European writers. Its details vary, but the gathering remains a memory of collective preparation.

Saint-Domingue was France’s richest colony. In the late eighteenth century, plantations produced sugar, coffee, indigo, and cotton for Atlantic markets. The colony’s population included hundreds of thousands of enslaved people, free people of colour, and white colonists divided by wealth and status. Its productivity rested on coercion. A rising in one district threatened the export revenue on which merchants in Cap-Français, Bordeaux, Nantes, and Marseille depended.

The uprising began in the northern plain during the night of 22 August. Plantations were burned, owners and managers were killed, and rebels destroyed mills, cane fields, storehouses, and records. The destruction struck the buildings and papers through which the plantation converted labour into property and property into revenue. Fire reached the machinery of the colony.

News travelled along roads and through plantation quarters. During the first days, colonial authorities struggled to determine the revolt’s scale. Some white colonists fled towards fortified towns; others organised armed groups. Free men of colour, already demanding political rights, watched the conflict and negotiated their position as white control weakened. French officials sent troops, but the colony’s geography and the number of enslaved people made restoration more difficult than suppressing a local disturbance.

Boukman was captured and killed in November 1791. His head was displayed in Cap-Français, a colonial practice intended to make the rebel leader’s body testify to the return of order. The uprising continued. Jean-François, Biassou, and later Toussaint Louverture emerged as military leaders, navigating alliances with Spain, France, free people of colour, and rival groups in a war whose legal and political terms repeatedly changed.

The ceremony at Bois Caïman gave rebellion a public form, but the revolt depended on organisation already present among plantation workers, communication among people moved between estates, knowledge of the terrain, and conflict among colonial authorities. The plantation concentrated labour and grievances together. It also created networks through which news and orders could pass.

Colonial officials described the rebels as criminals, fanatics, or foreign agents. Those labels moved the cause of revolt from the plantation’s daily violence to the supposed nature of the people who resisted it. The rebels’ actions were directed towards the structures that held them: fields, mills, stores, overseers, and records. They attacked a regime that had made the destruction of bodies and families a condition of profit.

In 1793, commissioners sent by revolutionary France offered freedom to enslaved people who joined the republican cause. The abolition of slavery in the French colonies was proclaimed in 1794, though war and local power shaped its application. Napoleon attempted to restore slavery in 1802. The struggle continued until French forces were defeated, and Haiti declared independence on 1 January 1804.

The law that made slavery hereditary began with a sentence about the child. Virginia’s statute of December 1662 declared that children would be bond or free according to the condition of the mother. The rule displaced English assumptions about paternal status and gave colonial owners a stable method for reproducing property through birth. A child’s future could be determined before the child could be named.

The Virginia slave codes supplied the body with restrictions. The 1705 act placed enslaved people within a property regime, permitted severe punishment, limited legal testimony, and made conversion to Christianity no guarantee of freedom. Later laws punished movement without a pass, unauthorised assembly, and teaching people to read. County courts, sheriffs, patrols, and household heads each received a role in preserving the inherited condition.

Barbados offered an earlier English Caribbean model. The 1661 slave code defined enslaved Africans as chattels and authorised punishment for resistance, flight, and assembly. White people were protected as a class, while enslaved people were governed as labouring property whose descendants would enlarge the supply.

French law reached the same inheritance through another vocabulary. The *Code Noir* of 1685 required baptism and Catholic instruction, recognised certain marriages, and instructed masters to provide food and clothing. It also authorised corporal punishment, restricted assembly, treated enslaved people as movable property, and followed maternal descent in determining children’s condition. Obligations towards the enslaved helped define the terms under which ownership continued.

Spanish America classified people through a more elaborate vocabulary of descent. Colonial casta paintings and administrative regulations distinguished peninsulares, creoles, mestizos, mulatos, zambos, and other combinations. The categories did not operate identically everywhere. Social status could be negotiated through marriage, office, wealth, or petitions. Ancestry nevertheless became legible to the church, the tax collector, the court, and the military recruiter.

In Spanish territories, slavery could be inherited through the mother, purchased freedom could be recognised in law, and manumission could produce intermediate statuses unavailable in the British Caribbean. Those variations gave colonial societies different ways to rank and reproduce unequal labour.

The devices differed in wording. One attached status to the mother; another defined people as chattels; another sorted descent through a vocabulary of mixture. Each converted a relationship created by capture, sale, and force into a condition that appeared to precede the individual. Birth became an administrative event.

When Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in most of its colonies in 1834, freedom arrived through a statute that preserved plantation labour. In Jamaica, emancipation took effect on 1 August 1834, but adults entered apprenticeship and continued working for former owners. Children under six were generally freed immediately; older children and adults remained bound under magistrates and estate managers.

The system collapsed earlier than its designers intended. Jamaican workers resisted through petitions, strikes, movement, and refusal of plantation discipline. Full emancipation followed on 1 August 1838. The end of legal slavery provided no land. Former owners retained the estates, while freed people negotiated wages, provision grounds, housing, and access to courts. The labour regime changed its contract while preserving unequal control of soil and capital.

Brazil abolished slavery on 13 May 1888, when Princess Isabel signed the *Lei Áurea*. The act declared slavery abolished in Brazil and revoked its contrary provisions. No compensation went to the formerly enslaved, and no broad land settlement accompanied freedom. Coffee planters in São Paulo increasingly recruited European immigrants, while many newly freed people entered precarious wage labour, domestic service, sharecropping, or urban work.

Jamaica’s apprenticeship and Brazil’s abolition differed in timing, law, and labour supply, but both left plantation economies standing after the worker’s legal status changed. In Jamaica, the magistrate and contract replaced the owner’s direct claim. In Brazil, the coffee estate retained its land and influence.
