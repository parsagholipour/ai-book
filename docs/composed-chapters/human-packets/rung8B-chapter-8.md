## Chapter 8: States, Slavery, and the Atlantic World

The powder horn was still warm in the hand of the Natchez warrior when the first Frenchman fell at the gate of Fort Rosalie on the morning of 28 November 1729. The fort stood above the Mississippi, on a bluff near the villages the French called White Apple and Flour, a rough enclosure of timber and earth with houses, storehouses, and a small garrison. Since sunrise, men had arrived from Jean-François-Benjamin Dumont de Montigny’s nearby concession, carrying corn and skins. Natchez visitors came among them with baskets and wrapped loads. Some carried guns beneath blankets. Others had left bows and quivers at the door, as visitors did when they entered the French settlement.

Sieur de Chepart, commandant at Natchez, was inside the fort. He had made himself hated before the morning began. The land he wanted lay near White Apple, and he meant to clear it for an indigo plantation. Through their chiefs, speeches, and formal exchanges of gifts, the Natchez resisted the demand. Chepart treated their negotiations as delay and pressed the matter with threats. French reports later made the demand a principal cause of what followed.

At the gate, the visitors waited until enough of their people had entered. Then the guns came out. Jean Rousseau, a French settler, was struck beside the palisade. Another man fell across the storehouse threshold, his hand still on the latch. The attackers moved through the settlement, from cabin to cabin and from the landing to the fields where men were cutting timber. The sound reached the fort in pieces: a shot from the riverbank, shouting behind the houses, and women driven toward the enclosure. No written declaration preceded the attack. The first killing served as its signal, and that signal passed from White Apple to the other Natchez villages.

Enslaved Africans in the fields saw the attack before they understood its extent. Some ran toward the fort. Others stayed where they were, uncertain whether the armed men meant rescue or another captivity. The Natchez took guns and powder from the French houses, opened storehouses, and carried away provisions. Several enslaved people joined them or were taken along; the French accounts, written afterward and shaped by the need to describe a general disaster, did not preserve each person’s choice.

Inside Fort Rosalie, defenders fired through loopholes as smoke gathered beneath the low roof. Chepart’s soldiers tried to hold the gate while families crowded behind them. By then the commandant would have understood that the men outside were not raiders who intended to withdraw after taking supplies. The attack had been arranged across the Natchez settlements, and the French position on the bluff was the place from which colonial authority had spoken.

By midday, the houses below the fort were burning. French reports counted more than a hundred dead, including labourers and settlers along the river, and described captured women and children as part of the colony’s loss. The numbers differ between accounts. The reports turn people into measures of property and power.

The defenders held their fire until the powder was nearly gone. A few Frenchmen tried to reach the river, going down the slope through smoke and trampled grass without a flag or message. Those who reached the boats pushed from the bank while Natchez guns cracked from the houses above. Chepart survived the first assault by leaving with the remnant of the garrison, but his command did not. By evening, the Natchez held the settlement, stores, and fields marked for seizure, while the French houses below Fort Rosalie still burned.

Dumont de Montigny wrote afterward from the French side of the disaster, preserving the sequence of attack and retaliation while placing the Natchez within a vocabulary of conspiracy and savagery. His history shows a settlement whose authority depended on armed possession. Once that possession was challenged, the French administration did not distinguish between the commander who had demanded the land and the communities beneath the fort. Colonists, enslaved Africans, Indigenous allies, women, children, traders, and soldiers occupied different positions in the colony, but the retreating administration gathered their deaths and capture into one account of lost property and lost power. The Natchez had attacked a colonial node: the place where land claims became orders, trade became dependence, and a garrison protected the conversion of a riverbank into a plantation.

A half-century later, another plantation system produced a record in which the dead appeared first as cargo.

On 29 November 1781, the Zong sailed through the Atlantic under Captain Luke Collingwood. The vessel had left the west coast of Africa with more than four hundred enslaved people aboard, bound for Jamaica. Navigation errors lengthened the voyage. Water ran short, disease spread, and the ship passed Jamaica without entering port. Those aboard had been confined in conditions that made death routine.

Collingwood ordered the first group thrown overboard. The later legal report stated that about 150 enslaved people were killed in this way over several days. Some were forced through the ports; others were taken from below deck and cast into the sea. On 1 December, after further deaths, the captain ordered another group killed. The number was eventually given as 132, though accounts varied. The stated reason was that the ship lacked water and that the owners could claim against their insurers if the captives were sacrificed.

The Zong was not prosecuted for murder. When the ship reached Jamaica, its owners claimed that the people thrown overboard had been jettisoned to save the vessel and its remaining cargo. Maritime insurance law allowed a claim when property was deliberately sacrificed for the common safety of a voyage. A first hearing in Jamaica rejected the owners’ account after the insurers refused payment, and the dispute reached London as Gregson v. Gilbert. The arguments concerned the ship’s navigation, its water supply, and whether the alleged necessity was genuine. The lives taken at sea entered the courtroom through the grammar of maritime loss.

Lord Mansfield heard the case at Guildhall in 1783. He treated the killing as evidence about the ship’s circumstances, not as a criminal proceeding against Collingwood or the owners. Robert Stubbs testified about the voyage and shortage of water. Mansfield concluded that the insurer’s refusal could not be overturned on the account presented, especially because the owners’ narrative contained inconsistencies. The insurers won.

The judgment did not declare the killings lawful. It placed them within a property dispute and left the people killed without a criminal forum in which their deaths could be named as murder. A captain could order human beings into the sea while the court considered whether an underwriter owed money.

Granville Sharp obtained papers relating to the case and tried to bring the killings before the public. His campaign connected the Zong to the growing movement against the slave trade, though the legal system offered no easy charge. In 1783, Olaudah Equiano told the story to Anthony Benezet and others. In *The Interesting Narrative*, published in 1789, he described the horror as evidence of what the traffic permitted when African lives were made into insured goods. Equiano did not need to have stood on the Zong’s deck to identify the logic that had carried people there. The court’s language had already exposed it.

The sea concealed the bodies but not the transaction. A claim was drafted, witnesses questioned, a judge weighed evidence, and an insurance company refused payment. The captain supplied the order, the owners the claim, the insurer the dispute, and the court a vocabulary in which killing could be argued as the sacrifice of cargo. The institution made the violence usable.

Plantation law began before a person reached the fields. In Barbados, the 1661 act titled “An Act for the better ordering and governing of Negroes” described enslaved Africans as people who had been “brought into this island and other parts of America” and were to be governed by special rules. It authorised severe punishment, restricted movement, regulated assemblies, and placed policing in the hands of masters, overseers, and officials. The enslaved were described as dangerous subjects and treated as movable property.

How did a code turn purchase into daily command?

A bill of sale transferred a person from one owner to another. A plantation book then assigned a value and a task, while an overseer’s list located the body in a work gang. A magistrate’s warrant gave coercion public force. A runaway advertisement sent the person’s scars, clothing, speech, and presumed destination into public circulation. The same person appeared under different administrative descriptions, but the entries belonged to one system.

On a Jamaican sugar estate, the work gang gathered before daylight. The driver distributed hoes, bills, or cane knives, and the overseer set the pace of cutting, planting, or carrying. Gang labour allowed an owner to measure output collectively while assigning punishment individually. A worker who slowed could be accused of insolence or idleness; the whip enforced the timetable that turned bodies into expected production.

The Jamaican Consolidated Slave Law of 1792 called enslaved people “movable property” and prescribed penalties for escape, theft, assembly, and resistance. It permitted stocks, chaining, branding, and whipping. Night meetings, drumming, and practices colonists identified as Obeah were treated as threats to public order. The law reached into hours when no cane was being cut and made gathering itself suspicious.

Reproduction belonged to the same economy. Under *partus sequitur ventrem*, a child inherited the status of the mother. The birth of an enslaved woman’s child increased an owner’s property without a purchase across the Atlantic. Sexual coercion, forced separation, and the sale of children helped reproduce the labour force that plantation production consumed. A ledger could record a mother, a child, an age, and a price in adjacent lines.

A runaway advertisement reversed the plantation’s private record and sent it into public circulation. In the *Royal Gazette* of Jamaica, an owner might describe an escaped man as “about five feet six inches,” name his clothing, mention a scar, and offer a reward. Ferrymen, constables, sailors, innkeepers, and other enslaved people were compelled to report movement. The advertisement converted a person’s face and history into search instructions.

The question left by these records is how much of a person’s life had to be made countable before cruelty could continue at scale. The answer appears in the overlap between purchase price and task list, between patrol and punishment, and in the child entered as an inheritable asset. Law prepared the categories through which labour, family life, movement, and bodily injury could be managed, extending beyond the authorisation of violence after disorder occurred.

Resistance occupied those same spaces of control, though it never took one form.

The Jamaican Maroons began with fugitives who escaped Spanish and then English slavery into the island’s mountains. At Nanny Town and other settlements, they cultivated provisions, knew the tracks through Cockpit Country and the eastern highlands, and attacked plantations when weapons or food could be taken. British troops struggled against people who refused the estate’s geography. In 1739, Governor Edward Trelawny signed treaties with Maroon communities led by figures including Cudjoe, recognising their settlements while requiring them to return future runaways. Armed resistance secured freedom, but the treaty also made Maroon communities auxiliary hunters within the colonial order.

At Stono, South Carolina, on 9 September 1739, enslaved people gathered near the Stono River under the leadership of men named Jemmy and, in some accounts, Peter. They seized weapons from a store, killed its owners, and moved south toward Spanish Florida, where fugitives had been promised freedom. Their march passed plantations and gathered participants before the colonial militia met them. The *South Carolina Gazette* reported the rising as armed disorder. The legislature answered with the Negro Act of 1740, restricting movement, assembly, literacy, and African cultural practices while tightening plantation discipline.

In Saint-Domingue, resistance included nightly meetings, poisoning accusations, marronage, work slowdowns, religious ceremony, and armed revolt. On 14 August 1791, enslaved people gathered at Bwa Kayiman in the northern colony. Later accounts associate the ceremony with Boukman Dutty and the uprising that began days afterward. Plantations burned across the Plaine du Nord. Insurgents attacked mills, warehouses, overseers’ houses, and the fields whose sugar enriched merchants in Cap-Français, Bordeaux, and Nantes.

The revolution changed language as it changed territory. Toussaint Louverture negotiated with Spanish and French officials, commanded armies, and issued regulations intended to keep plantations producing under a new political order. Jean-Jacques Dessalines took the harder step. At Gonaïves on 1 January 1804, the Haitian Declaration of Independence announced that the former colony had become Haiti and cast emancipation as the recovery of sovereignty from a power that had made enslavement its law. Those once compelled to produce sugar now claimed the right to govern the land and define its enemies.

In Brazil, fugitives established quilombos in forests, hills, and frontier zones. Palmares, in the northeast, endured for much of the seventeenth century, with settlements linked by paths, gardens, workshops, and systems of defence. Colonial officials described its inhabitants as a threat to Portuguese property and sent repeated expeditions against them. In 1694, troops under Domingos Jorge Velho destroyed the principal settlement at Macaco. Zumbi escaped for a time and was captured and killed in 1695. These forms cannot be placed on a single ladder from minor defiance to full revolution. A hidden garden, a treaty, an armed march, or a declaration of independence each altered the plantation’s reach. South Carolina expanded surveillance after Stono. Jamaica negotiated with Maroons when military victory proved costly. Portugal treated Palmares as a territorial rival. Revolutionary Haiti made the plantation state answer to formerly enslaved soldiers.

Saint-Domingue and British Jamaica were both slave societies built around sugar, yet they produced different political worlds. In Saint-Domingue, the richest Caribbean colony before 1791, large plantations stretched across the northern plain and the western and southern provinces. French merchants supplied credit, ships, tools, and manufactured goods; absentee proprietors depended on attorneys and managers; enslaved Africans were continually imported because mortality exceeded natural increase on many estates. Cap-Français connected the plantation interior to Atlantic commerce, while free people of colour occupied an unstable legal position between white supremacy and the enslaved majority.

Jamaica also depended on sugar, imported Africans, and violent labour discipline, but its political structure developed through a different relation between plantation and state. After the English conquest of 1655, planters built assemblies, parish institutions, and militia networks that defended local property while remaining tied to the British imperial navy and commercial system. The mountainous interior gave Jamaican Maroons durable bases, and the treaties with Cudjoe’s communities created a formal distinction between enslaved plantation labourers and armed Black communities recognised as free under colonial conditions.

The difference can be seen in the role of armed force. In Jamaica, militia service was central to white political identity. Planters and overseers assembled against rebellions, patrolled roads, guarded ports, and pursued runaways. During Tacky's War in 1760–61, named for the Akan leader Tacky, the colonial government mobilised militia, regular troops, sailors, and Maroon trackers. Suppression depended on this layered force. Jamaica’s slave regime was defended by a local white military society whose members understood the protection of property as a civic duty.

Saint-Domingue’s rulers possessed soldiers and militias, but racial divisions and imperial rivalries made that force less stable. The French Revolution’s declarations of rights, petitions by free people of colour, conflict between white factions, and foreign intervention opened fractures through which enslaved insurgents moved. When France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1794, the decision followed years of revolt and war rather than peaceful administration. Louverture’s armies then confronted British, Spanish, and French forces while trying to preserve production under emancipation.

Jamaica experienced no comparable successful seizure of the colonial state. The Baptist War of 1831–32, led in part by Sam Sharpe, spread through western Jamaica as enslaved people demanded freedom and refused plantation labour. The rebellion was defeated; hundreds were executed, and Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay in 1832. Parliament abolished slavery in the British colonies in 1833, with apprenticeship delaying full freedom until 1838. Emancipation arrived through imperial legislation after rebellion and abolitionist pressure, while the local plantation class retained land, capital, and political influence.

Haiti’s independence emerged from a war in which enslaved people became the army that destroyed the colonial regime. Dessalines’s declaration named France as the power that had violated liberty and announced a state born through defeat of the plantation order. Jamaica’s emancipation preserved much more of the estate landscape. Formerly enslaved people gained legal freedom but had to seek land, wages, and access to courts in a society where white proprietors still held most of the territory. Neither colony was humane. British Jamaica maintained slavery through militia, law, patrols, and commercial protection; Saint-Domingue maintained it through plantation managers, racial law, military force, and Atlantic credit. Their divergence arose from geography, imperial crisis, demography, the organisation of free people of colour, the strength of local militias, and the success of insurgent armies. The same commodity could be produced under different political pressures, and those pressures changed what violence could accomplish.

After emancipation, coercion survived by changing its paperwork. In Jamaica, planters offered labour contracts for wages while retaining control of housing, provision grounds, and access to employment. Magistrates enforced contract rules; police arrested people accused of vagrancy; merchants advanced goods on credit at prices that kept families in debt. A labourer who left an estate could lose a house, face arrest, or find no other employer willing to hire him. The whip disappeared from the statute, but contract, police, and debt placed work back inside a narrow corridor.

Freedom altered the legal person before it altered the distribution of land. The plantation no longer owned the worker, yet the estate could still own the cane fields, mill, road, and store. Where government defined mobility as vagrancy and treated unpaid debts as criminal evidence, emancipation released the body from sale while leaving livelihood under supervision. In Jamaica, the old violence survived as a system of employment, arrest, and indebtedness.
