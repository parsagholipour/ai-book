## Chapter 1: What Do We Mean by Human Aggression?

> “3. If any one bring an accusation of any crime before the elders, and does not prove what he has charged, he shall, if it be a capital offense charged, be put to death.”
>
> — Hammurabi, *Codex Hammurabi (King translation)*

The herald called Meletus into the court, and the young poet stepped forward before the dikasts. There were 501 of them, seated on wooden benches beneath the open sky of the Agora, each with a voting token in his hand. Socrates stood facing them in the same patched cloak he wore in the streets of Athens. He was seventy years old. The accuser held the formal indictment: Socrates was guilty of not worshipping the gods whom the city worshipped, introducing other divine powers, and corrupting the young.

Anytus was there beside Meletus, a democratic politician and leather manufacturer whose son had spent time with Socrates. Lycon, an orator, completed the prosecution. Their speeches had already been heard. Meletus now answered Socrates, who had taken hold of him by questions. The court was not a private exchange, and Meletus was not speaking to Socrates alone. Every answer was given before the men who would decide whether the city’s accusation had been proved.

“Tell these men, Meletus,” Socrates said, “who improves the young?”

Meletus said that the laws did, and the judges, and the members of the Assembly, and the other Athenians. Socrates asked whether all Athenians improved horses while one man alone ruined them. Meletus agreed. The question turned the prosecution’s general accusation into a claim about responsibility: the whole city improved its young, while Socrates alone corrupted them.

“Then you are very fortunate,” Socrates said, “for you have found that all the Athenians make the young good and honorable, except one man.”

A murmur moved across the benches. Socrates turned again to Meletus. If he corrupted the young, did he do so knowingly or unknowingly? Meletus said knowingly.

“Do you say that knowingly I corrupt the young, and that I make them bad?”

“Yes.”

“Then, by all the gods, Meletus, tell us whether you think it better to live among good citizens or bad ones.”

Good citizens, Meletus said.

And did bad citizens harm those nearest to them, while good citizens helped them? Meletus agreed. Socrates continued along the answer he had forced from him. If he made the young bad, he would eventually suffer from their wrongdoing. No one deliberately brings harm upon himself from the people nearest to him. If the corruption existed, then it was not intentional; and if it was unintentional, the law required instruction rather than prosecution. The court heard a legal accusation being pulled into a contradiction about motive.

Meletus had accused him also of believing in no gods. Socrates pressed him on that point. Did he say Socrates taught divine things? He did. Then Socrates believed in divine things, and no one could believe in the works of horses without believing in horses, or in the music of flute players without believing in flute players. Divine things belonged to divine beings.

“You say that I believe in no gods, and that I believe in gods,” Socrates said. “That is the language of a man who is contradicting himself.”

The exchange did not remove the indictment. Socrates’ questions exposed what he regarded as its weakness, but the dikasts had to decide whether that weakness outweighed the prosecution’s case. Their authority lay not in discovering what Socrates felt or intended, but in converting speeches into a civic judgment. The young poet, the politician, and the orator had brought a charge; 501 citizens would determine whether the city would accept it.

When the speeches ended, the jurors dropped their ballots into the urns. The bronze made a small hard sound each time it struck the vessel. The verdict was guilty. Socrates stood while the prosecutors asked for death.

The court then required him to name his own penalty. He looked toward the benches. The first vote had condemned him; the next would decide what the condemnation required.

“What should be done with a man who has never been quiet, who has neglected what most men care for—money-making, household management, military commands, public speaking—and has spent his days examining himself and others?”

He said that such a man deserved free meals in the Prytaneum, where the city honoured its victors and benefactors. The words landed badly. A penalty that Socrates presented as proper civic recognition sounded to the jurors like defiance. He could pay a fine, he said, but he had no money. Plato and others would stand surety for thirty minae.

The jurors voted again. The death sentence was carried. A public body had determined the charge, heard the defence, received the proposed penalty, and fixed the punishment. Socrates had not been seized in an alley or killed during a quarrel. His death had acquired its force through the sequence of the court’s decisions.

The attendants waited to take Socrates away because the sacred ship to Delos had just been sent, and while it was gone the laws forbade an execution. He remained in Athens, in the prison near the court, with his friends coming to see him. The delay was not mercy granted by an individual jailer. It followed a religious rule recognised by the city, and the rule controlled the moment at which the sentence could be carried out.

On the last day, Crito arrived before sunrise. The prison door stood open. Socrates was awake on his bed, and the chains had been removed from his ankle. In Plato’s account, the room is quiet enough for conversation. The condemned man has not been hidden from his friends, and the jailer is familiar with him. Although the open door stands before him, Socrates remains under sentence, and the day has arrived on which the city’s prohibition expires.

“The ship has come from Delos,” Crito said.

The jailer entered carrying the cup. He had known Socrates for many years and began to weep. His knowledge of Socrates was personal, but his role was official: he was the man who brought the means by which the court’s sentence would become death.

“You are the best and gentlest and noblest of all the men who have ever come here,” he said.

Socrates looked at him.

“Goodbye to you too,” he said, “and we will do as you say.”

The man turned away. Socrates took the cup in both hands, raised it, and drank. The poisoner told him to walk until his legs grew heavy. He handed back the cup. The instruction made the execution a procedure with a sequence that the jailer knew and Socrates followed.

Socrates walked across the room. After a while he lay down. The jailer touched his leg, then another part of him, and said that he was becoming cold. Socrates pulled the covering from his face.

“Crito,” he said, “we owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay it, and do not neglect it.”

“I will,” Crito said. “Is there anything else?”

There was no answer. Crito waited. Then he closed Socrates’ mouth and eyes. Plato’s *Phaedo* records the jailer’s tears, the cup, the walk, the touch that identified the spread of the poison, and Crito’s final act. It gives little account of the attendants or of the preparation of the poison. What it preserves is the passage from verdict to sentence, from sentence to guarded room, and from guarded room to a death administered by a known official.

The scene in Plato’s *Phaedo* leaves violence in an unusual shape. Socrates is not beaten or dragged through a crowd. No executioner’s blade appears. The city condemns him through a vote, delays the sentence according to a religious calendar, places him in a guarded room, and supplies a cup whose contents the jailer knows how to prepare. Anger may have entered the prosecution; resentment may have coloured the speeches of Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon. Yet the death itself came through offices, procedures, and persons performing assigned tasks. The jailer wept, but he carried the cup. That difference gives the word aggression its first boundary. Anger is a feeling, sometimes hot and immediate, sometimes stored for years. It may follow insult, fear, humiliation, or the belief that an injury has gone unanswered. Socrates’ irony in the Athenian court could provoke anger, but irony was not itself an assault. A threat spoken in the Agora could frighten a man without touching him. Aggression begins when conduct is directed toward causing harm, or toward compelling another person through the credible prospect of harm. Violence is narrower: it involves physical force or the immediate use of it. An accusation, a threat, or the seizure of property may be aggressive without becoming physical violence.

The difference between anger and aggression is visible in the trial’s small mechanics. The jurors could condemn Socrates by hearing the prosecution, casting their ballots, and accepting the civic consequence of a guilty verdict, whether or not they felt hatred for him. Plato’s *Apology* presents Socrates as a man who regarded his questioning as a duty, while the city’s legal form translated unease about his teaching into a public charge. A personal encounter could become a civic proceeding. Once that happened, the relevant question was no longer simply who wished whom harm, but which body possessed the right to define harm and impose a penalty.

Interpersonal violence keeps the action closer to the person who inflicts it. A husband striking his wife, a neighbour stabbing a neighbour in Athens, or a slave owner beating a captive may each involve unequal power, but the injury occurs within a relationship that can often be identified: one body, another body, a particular act, a particular wound. The purposes vary. Violence may punish, dominate, frighten, extract sex, settle a grievance, or prevent departure. Its setting can be a house, a road, a workshop, or a prison. The absence of a battlefield does not make it private in consequence, especially where custom or law permits one person to command another. The directness of the act does not tell us whether the surrounding society has authorised it, ignored it, or made it routine.

Raiding changes the scale without yet becoming warfare. A band crosses a boundary, attacks a settlement, seizes cattle or people, burns stores, and withdraws before a larger force can gather. The attack may be carefully planned, but its object is usually limited: movable wealth, captives, revenge, or the display of reach. In the Icelandic sagas, the vocabulary of feud and reprisal often places a household’s armed followers beside the more sudden violence of a cattle raid; in the early medieval Irish annals, cattle and captives appear as both wealth and measures of power. A raid can kill many people, but its organisation is commonly temporary and its political aim narrower than the conquest of a territory.

Warfare requires more than several people hurting several others. It needs an enduring group that can gather fighters, identify an enemy, provision movement, recognise leaders, and decide when violence begins or ends. The group may be a kingdom, a city, a lineage coalition, a confederacy, or an insurgent movement. Spears and slings, muskets and artillery, and drones directed from a room far from the target all extend the reach of a decision. Technology alters distance and speed, while organisation determines whose bodies will be placed at that distance and for what declared purpose. The Assyrian armies represented on the palace reliefs of Nineveh did not emerge from aggression alone; they depended on levies, roads, scribes, officers, deportation policies, and a royal account of legitimate rule.

Policing occupies a difficult place between protection and coercion. A constable restraining a suspected thief, a magistrate ordering a beating, and a secret police officer taking a dissident from his home may all claim to enforce public order. Their actions differ in law, scale, accountability, and target, but each uses an authorised threat to compel conduct. In Athens, the Eleven supervised prisons and executions. In eighteenth-century London, watchmen and magistrates worked through a patchwork of parish offices. In colonial India, police and district officials carried the state’s orders into villages where law was inseparable from revenue collection. The uniform, the warrant, the cell, and the ledger turn force into a public function, whether the person subjected to it accepts the authority or experiences it as unlawful violence.

Organised atrocity is more expansive still. It describes systematic or large-scale violence directed against civilians or against a population defined by identity, status, or political classification. Genocide, crimes against humanity, ethnic cleansing, and some forms of mass enslavement belong to this field, though the legal categories were developed at different times and do not fit every historical case neatly. Atrocity requires a route by which people are classified and then exposed to harm. That route may run through lists, transport, guards, confiscation, propaganda, or administrative language that makes a class of people removable or killable. The killing may be performed by soldiers, police, auxiliaries, settlers, prison officials, or local collaborators. Its distinctive feature is the passage institutions create from classification to harm, regardless of whether every participant feels the same hatred.

The six terms describe different relations between an act and the group that carries it out. Anger names an emotion. Interpersonal violence names harm within a direct relationship. Raiding names a limited armed incursion. Warfare names sustained conflict between organised groups. Policing names authorised coercion in the enforcement of order. Organised atrocity names systematic violence against a population. They can overlap, as colonial rule shows: a soldier may strike a prisoner, a patrol may raid a village, a court may enforce an expropriation, and an administration may arrange forced labour across a whole territory. The same event can be experienced as personal terror and recorded as routine government.

Organisation gives aggression a destination, a schedule, a vocabulary, and a supply line without making it disappear. That is why the number of people involved is less revealing than the path by which a decision reaches them. The cup in Socrates’ prison was an object of death, but the institution is the subject of the scene: the court that condemned him, the law that delayed the execution, and the officials who made the sentence executable.

A buried body preserves no verdict. It preserves what the ground has allowed to remain.

At Jebel Sahaba, on the eastern bank of the Nile in what is now northern Sudan, the dead were placed in a cemetery roughly 13,400 years ago, long before writing, kingship, or cities. The site was excavated in the 1960s by a team associated with the construction of the Aswan High Dam, and the remains are now held at the Musée de l’Homme in Paris. More than sixty individuals were recovered from the cemetery, including men, women, and children. Many had injuries compatible with sharp stone points. Some points remained lodged in bone; other wounds had healed, showing that certain people had survived earlier attacks before they died and were buried at Jebel Sahaba.

The cemetery does not present the dead as a heap left after one visible event. Bodies lay flexed on their sides, often in graves that had been reopened or disturbed by later burials. The people who placed them there carried out a repeated practice rather than leaving the dead where they fell. Grave goods were sparse. The arrangement records attention to the bodies even when the material placed with them was limited, and it suggests that the cemetery remained available to a community that returned to it. That community lived beside a river whose seasonal changes shaped access to fish, plants, game, and habitable ground. Around the cemetery, the archaeological record includes evidence of a difficult late Pleistocene environment, when climatic instability could narrow the places where people could reliably live. The Nile offered a route through that landscape, but it also concentrated movement. Groups using the same productive ground could encounter one another there, and a cemetery beside the river does not tell us whether those encounters were planned, accidental, or recurrent.

Earlier descriptions treated the injuries as evidence of a single massacre. The pattern is more stubborn than that. Projectile points and cuts occur in different parts of the skeleton, and several individuals show multiple episodes of trauma. Healed injuries sit beside fatal ones. The bodies do not all tell the same story. One person may have been struck from the front, another from behind, and another wounded repeatedly over a longer period. The cemetery could have received people killed in raids, ambushes, or recurring clashes between neighbouring groups whose routes converged at the river.

A point embedded in bone is a strong kind of evidence because it preserves the relation between weapon and body. A fracture that healed indicates survival and access to care, even if the care consisted only of rest and protection. A lesion that did not heal indicates that death followed before the body could repair itself. The distribution of wounds can suggest whether projectiles were thrown or shot from a distance, while the absence of certain injuries may reflect the weapon, the angle of attack, or the later destruction of bone. Archaeologists must read the skeleton with the grave, the sediment, and the associated artefacts, because a wound separated from its context easily becomes a story made larger than its evidence.

Jebel Sahaba does show repeated lethal violence. It also shows that violent encounters formed part of the lives of people who buried their dead with care. The community’s existence cannot be reduced to the cemetery’s trauma. Fishing equipment, animal remains, stone tools, and the location beside the Nile point toward labour directed at subsistence and movement as well as conflict. The dead were not merely casualties in an abstract population. They belonged to a group capable of returning to a burial place, handling bodies, and maintaining a relation with the injured until death or recovery.

The assemblage has often been placed in arguments about whether prehistoric societies were more violent than modern states. Such comparisons compress unlike evidence into a single rate. Jebel Sahaba contains bones, points, graves, and sediments; a modern state leaves police reports, hospital records, censuses, newspapers, prisons, and photographs, while also hiding violence in classified files or destroying its victims before they can be counted. The ancient cemetery can establish the occurrence and recurrence of lethal attacks. It cannot provide the denominator needed to rank all human aggression across time. Its value lies in the pattern it preserves: violence was possible, sometimes repeated, and embedded in a social world that also required cooperation.

The injuries raise a question that the graves cannot settle. Were the attacks driven by hunger, territorial pressure, revenge, or a conflict over movement along the river? The environment may have intensified competition, but a harsh landscape does not select victims by itself. People chose targets, carried weapons, recognised allies, and decided whether the dead would be abandoned or buried. Even where no ruler’s seal survives, social decisions remain in the arrangement of the cemetery.

Comparisons become harder when one region leaves a cemetery and another leaves a law code. The injuries at Jebel Sahaba register force on bodies; the record from Babylon registers an official effort to classify force after it occurred. A burial assemblage can show that people were attacked. A statute can show how a court wished injury to be named, valued, and punished. Neither source supplies a universal scale. They answer different questions because different societies made different things durable.

Absence is especially dangerous. No mass grave found in a valley does not prove that its inhabitants lived without violence. Bodies may have been exposed, cremated, washed downstream, buried singly, or destroyed by later settlement. A written archive full of punishments does not prove that every offence was common; it may reveal the anxieties of scribes and rulers more clearly than the conduct of ordinary households. The absence of a weapon from a grave may mean it was not used, or that it was removed, recycled, or never placed with the dead.

What, then, can comparison responsibly ask? It can ask how a society defined an injury, who was entitled to retaliate, which officials could use force, and whether food, land, labour, or captives were made available through violence. It can compare the pathways by which harm became possible rather than pretend that every society left equal counts of harm. Whatever the relative violence of Jebel Sahaba, Babylon, Athens, or a modern city, the question concerns how the Nile cemetery, the Athenian prison, and the Babylonian court converted human conflict into traces of different kinds.

Though uneven, the surviving record preserves wounds, ballots, chains, cups, graves, seals, and sentences. Each trace bears the shape of the decision that produced it. In the Babylon of Hammurabi, about 1750 BCE, the king set the burden of proof for a capital accusation in these terms: “If any one bring an accusation of any crime before the elders, and does not prove what he has charged, he shall, if it be a capital offense charged, be put to death.”
