## Chapter 8: States, Slavery, and the Atlantic World

The Dolben Act received the royal assent on 28 June 1788, though Parliament renewed and altered its provisions before making the measure permanent in 1799. Its sponsor, Sir William Dolben, had seen the slave ship *Zong* discussed in Parliament after its owners claimed insurance for enslaved Africans thrown into the sea. The bill that followed did not abolish the trade, question the ownership of people, or call murder by its name when captives were cast overboard. It regulated the number of bodies a ship could carry. That regulation took the form of a diagram. Printed with the bill, the ship’s plan divided the decks into spaces for men, women, and children. The bodies appeared as measurements—rows, compartments, and shaded areas showing how many captives could be carried under the proposed rule. The diagram translated a voyage into a problem of capacity. A vessel of 201 tons, for example, received one allowance; a larger vessel received another, according to a scale Parliament treated as a tolerable relation between cubic measure and human life.

The scale described no ship as it actually existed. It instructed the owner. The master was to calculate tonnage, multiply it by the prescribed allowance, and remain within the resulting number. For vessels under 200 tons, the bill allowed one enslaved person for each ton. Between 200 and 300 tons, it allowed one person for each ton up to 200, then one person for every two tons above that threshold. As ships grew larger, the ratio declined. Parliament accepted the argument that the smallest vessels were most crowded and that larger ones could carry people with somewhat less compression. The arithmetic looked moderate because violence had already been made measurable.

A second schedule addressed time at sea. Ships sailing from Africa to the West Indies received a maximum number of captives according to tonnage and the length of the voyage. On a longer passage, the permitted density was lower. But a voyage of two weeks and one of several months did not bring different air, water, or food into the hold. The difference entered as a column in a table. A captain could satisfy the table while men lay shackled in pairs, women were confined separately, and children were placed wherever a human body could be fitted.

Parliament’s language returned repeatedly to health. The bill proposed rules for ventilation, cleanliness, diet, and the treatment of the sick. It required inspection before departure and empowered appointed officers to record the number of people on board. Supporters presented the measure as a means of preventing mortality that discredited British commerce and endangered sailors as well as captives. The ship became a dangerous environment requiring supervision, like a mine or a crowded barracks. Enslaved people entered the language of preservation because their deaths reduced the value of the cargo and threatened the reputation of the trade.

The diagram exposes the limit of that remedy. A plan can show where a body is expected to lie. It cannot show the heat of the deck, the sound of coughing in darkness, or the force with which an iron shackle cuts an ankle when the ship rolls. Parliament did not lack descriptions of those things. Witnesses had reported the smell, the crowding, disease, and the cries of people packed below deck. The diagram answered that evidence by setting a legal maximum.

Dolben had inspected a slave ship in the Thames in 1788. He later told the Commons that the sight had shocked him. His speech made the hold visible to men who would never enter it: captives lying on shelves, unable to sit upright, surrounded by excrement and sickness. He described the enslaved as treated worse than animals while speaking inside a political world that accepted the trade’s continuation. His bill moved between compassion and management. It condemned excess while leaving ownership intact.

Abolition would have challenged the merchants, planters, insurers, shipowners, and state that protected the commerce. Regulation offered a narrower remedy and promised that the trade might continue in cleaner form. The ship would still sail from Liverpool or Bristol to the African coast, exchange firearms and manufactured goods for captives, cross the Atlantic, and discharge sugar-bound labourers in Jamaica or Barbados. The legal alteration concerned the number assigned to its tonnage.

The measure created a new form of evidence. The captain’s declaration, the ship’s register, an inspector’s observation, and the mortality figures could be compared after the voyage. A dead captive could appear in a total. A vessel could be ranked by the number carried per ton. Owners could be fined for exceeding the allowance, while their losses could still be discussed through insurance. The maritime bureaucracy that made a voyage financeable now made its human contents auditable.

The *Zong* case had already shown the consequences of that bookkeeping. In November and December 1781, Captain William Collingwood’s ship carried more than four hundred enslaved Africans toward Jamaica after a navigational error delayed its arrival. Disease spread and water became scarce. Collingwood and his officers ordered 122 people thrown overboard, while others died aboard ship. The owners claimed that the captives had been jettisoned because the vessel faced shortage and that the loss was covered by marine insurance. Lord Mansfield’s notes on *Gregson v. Gilbert* record the narrow question presented to the court: whether the insurers should pay.

Mansfield’s hearing concerned a contract involving the men responsible for homicide. The dead entered the courtroom as insured property, and the judge rejected the claim because the evidence did not establish the necessity alleged by the owners. That decision was a moral failure. It also reveals the legal architecture that made the massacre intelligible to British commerce. People could be insured, lost, and claimed for. The court did not need to deny their humanity in abstract terms; it needed only to let the policy’s categories control.

Seven years later, the Dolben Act entered this world of schedules and claims. Its plan of the hold stood within the same conversion of bodies into cargo. The measure sought to improve the calculation rather than end it. Tonnage became a proxy for breathable space, the length of the voyage a proxy for risk, and an inspector’s certificate a proxy for humane conduct. Violence was not removed from the voyage. It was placed under a rule.

Enforcement remained difficult. Ships sailed from ports far from Westminster. They carried captives taken through African wars and raids that British officials did not control, then crossed an ocean where no inspector could watch the hold continuously. A captain could repair a count after a death, conceal the circumstances of a beating, or alter the ship’s records. Although the Act could not govern every action, its weakness showed how much authority remained with the owner, captain, surgeon, and crew.

In 1799 Parliament made the tonnage regulations permanent and extended the principle of supervision. Abolitionists then used the Act’s images against the trade. Diagrams devised to regulate crowding could be displayed as evidence of crowding. A reader who saw black rows compressed beneath a deck no longer needed to imagine the ship as an abstract carrier of goods. The law had supplied an image of the violence it attempted to contain.

The Atlantic archive preserves the decisions of men who possessed the power to name, count, confine, and sell. A parliamentary bill, a ship’s plan, an insurance action, and a captain’s log do not preserve the same human experience, but they preserve the administrative marks violence left behind. The number written where a person had been can be followed back to the rule that permitted confinement and forward to the market that priced the survivor.

The hold was one room in a larger machine. Its dimensions mattered because the plantation had already created a demand for labour, the merchant had raised credit, the state had protected a route, and the buyer expected sugar. On a Jamaican estate, the ship’s captive was not the end of a transaction. He or she was the beginning of a regimen joining land, debt, work, punishment, and accounting.

A sugar plantation began with land that could be claimed, surveyed, and cleared. In Jamaica, mountains and valleys divided estates into parishes, while productive cane lands were concentrated in districts such as St James, Clarendon, and St Thomas-in-the-Vale. Forest had to be cut, roots removed, roads opened, and water directed towards mills. Before the first crop could be sold, the planter borrowed for enslaved labourers, livestock, boilers, carts, and buildings. The mortgage attached the land to the people forced to work it.

Cane exhausted soil quickly and demanded exact timing. Planting, cutting, crushing, boiling, and curing followed one another with little interval. Once cut, cane had to reach the mill before its juice deteriorated. The mill governed the plantation clock. During crop time its rollers turned through the night, powered first by animals and later by water or steam, while enslaved workers in the boiling houses converted juice into crystallised sugar.

Gang labour arranged the field by age, sex, and assigned capacity. The first gang performed the heaviest work, cutting cane and digging drains. A second followed with lighter field tasks, while children and older people weeded, carried, or tended provisions. An overseer or driver set the pace. The plantation book imposed the division, turning a group of people into a sequence of tasks and hours.

The task system worked differently but served the same demand for extraction. Each person or small group received a defined amount of work: a number of cane rows to weed or a stretch of ditch to clear. The task might be measured in acres, bundles, carts, or hours. When it was finished, an enslaved worker might cultivate a provision ground, mend clothing, visit family, or rest. When unfinished, the task exposed the compulsion underlying its apparent flexibility: the driver could demand further labour or punishment.

At Codrington in Barbados, Christopher Codrington’s will in 1710 directed that the plantations be given to the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel and that at least 300 enslaved people be maintained there. The instruction joined philanthropy to ownership. A religious society inherited fields, mills, houses, and people as parts of one estate, and its income depended on the labour discipline that supported a private planter.

When the society eventually received compensation for 411 enslaved people after emancipation in 1838, the state’s payment recorded the person once more as an asset. The language of improvement had not displaced the language of property.

Jamaican credit made ownership expandable. A planter could mortgage an estate and its enslaved population to obtain funds for equipment or additional labourers. The mortgage depended on anticipated crops, market prices, and the assessed condition of the people attached to the land. Fever could reduce productive capacity; rebellion could damage buildings and interrupt milling; a fall in sugar prices could make a profitable estate insolvent. Within that calculation, punishment appeared as maintenance and food as investment.

The driver stood between the written order and the body in the field. He carried a whip, supervised the gangs, and reported output. The overseer recorded the day’s work and identified those who failed, ran away, stole cane, slowed the gang, or resisted an instruction. Magistrates and parish officials existed outside the estate, but the owner’s authority penetrated daily life more continuously than any court. A person could be sold, confined, beaten, separated from a child, or sent to another property through decisions made in a counting house.

Sugar made the mill a central instrument of discipline. During harvest, an estate might work through the night because crushed cane could not wait. Women cut and stripped cane, men hauled loads and worked machinery, and boiling-house labourers endured heat and fumes. Children carried fuel or fed animals. The crop’s schedule became an argument for coercion: any pause threatened waste, and resistance could be described as economic loss.

The plantation book made this argument durable. It recorded births, deaths, purchases, punishments, rations, illnesses, tools, and yields. On one page a person appeared as a name or number; on another, as a driver, carpenter, field hand, or invalid. Such categories distributed labour and helped owners estimate returns. They also show how care was forced into the system. Food had to be issued because labourers needed strength, medicines because sickness reduced output, and repairs because tools affected production. Provision sustained ownership; it did not soften it.

A plantation produced sugar by making violence routine enough to disappear into procedure. The lash was one part of the process, alongside sale, patrol, the pass system, confinement, and the threat of removal. Resistance ranged from slowing work and damaging equipment to flight, poisoning, conspiracy, and revolt. The estate answered by enlarging surveillance. Its order was never complete, which is why it required repeated acts of enforcement.

In South Carolina, the Stono rebels moved on Sunday, 9 September 1739, near the Stono River southwest of Charleston. The date was the feast of the Nativity of the Virgin Mary, and the colony’s white militia was depleted because many men had gone to attend elections. The record begins with an armed group at Hutchinson’s store. They killed the two men present, took weapons, and gathered followers as they marched south.

According to the account later used by the colonial assembly, the rebels carried guns and displayed a banner. Their leaders included Jemmy, described as an Angolan, and other Catholics or former Catholics who may have understood Spanish Florida as a place where escape was possible. They killed several white colonists, burned houses, and moved towards the Edisto River. Weapons were taken, people recruited, and a route chosen.

Lieutenant Governor William Bull encountered the group with four companions. His report to the assembly described seeing armed rebels on the road and turning back to alert the colony. South Carolina’s militia assembled under Colonel James Moore. By the afternoon of 10 September, the militia had overtaken the rebels. A battle followed. Survivors dispersed into the woods or were captured.

The colonial account reported that about forty-four enslaved people and twenty-one whites had been killed during the rising and its suppression, though later reconstructions vary. The assembly responded with executions, sales away from the colony, and a new Negro Act in 1740. The statute restricted movement, assembly, education, and the hiring of enslaved people; it also tightened patrols and expanded owners’ control over work and punishment. A rebellion became evidence for a wider system of inspection, and the militia’s victory became a statute governing those who had not rebelled.

Brazil and Jamaica produced similar commodities through different colonial histories. On a Brazilian *engenho*, the mill and boiling house stood within an estate that combined cane fields, workshops, a chapel, storehouses, and quarters. Portuguese America had developed sugar production from the sixteenth century, and by the eighteenth century Bahia and Pernambuco possessed long-established plantation landscapes. The owner’s household, priests, overseers, artisans, and enslaved workers occupied one compound whose name could refer both to the mill and to the whole enterprise.

Jamaica’s plantation parish had a more fragmented appearance. Estates spread across valleys and hills, separated by roads, forests, neighbouring properties, and towns such as Spanish Town or Montego Bay. The dispersed landscape did not mean weaker control. Parish vestries, magistrates, militia patrols, and estate managers connected properties to colonial government. A runaway could leave one plantation and enter another jurisdiction without leaving the reach of advertisements, passes, or mounted patrols.

In Brazil, the *senhor de engenho* relied on a long-established Portuguese legal and religious vocabulary. Baptism, marriage, manumission, and Catholic brotherhoods gave enslaved people recognised but sharply limited places within colonial society. In Jamaica, Anglican institutions were less integrated into plantation life during the earlier period, and planters often treated Christian instruction as a threat to discipline. These differences shaped the spaces in which enslaved people formed families, worshipped, negotiated, and sought freedom.

The labour process varied as well. Brazilian estates commonly organised field workers in gangs, while Jamaican planters developed task work alongside gang labour, especially where work could be assigned by measured quantity. A Jamaican worker might complete a specified area of hoeing and then use the remaining time on a provision plot. A Brazilian field gang moved under closer collective direction. One system pressed the worker through a quota; the other kept the body within a synchronised group. Neither released labour from coercion.

Scale altered the pressures. Brazil’s plantation zones included large estates, but sugar production coexisted with cattle raising, subsistence cultivation, mining, urban markets, and vast interior frontiers. Jamaica’s economy became unusually dependent on sugar and imported enslaved labour. By the eighteenth century, its planters were intensely exposed to Atlantic prices, hurricanes, war, and mortality. A Jamaican estate could be prosperous in one account year and burdened with debt in the next, encouraging severe extraction from a workforce owners replenished through purchase.

The colonies also differed in the geography of escape. Brazil’s forests and interior settlements supported *quilombos*, including Palmares, which survived through much of the seventeenth century before Portuguese forces destroyed its main settlement in 1694. Jamaica’s mountainous interior became the refuge of Maroon communities descended from people who escaped Spanish and later British plantations. The First Maroon War ended with treaties in 1739 and 1740. Communities at places such as Accompong received land and a degree of autonomy in exchange for returning new runaways and assisting colonial authorities. That bargain made Jamaica’s state visible in a particular way. The colony could not conquer every mountain refuge, so it recognised some fugitives as political communities while requiring them to police the boundary of slavery. Brazil’s authorities likewise alternated between military destruction, negotiation, and incorporation. Terrain, military expense, rival imperial claims, and local organisation changed the terms of plantation power.

An *engenho* and a Jamaican parish were not different because one rested on coercion and the other on freedom. Both joined land, credit, law, and armed force to the sale of sugar. Brazil’s Catholic institutions and interior frontiers gave enslaved people certain routes for negotiation and flight; Jamaica’s concentrated sugar economy and British military structures produced others. The Atlantic market could support distinct local systems because states and communities made distinct choices about labour, settlement, religion, and force.

Economic interest explains why planters purchased captives and merchants carried sugar, but it does not by itself explain the form slavery took. A society could seek cheap labour without defining a child’s status through the mother, forbidding a gathering, authorising corporal punishment, or granting a patrol the right to enter a cabin at night. Those rules required arguments about persons.

The Barbados Slave Code of 1661 supplied them in unusually direct language. It described enslaved Africans as people brought to the island and treated them as a dangerous population requiring control. The statute authorised severe punishment, restricted movement, and protected owners from ordinary consequences when an enslaved person died under correction. Later Barbadian laws expanded the regulations. The code made racial slavery hereditary and placed public force behind private ownership.

Edward Long, writing in *The History of Jamaica* in 1774, defended the colonial order through claims about African difference. He described Africans as naturally inferior and used comparisons with animals and ancient peoples to present enslavement as a civilising necessity. His prose did not create the plantation economy, which preceded his book. It gave the economy a learned vocabulary in which domination could appear as knowledge and cruelty as improvement.

Profit was indispensable. Sugar, land, credit, and shipping explain the persistence of bondage. They do not explain why law made Blackness a mark of inherited status, why Christian instruction could coexist with family separation, or why a planter’s violence was discipline while an enslaved person’s resistance was crime. The Barbados code and Long’s defences show that slavery needed a theory of who could be owned, not merely a market that rewarded ownership.

The people who lived under these rules made communities that the records only partly preserve. In Jamaica, Maroon settlements at Nanny Town and later Accompong defended territory, cultivated food, exchanged intelligence, and negotiated with British officers. Nanny, remembered in Jamaican traditions as a leader of the Windward Maroons, became associated with resistance in the Blue Mountains, though surviving colonial records describe her unevenly and often through hostile eyes. The treaty of 1739 recognised that escape could become sovereignty when fugitives held ground, organised defence, and made conquest too costly.

On the plantations, survival also took quieter forms. At Newton Plantation near Bridgetown, Barbados, archaeological work in the burial ground identified the remains of enslaved people whose lives had left few written traces. Coffins, burial positions, beads, buttons, nails, and the condition of bones showed choices in mourning and evidence of lives shaped by hard labour, repeated injury, illness, and childbirth. The graves placed individual bodies within a community, while owners had counted them chiefly as labour in the estate ledger.

What could a burial mean to the people who prepared it? It may have been an assertion of kinship, a Christian rite, an African practice carried across the ocean, or a negotiation among traditions that no single record can separate. The answer may depend on comparing Newton with plantation inventories, church registers, oral histories, Maroon traditions, and the changing patterns of names in manumission papers.

Survival was not the opposite of accommodation. A person might work within the task system, cultivate provisions, attend a baptism, bargain with an overseer, hide a child’s identity, and still resist the terms imposed by the estate. Another might flee to the mountains and later return under treaty conditions. The Atlantic world offered constrained choices, and each choice unfolded inside laws that had already decided whose freedom could be taken.
