## Chapter 8: States, Slavery, and the Atlantic World

The diagram of the *Brookes* made a ship into an argument. Printed when abolitionists sought to show British readers what the Atlantic trade required, it represented enslaved Africans arranged in close rows below deck, bodies fitted to a space measured for cargo. The image did not show the negotiations on the African coast, the merchants who financed the voyage, or the plantation account that awaited the ship’s arrival. It showed the hold. Its force came from converting an arrangement normally hidden from public sight into a surface that could be inspected: a plan of bodies and restricted movement. The vessel appeared as a machine for keeping people alive long enough to sell them.

By the time such images circulated, the traffic had joined Bristol, Barbados, Jamaica, West and Central Africa, and the markets of the Caribbean in a single commercial chain. Between 1698 and 1807, an estimated 2,108 ships departed from Bristol for Africa, carrying more than 500,000 enslaved Africans to the Caribbean and North America. In 1749 alone, Bristol vessels transported 8,560 people valued at £149,400. The number belongs to a port, but its consequences were carried by people who never saw Bristol: those confined on ships, families broken on the African coast, and labourers whose forced work produced sugar after the crossing. A price could be entered in a ledger at Bristol while fever, thirst, resistance, and death were endured in the space the *Brookes* diagram made visible.

Mortality during the Middle Passage was not an accidental subtraction from an otherwise orderly enterprise. It was built into a voyage in which captains, investors, and crews treated human beings as cargo whose value could be compared with provisions and freight. The ship required food and water, but it also required confinement. The people below deck were denied the room needed to stand, turn, sleep, or separate the sick from the healthy. Disease moved through the hold because the arrangement that increased the number carried reduced the conditions under which they might survive. A ship could reach the Caribbean with fewer people than it had taken aboard and still be called profitable.

The deaths at sea changed their setting on arrival. A survivor landed in Jamaica with a body weakened by confinement and with a status already assigned by sale. The island’s plantation economy required workers who could be compelled to cut cane and continue through the injuries and illness produced by the work itself. Sugar was a crop of sequence: planting, weeding, cutting, crushing, boiling, curing, and shipping had to be coordinated, and delay at one stage could spoil the value of another. Enslaved labour was made to answer the clock of the mill.

A plantation was a settlement and workplace as well as an accounting unit, connected by roads and coastal shipping to Kingston and to Atlantic buyers. The person who cut cane was also counted as property. The person who became ill affected the value of an estate. A child born on a plantation could be entered into the future labour supply, while an older worker might be assigned tasks according to declining strength. An overseer translated a daily order into movement, pace, and punishment. The commercial logic of the voyage thus entered the ordinary hours of Jamaican life. The hold had been measured for bodies; the plantation measured their time.

The Codrington estates in Barbados make the connection between moral language and forced labour especially plain. Christopher Codrington bequeathed the plantations in 1710 to the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts, directing that “three hundred negros at Least always Kept there on.” The sentence treated the continued presence of enslaved people as a condition attached to property and charitable purpose. A college was to be funded, but the funding rested on sugar estates whose operations required the coercion of those three hundred people and others around them. Christian instruction and plantation revenue did not occupy separate worlds. They were administered together.

The word “Society,” branded on enslaved people at Codrington, compressed that connection into a mark on the body. Branding identified ownership and announced it publicly. The chest became a place where an institution wrote its name into flesh. The practice ceased after 1741, following complaints and a march by enslaved people to Bridgetown. That change followed action by the people subjected to it. Their action forced an institution to alter one practice while leaving the plantation economy in place.

Jamaican labour regimes depended upon more than the private temper of individual planters. A cruel overseer could inflict immediate pain, but the plantation’s power came from the authority surrounding him, the courts that protected ownership, the patrols that pursued escapees, and the merchants who advanced credit. When an enslaved worker slowed, fled, broke a tool, concealed food, preserved a language, or refused an instruction, the response travelled through these connections. The lash was visible; the credit system, the sale, and the legal title made it durable.

Bristol’s ships carried manufactured goods and weapons toward Africa, returned with captives, and brought sugar and tobacco into a metropolitan economy that refined, processed, insured, and sold them. The profits reached merchants, investors, shipbuilders, and local industries. A household in England could sweeten tea without seeing the boiling house in Jamaica, just as a reader of an abolitionist tract could study the *Brookes* without entering its hold. Distance concealed the relation between consumption and compulsion, but the ships maintained it.

The Atlantic system also grew through the destruction and displacement of Indigenous peoples. European colonizers attempted to enslave Indigenous populations, while disease, famine, warfare, and resistance transformed the labour supply on which colonial ventures depended. In the Caribbean, Taíno resistance to Spanish enslavement was met with massacres. In North America, the Yamasee War formed part of a wider history in which Indigenous resistance altered the balance among traders, colonies, and enslaving powers. The turn toward African labour was not a simple substitution made by an abstract market. It followed encounters in which peoples resisted, died, fled, negotiated, and were dispossessed.

A labour shortage described what planters wanted and did not possess in sufficient quantity. It did not explain why one population was hunted, another transported, and a third enclosed behind colonial boundaries. The Atlantic economy demanded workers, but states and merchants helped decide whose freedom could be taken and whose resistance would be answered with armed force. The plantation was profitable because law made a human being transferable and armed institutions made refusal costly.

The consequences reached forward into abolition, emancipation, and memory. The SPG continued to benefit from Codrington’s plantations until 1838, when slavery in the British colonies ended. That date closed one legal regime, but it did not erase wealth accumulated through generations of uncompensated labour or the classifications built around it. The institution had survived changes in management, religious justification, and public opinion because it was embedded in land, credit, shipping, inheritance, and state power. Its end required the withdrawal of legal protection from ownership in persons.

A diagram such as the *Brookes* could expose the arrangement, but exposure alone did not dismantle it. The hold was an image of confinement; Jamaican sugar was the commodity that made confinement pay; Barbados law was the framework that made ownership intelligible to courts and creditors. Across the Atlantic, violence became ordinary by being divided among offices and occupations. The captain supervised the voyage, the buyer inspected the captive, the planter assigned the task, the magistrate heard the complaint, and the merchant settled the account. Each could describe a limited duty. The system joined those duties into a life.

The Barbados Slave Code of 1661 turned this divided violence into a legal order. Its language placed enslaved people within a category of property while specifying the punishments through which that property was to be governed. The code was not a description of plantation life written from outside it. It instructed colonial officials, masters, and courts about what they were permitted to recognise. A person could be held, transferred, disciplined, and inherited because the law gave those acts a form.

Property law supplied the central grammar. Land could be owned and goods could be moved between estates; the code drew enslaved Africans into the same world of possession while preserving a separate set of rules for punishing them. Enslaved people were valuable because they were living workers, yet they were treated legally as objects of ownership. They could be made responsible for offences against white authority while being denied the freedom that would make responsibility equal before the law. That double position appears in the treatment of movement. A person who left an estate without permission was not merely absent from work. Flight challenged the owner’s title, the overseer’s command, and the expectation that the body purchased at sale would remain available. Punishment for running away therefore defended a property relation through bodily pain. The law could describe the offence as an individual act while protecting the larger claim that one human being belonged to another.

The punishments specified by the code included whipping, mutilation, and death for offences defined in relation to colonial order. Their significance lay not only in their cruelty but in their placement within a statute. Violence that might have been condemned as assault in another setting was authorised when directed by a master or official against an enslaved person under approved conditions. The code did not abolish private violence; it sorted private violence into permitted and prohibited forms. It protected white people from some acts by enslaved people while giving white owners wide power over those they held.

A statute also required administration. Magistrates, constables, patrols, jailers, and plantation managers translated its categories into decisions. The code’s power lay in the expectation that an enslaved person could be pursued, confined, questioned, punished, or returned, and that colonial officers would treat these actions as part of their work. The office that pursued a runaway joined the estate that claimed him. The court that heard a case stabilised the market in which he had been sold.

Religious language could stand beside this property order without displacing it. The wider Atlantic system claimed that enslaved people might be Christianised while remaining in bondage. The SPG’s management of Codrington illustrates the arrangement: evangelisation and financial benefit were attached to the same plantations. Christian instruction could be offered as a duty while the law preserved hereditary enslavement. A religious obligation did not make the enslaved person a rights-bearing equal. It could instead make discipline appear compatible with care.

The code’s racial categories made that compatibility easier to sustain. Enslavement was increasingly associated with African descent, and colonial law hardened the division between white freedom and Black bondage. In Virginia, a statute of 1662 declared that children inherited the “condition” of their enslaved mother, ensuring that the status of slavery passed through birth. Barbados and Virginia were not governed by identical texts, but the principle that slavery could reproduce itself biologically gave owners a future labour force and gave racial hierarchy a legal shape.

A legal category could outlast the argument that first defended it. The code did not need every planter to articulate the same theology or every magistrate to share the same private motive. It needed them to recognise who could be owned and who could command. A visitor, merchant, or minister might object to particular excesses while accepting the arrangement that made the objections necessary. The statute’s narrow condemnation of unauthorised cruelty could coexist with its broad protection of ownership.

The body remained the place where this order was enforced. A branded chest at Codrington, a scar from whipping, a confinement cell, and a notice concerning a runaway belonged to different moments of punishment, yet each made the same legal division visible. The body of the enslaved person carried value for the owner and vulnerability before the law. The Barbados code converted that vulnerability into a colonial rule.

Read as property law rather than as a list of punishments, the code organised inheritance, sale, labour, movement, and discipline around a racial division that colonial government maintained. It did not merely reflect violence already present on plantations. It gave violence a recognised direction. A master’s command could be treated as management; an enslaved person’s refusal could be treated as crime. The law made coercion appear as administration.

On a Sunday in September 1739, enslaved people gathered near Stono in South Carolina. The surviving accounts name Jemmy as a leader and describe a movement towards Spanish Florida, where the participants understood that freedom might be available under Spanish rule. They seized weapons and killed two storekeepers, then moved along the road, recruiting others and setting fire to buildings. The episode entered colonial memory as the Stono Rebellion, but its first hours were lived as a decision to leave a plantation society that defined African descent as perpetual labour.

Florida was not an imagined place at the edge of the world. It was a competing imperial territory whose promises gave flight a direction. The rebels carried weapons because escape across a frontier required protection, and they moved together because a small group could be captured more easily than a larger one. Their route connected local knowledge of roads and plantations to the international rivalry between Britain and Spain.

South Carolina’s white colonists responded by assembling the militia. The militia pursued the rebels, and a battle followed in which many were killed. Colonial authorities later displayed severed heads along the road as a warning. The punishment addressed more than those who had taken part. It was intended for enslaved observers, planters uncertain of their security, and anyone considering whether Spanish Florida could offer a route out. The state answered a collective act with a public demonstration of its power to identify, kill, and mark bodies.

The surviving descriptions were written by colonial observers, and they preserve the authorities’ fear more fully than the rebels’ speech. Jemmy’s name remains, along with the destination, the weapons, the fires, and the militia response. The purposes of each participant cannot be recovered with equal detail. Some joined the march; others were compelled or persuaded; some may have seen Florida, religion, revenge, or immediate escape as the decisive aim. The sources support the event’s sequence more securely than the inner life of those who moved through it.

South Carolina’s response continued after the fighting. The colony tightened controls over movement and assembly and placed greater pressure on communication among plantations. These measures treated the rebellion as a problem of circulation. Roads, meetings, weapons, and messages could connect people dispersed across estates. The plantation system required separation for supervision, but the same roads that carried goods could carry news and fugitives.

Stono exposes the limits of an economic description of slavery. The rebels did not attack a sugar mill in order to renegotiate wages, nor did they petition for a different allocation of plantation profit. They acted against a system that claimed their persons. The route towards Florida, the seizure of arms, and the militia’s pursuit show a political conflict over jurisdiction and freedom. The colony’s violence had economic results, but the challenge it faced was political.

The authorities’ use of public punishment repeated the logic seen in the Barbados code. A body could be made into evidence of the state’s right to govern. The severed head was meant to transform rebellion into a lesson, just as a brand transformed ownership into a mark. In both cases, violence worked through an audience. The target was one person or several; the intended reach was a population.

Resistance in the Atlantic did not take one form because enslavement did not operate in one setting. On the mainland, maroon communities occupied forests, mountains, swamps, and borderlands where plantation patrols found pursuit difficult. In Jamaica, communities of escaped people made flight a continuing political fact, not merely an individual disappearance. Their survival depended on food, paths, warning systems, and armed defence. A settlement that remained beyond the reach of a planter’s court challenged the claim that every enslaved person belonged within plantation jurisdiction.

Work slowdowns used a different geography. The field, the mill, the kitchen, or the workshop became a place where time could be withheld. A tool could be mishandled or a task delayed. Such acts rarely entered colonial accounts in proportion to their frequency because owners recorded lost output more readily than workers’ intentions. Yet the plantation’s demand for speed made delay consequential. Sugar production was vulnerable to interruption, and the worker who controlled one part of the process possessed a form of leverage.

Poisoning accusations reveal the fear produced by that leverage. Planters and officials sometimes accused enslaved people of using poison against owners, overseers, livestock, or fellow workers. The accusation could arise from illness, death, or rumours carried between plantations, and it could produce trials and executions. Its importance lay in the limits of surveillance. The master might command the field, but he could not easily see what entered food, medicine, or water. Suspicion filled the space where supervision failed.

Literacy networks carried resistance beyond the immediate plantation. Reading and writing could transmit a message, preserve a religious text, assist a petition, or link people separated by sale. Where colonial authorities feared gatherings and correspondence, the ability to read a notice or compose a letter altered the distribution of knowledge. Literate enslaved people were not simply recipients of European religion or law. They could use the forms of those institutions to communicate, warn, and organise.

Shipboard revolts began under the conditions represented by the *Brookes*. The hold was designed to prevent movement, but confinement concentrated people who carried knowledge of navigation, language, warfare, and the coast. Captives could refuse food, damage equipment, attack crew members, jump overboard, or coordinate an uprising when a watch weakened. A revolt at sea faced weapons, chains, and the ocean. Its repeated possibility forced crews and owners to treat the enslaved not as inert cargo but as people capable of collective action.

Resistance could also be religious and cultural without fitting neatly into a European category of revolt. Names, burial practices, songs, kinship, foodways, and forms of worship maintained social worlds that sale and transport sought to break apart. Cultural preservation did not remove the need for flight or armed struggle. It supplied relationships through which those actions could become imaginable. The person who remembered a language, learned a route, or carried a story between estates helped keep the enslaved population from becoming the isolated labour force owners wanted.

Indigenous resistance altered the Atlantic system as well. Taíno opposition to Spanish enslavement, the uprisings of Africans on São Tomé, and the Yamasee War each forced colonisers to revise their expectations about labour and territory. The São Tomé revolt led by Amador in 1595 included more than half of the island’s enslaved population and contributed to the abandonment of slave-based sugar production there. A revolt could damage not only a plantation but the viability of a colonial economy.

The forms differed, but each interfered with the claim made by slaveholders that an enslaved person was available for command at every moment. Maroons withdrew bodies from the plantation. Slowdowns withdrew time. Poisoning accusations revealed concealed knowledge. Literacy networks withdrew information from exclusive white control. Shipboard revolts challenged the movement of people across the sea. Resistance did not always overthrow the law, but it forced the law to show its armed foundations.

The Atlantic slave system was economic in a direct and substantial sense. Sugar, tobacco, and later cotton generated revenue; enslaved people were bought and sold; plantations were pledged in credit markets; ships, insurers, merchants, and processors profited from the trade. Bristol’s 1749 figures show commerce attaching prices to human transport, while the Codrington bequest shows a religious corporation treating a plantation and its enslaved population as a source of income. Any account that removed profit from this history would remove one of its central causes.

Economic determinism becomes too narrow when it treats the price of labour as a sufficient explanation for the form slavery took. Demand for workers did not itself determine why African descent became the basis of hereditary status, why children followed the condition of enslaved mothers, or why a runaway was punished as a criminal while an owner’s title was protected as property. Markets required rules. In the Atlantic colonies, legislators and judges supplied rules that made some people alienable, inheritable, and permanently unequal.

Racialisation was not a decorative ideology placed over an economic core. It solved practical problems for slaveholders by giving the labour system a visible and reproductive boundary. When enslavement passed through the mother, the child born on a plantation entered the owner’s property without a new voyage from Africa. When Blackness was associated with bondage and whiteness with freedom, patrols and courts could identify whom to watch and whom to empower. The law converted descent into an economic asset.

The legal order also protected the system from the instability that ordinary property disputes could produce. An enslaved person could be mortgaged, inherited, insured, or sold, but could also be punished for attempting to leave. Property law therefore joined criminal law. Courts, patrols, jails, and militia defended the owner’s claim. The response to Stono demonstrates the arrangement: South Carolina treated collective flight towards Florida as an armed political threat, not as a disagreement over compensation.

Ideology made this order speakable. Racial hierarchy represented Africans as naturally suited to servitude; religious arguments portrayed Christian instruction as compatible with bondage; colonial language described coercion as discipline and ownership as stewardship. The SPG’s plantations embodied the contradiction. An institution organised to spread Christianity received income from people branded with its name. The system did not require every beneficiary to think alike. It required shared categories that kept profit, piety, and punishment within one recognised world.

Planters also had economic reasons to limit certain forms of violence. A badly injured worker lost value, and indiscriminate killing could reduce the labour force. That calculation did not humanise the system. It made bodily preservation another aspect of ownership. The enslaved person might be kept alive because his labour, fertility, or resale price mattered. A limit placed on one punishment could protect the asset while preserving the relation that made the person an asset.

Economic motives alone also could not explain the intensity of ideological enforcement. Branding at Codrington, public displays after Stono, restrictions on movement, and the policing of literacy addressed obedience and racial authority as well as output. A worker who slowed production challenged profit, but a worker who spoke, gathered, read, fled, or carried a weapon challenged the social rank on which production depended. The plantation needed people to work and to understand that command belonged to others.

Atlantic slavery was sustained by a fit among several powers. Merchants supplied ships and credit. Colonial assemblies defined status. Courts recognised ownership. Militias pursued escape and rebellion. Religious bodies could justify instruction while receiving revenue. Planters organised labour through punishment and surveillance. Each office had a distinct function, and none alone explains the whole system. Their cooperation made a racial property regime durable across oceans.

The economic account remains indispensable because it identifies the rewards that drew states and investors into expansion. It becomes misleading when it turns law and ideology into reflections of profit, as though the same profits could have been secured through any available labour relation. The Atlantic colonies did not merely find workers and exploit them. They constructed a population that could be owned, marked, inherited, pursued, and disciplined, then defended that construction with moral language.

The history that began in the measured hold of the *Brookes* entered plantations, households, ports, courts, maroon settlements, militia campaigns, and churches. The records preserve prices and statutes, ship routes and punishments, the name of Jemmy and the destination of Florida. They also preserve the fact that enslaved people repeatedly forced changes in the conduct of owners and states. The Atlantic world was built through coercion, but coercion had to be organised anew wherever a person resisted the place assigned to him.
