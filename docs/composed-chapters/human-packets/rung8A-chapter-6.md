## Chapter 6: Frontiers, Faiths, and Political Fragmentation

Zengi entered the history of Edessa twice: as the man who took the city and as the ruler whose achievement others had to explain. On 24 December 1144, after a siege that had begun in the autumn, Edessa surrendered to Imad al-Din Zengi, atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo. The city stood exposed among the Muslim principalities of northern Syria and the Latin counties created after the First Crusade. Its walls enclosed Armenian, Syriac, and Frankish communities; its garrison served a Latin count whose power rested on castles, local agreements, and support from beyond the Euphrates. Zengi needed one fortified capital to show that the frontier had changed hands; destroying every settlement in the region was unnecessary.

Ibn al-Athir, writing in Mosul and looking back on events that had transformed regional politics, placed Zengi within a longer Muslim struggle against the crusader states. He presented the atabeg as a determined ruler who recognised Edessa’s weakness and acted while its defenders were isolated. Count Joscelin II was away in the countryside with much of the force that might have contested the siege. Zengi gathered troops from Mosul and Aleppo, brought siege equipment, and pressed the walls rather than waiting for a field battle in which Frankish cavalry might have been more effective. The choice was practical. A wall turned scattered local power into a target.

Edessa’s fall depended on the political geography outside the walls as much as on the soldiers before them. The county had been diminished by lost territory, rivalry among its rulers, and the difficulty of securing help from Jerusalem or Antioch in time. Zengi’s authority was hardly uniform across northern Syria and Iraq, but it allowed him to draw men and supplies from several centres and keep a campaign going beyond its first failed assault. Siege warfare was an exercise in administration. Grain had to be collected, animals fed, engines moved, and commanders held to a purpose while the besieged calculated the chance of relief. The men on the walls saw the army; Zengi’s power also ran along the roads behind it.

Ibn al-Athir described the capture as a triumph for Islam and a calamity for the Franks, while preserving the violence that followed. Edessa was taken by storm after a breach, and many inhabitants were killed. Others were enslaved or driven away. The Latin population, associated with the ruling power, suffered especially, while some Armenians and Syriac Christians were permitted to remain or later returned. These were decisions about usefulness, loyalty, ransom, and the future tax base. A conquered city could be punished as an enemy and preserved as a source of revenue. That made Zengi a frontier ruler in a more exact sense than the title suggests. He was not merely positioned between settled regions and hostile peoples. He governed a zone where authority had to be demonstrated repeatedly through movement, appointment, taxation, and war. Mosul supplied a base in Iraq; Aleppo gave him access to Syria; Edessa became both trophy and warning. His victory helped inspire the Second Crusade, whose preachers and princes treated the loss of Edessa as proof that the Latin states required renewed military support. Zengi altered the calculations of rulers far beyond the city he had taken.

The man who ordered attacks on a Christian county was also a patron. Zengi endowed religious buildings, employed administrators, and cultivated the public language of piety. His court needed jurists and secretaries as much as horsemen. A ruler of Mosul governed through the yearly collection of revenues from cities, markets, irrigation works, and land grants, with raids forming only one instrument of rule. His authority appeared in mosques and appointments, in gifts to allies and salaries to officials, and in the protection offered to some subjects after others had been dispossessed. This combination makes Edessa more than a tale of religious hatred. Zengi’s public image depended on holy-war language, and the capture of a crusader city could be presented as sacred restoration. The campaign also followed dynastic logic. Aleppo had to be held against rivals; Mosul had to be connected to western possessions; the Frankish frontier offered an enemy against whom military success could become legitimacy. Faith gave the conquest a moral shape. The court, the army, and the tax collector gave it duration.

Zengi did not establish a stable order in every place he conquered. He was assassinated in 1146, less than two years after Edessa fell, by a servant during the siege of Qal'at Ja'bar. His sons divided his territories, with Nur al-Din taking Aleppo and Saif al-Din Ghazi taking Mosul. The division showed how much rested on the person of the ruler and how incomplete the union of his lands remained. Edessa nevertheless survived as a political fact. Nur al-Din inherited the claim of resistance, and later Saladin built upon a vocabulary in which the recovery of Muslim lands and the construction of a disciplined sultanate belonged together.

Ibn al-Athir wrote about men who prayed, marched, negotiated, and killed, but his history gives force to the offices connecting those acts. The atabeg was not a wandering champion enlarged by legend. He was a governor with a treasury, a commander with siege trains, a patron with a court, and a conqueror who had to decide which inhabitants could be incorporated after the gates opened. Edessa became famous because one frontier ruler made its vulnerability serve a larger political project.

Jerusalem raises the question Edessa leaves behind: when a city becomes the object of sacred war, does religious authority restrain the army by placing rules around violence, or enlarge the number of people whom the army may treat as enemies?

The siege of Jerusalem in July 1099 offers the starkest answer from the crusading movement. After the city’s capture, Latin and eastern Christian accounts describe widespread killing among Muslims and Jews. The crusaders had crossed Anatolia, Syria, and Palestine through hunger, disease, ambush, and quarrels over command. Vows, relics, sermons, and oaths gave the march a religious purpose, while a walled city supplied its political prize. The assault required ladders, towers, engineers, and ships carrying timber along a coast contested by Fatimid forces. Zeal did not replace logistics; it directed them toward a city whose symbolic value made surrender less acceptable and massacre more readily celebrated.

Across the Sahara, Mali’s rulers faced a different problem. Gold moved from the western Sudan towards North African markets, but gold did not secure a road. Wells, guides, caravan leaders, provincial officials, and armed escorts had to make passage predictable. Al-Umari’s description of Mali, compiled in Cairo in the fourteenth century from reports by travellers and merchants, emphasises the authority of the mansa, the wealth associated with gold, and the court’s ability to receive and redistribute goods. His account presents a political order strengthened by the ruler’s power to protect routes and adjudicate disputes. A ruler guarding a caravan did not need to sanctify every act of coercion as a war for the faith.

The comparison is not between a violent Europe and a peaceful Africa. Mali fought, expanded, punished, and extracted. Jerusalem’s conquerors could negotiate, ransom, and govern. Their violence nevertheless travelled through different institutions. In 1099, a papal summons joined armed pilgrimage to territorial seizure, and the city’s population could be reclassified as an obstacle to sacred possession. In Mali, al-Umari’s ruler rested more visibly on control of movement, tribute, and exchange across a large region. His success lay in making merchants return.

Would a different religious vocabulary alone have changed Jerusalem’s fate? The evidence cannot answer that. The city was attacked by leaders with competing claims and soldiers expecting reward, after a campaign had created a harsh moral division between besiegers and those inside the walls. Mali’s gold routes show another possibility: political power could gain prestige by reducing uncertainty for commerce while retaining the means to compel obedience. The unresolved question lies at the moment when sacred identity turns a population into a permitted target, and at the moment when continued exchange makes protection more profitable than destruction.

A caravan moved safely because authorities learned how to charge for danger without allowing danger to consume the trade; an empty road offered no such guarantee.

On the Silk Road, tolls were collected at gates, bridges, market towns, and passes. A merchant travelling from Central Asia towards Iran or China might pay more than once to officials controlling separate stretches of the route. The payment could be called a tax, fee, customs charge, or contribution to protection. Its name mattered less than the bargain at the checkpoint: armed men who took payment were expected to keep the road open, and a ruler who failed risked losing merchants to another route. A toll became durable when a fort, a magistrate, and a market stood behind it.

Anatolian caravanserais made that bargain concrete. Under the Seljuks of Rum, especially during the thirteenth century, fortified inns were built along routes connecting Konya, Kayseri, Sivas, and the Mediterranean ports. Their thick walls and guarded gates allowed caravans to stop within a protected enclosure. Wells, stables, prayer spaces, and storage rooms kept travellers and goods together under supervision. The buildings were not merely shelters. They concentrated merchants where officials could inspect cargo, settle claims, and provide security. Passage through a political landscape became a series of controlled entries.

Protection could break down. A frontier fortress might be poorly supplied, a governor might divert the garrison’s pay, or a local chief might find plunder more profitable than customs revenue from living merchants. When a town was fortified, it became both shield and prize. Its walls protected a market and advertised a ruler’s reach; they also gave raiders a reason to burn surrounding villages, seize livestock, and wait for the garrison to emerge.

Seasonal raiding in the Maghreb followed pasture, harvest, and political opportunity. Tribal groups could move against herds, fields, or caravans when local resources were thin or a neighbouring ruler appeared unable to retaliate. A captured flock could sustain a camp, while a successful raid could finance weapons and attract followers. Maghrebi rulers alternately fought such groups, recruited them, rewarded them, and incorporated their leaders into provincial politics. The horsemen threatening a route might later escort it under another agreement.

The sequence ran in both directions. A ruler built a fortress to protect a road; the fortress required taxes; taxes required merchants to keep travelling; merchants required protection from the people whose military service the ruler might hire. If protection failed, raiding increased, revenue fell, and the garrison weakened. If it succeeded, tolls supported the walls that made further protection possible. Tribute joined the chain by turning conquered communities into regular suppliers of grain or cash. A raid took wealth once. Tribute tried to make the taking repeatable.

Fortress networks on Byzantine, Islamic, and Central Asian frontiers worked through similar tensions even when their architecture and political languages differed. A fort sorted border violence into permitted forms: patrols, reprisals, escorts, tax collection, hostage-taking, and authorised campaigns, without bringing that violence to an end. The frontier was not a line but a field of negotiations in which the difference between a merchant, a tributary, a rebel, and an enemy could change at the next gate.

Baghdad stood where these arrangements became imperial in scale and catastrophic in failure. In January 1258, Hulagu Khan’s army approached the Abbasid capital after moving west through Iran and Iraq. The Mongol commander had been sent by his brother Möngke, the Great Khan, to extend Mongol power over the remaining independent centres of the Islamic Middle East. Alamut had fallen in 1256, and the Ismaili state in northern Iran had been dismantled. Hulagu’s next objective was Baghdad, where the Abbasid caliph al-Musta'sim still possessed immense religious prestige, though his military power had contracted far behind the city’s reputation.

The approach was not a sudden eruption from an empty steppe. Hulagu’s army travelled through territories that had been surveyed, compelled, and supplied. Mongol commanders demanded submission from cities and rulers. Engineers, Chinese specialists, Persian administrators, and subject troops accompanied the cavalry. The caliph’s court had received warnings, but it misjudged the danger, trusting that Baghdad’s walls, the caliph’s title, and divisions among his enemies would make a direct assault too costly.

Ata-Malik Juvayni, a Persian administrator who served the Mongol state and later governed Baghdad, wrote his history within the political world created by the conquerors. His account of the city’s fall is therefore courtly history, but it is also the testimony of a man who knew Baghdad’s institutions and the conquerors’ methods. He described Hulagu’s advance, the caliph’s failure to organise effective resistance, and the destruction that followed. His narrative gave the campaign a moral and cosmic scale while preserving the administrative fact that the Mongols had defeated a government unable to command the resources its capital contained.

The first pressure fell on the approaches. Mongol forces crossed the Tigris by bridges and boats, while other units moved around Baghdad to prevent escape or outside relief. The caliph’s army went out and was defeated. Medieval accounts differ over details and numbers, but the strategic result is clear: the defenders lost the initiative before the walls could protect them. Water, roads, and open ground became Mongol weapons. Baghdad was not simply encircled by men standing in a ring; it was separated from the countryside that fed it and from allies who might have forced Hulagu to divide his forces.

Inside the capital, the caliph’s authority narrowed with every day of siege. The Abbasid dynasty had ruled from Baghdad since 750, surviving changes in military leadership, provincial autonomy, and rival caliphates. By the thirteenth century, the caliph could still issue commands, receive envoys, and confer legitimacy, but the resources behind those acts had shrunk. The walls remained visible evidence of power. The army needed to defend them was less certain. An ancient office could survive after its orders stopped travelling.

Negotiations followed. Al-Musta'sim sent representatives, and Hulagu demanded submission. Courtly accounts placed responsibility for the crisis on the caliph’s advisers, particularly the vizier Ibn al-Alqami, whom later Sunni writers accused of treachery and collaboration. Juvayni repeated hostile traditions about the court, but the political explanation requires more than a villain. The caliphate had been weakened by faction, restricted revenue, and the autonomy of military commanders. Blaming one minister made a complex failure legible. It also allowed later writers to say that Baghdad fell through betrayal rather than because the Abbasid order could no longer defend its capital.

The Mongols breached or entered the city in early February. On 10 February 1258, al-Musta'sim surrendered. The date appears in chronicles amid calculations of the Islamic calendar and the sequence of Mongol demands. The caliph was taken to Hulagu’s camp. Later stories say that he was killed by being wrapped in a carpet and trampled, because Mongol custom supposedly avoided spilling a ruler’s blood. Juvayni’s account does not need that image to establish the political fact. The Abbasid caliph had ceased to be an independent sovereign. The ceremony of submission transferred command from the descendant of the Prophet’s uncle to a Mongol prince acting for a wider imperial house.

Then the killing began.

The sources describe streets filled with corpses, houses entered and stripped, mosques and palaces damaged, and large numbers of inhabitants put to death. Medieval chroniclers often give enormous figures, including claims that hundreds of thousands or more than a million people died. Such totals cannot be accepted as precise counts. Baghdad was a very large city, perhaps with several hundred thousand inhabitants depending on how its suburbs and dependent settlements are counted, and siege, massacre, flight, famine, and disease make an exact total unreachable. The scale was nevertheless severe. One of the central capitals of the Islamic world was subjected to organised plunder and killing over days and perhaps weeks.

Juvayni turned destruction into an image of historical reversal. He wrote that the city became a place where the living could scarcely move among the dead and described libraries, palaces, markets, and houses exposed to fire and ruin. His account of the Tigris darkened by ink from books became one of the most repeated stories of the conquest. The image may be literary rather than an eyewitness measurement, but it expresses what an administrative record would miss. Baghdad’s libraries held the memory of a scholarly capital, where knowledge, courtly patronage, and caliphal presence occupied the same urban space.

The conquerors did not destroy everything. Christian communities and some officials received protection. The Mongols needed administrators, craftsmen, tax collectors, physicians, and people who understood the city’s canals and markets. Religious buildings could be spared when a commander or influential patron claimed them. Al-Musta'sim’s family and court servants were treated according to rank and political usefulness, though many others faced killing or enslavement. The difference between a protected person and a dead one could depend on confession, occupation, household connection, or intervention by a Mongol officer.

Juvayni himself was later appointed governor of Baghdad under the Ilkhanate. His writing about the catastrophe thus stood close to the machinery that followed it. He knew that the Mongols could not govern a ruined city without restoring some of its revenue. Irrigation channels needed clearing. Markets required merchants. Workshops needed labourers. Officials had to assess surviving property and gather taxes. The same imperial government that had ordered exemplary destruction now had to turn violence into an income-producing province. The conquerors used terror to secure surrender elsewhere, then moderated destruction where continued extraction required a living population.

The siege tested submission across Iraq as much as it assaulted one capital. If Baghdad resisted and survived, other rulers might calculate that the Mongol army could be checked. If Baghdad fell with spectacular force, the example could travel along the roads faster than the army. Hulagu’s campaign therefore combined negotiation, demands, military encirclement, and punitive sack. Cities that surrendered on terms could preserve more inhabitants and property. Those that resisted risked annihilation or something close to it. Mercy and massacre were both instruments of a political language.

Baghdad was not treated as an ordinary fortress. Its capture removed a rival source of universal religious legitimacy. The Abbasid caliph had no army capable of matching Hulagu, but his office could authorise resistance and give enemies a centre around which to rally. By ending the independent caliphate in Baghdad, the Mongols declared who could grant authority in the region. The killing of inhabitants and seizure of the palace joined the removal of an institution whose symbolic reach exceeded its military means.

A different account might focus on the Mongols as nomadic conquerors destroying sedentary civilisation. That preserves part of the contrast but misses the campaign’s working parts. Hulagu’s army depended on settled supplies, siege engineers, local intelligence, and Persian officials. The Mongol imperial system was mobile at its core, yet it carried procedures for taxation, hostage-taking, census, and communication across conquered lands. Baghdad fell when a highly organised imperial army confronted a divided government and converted victory into a new provincial administration.

The city’s defenders had institutions, but they did not align. The caliph’s household, military commanders, urban notables, provincial rulers, and religious scholars did not form one effective command. Some feared the caliph’s ministers more than they feared the Mongols. Others waited to see whether surrender would preserve property. The city’s size became a burden when authority was uncertain: many neighbourhoods and armed groups, but no trusted plan for coordination. A wall protects those who accept the same commander. It becomes a trap when each quarter waits for another to act.

The chronicles preserve several kinds of grief. Juvayni wrote as a Persian Muslim official who witnessed the collapse of the Abbasid centre and served the conquerors afterwards. Other Muslim writers in Syria, Egypt, or later Ilkhanid territory remembered Baghdad as a wound in Islamic history. Christian sources sometimes emphasised the Mongols as possible allies against Muslim powers, especially before the sack, and later noted the protection offered to some Christian communities. These positions offered differing accounts of the dead while recognising that the conquest had rearranged the region’s political map.

News travelled with survivors. Merchants, refugees, scholars, soldiers, and envoys carried accounts west towards Aleppo, Cairo, and Damascus and east towards Iran. Baghdad’s fall influenced the calculations of the Ayyubids and Mamluks, whose rulers now faced an empire that had destroyed the caliphate and occupied Iraq. In 1260, Mongol forces advanced into Syria and captured Aleppo and Damascus before the Mamluks defeated them at Ayn Jalut. The later Mamluk court in Cairo sheltered an Abbasid claimant, using the restored caliphal title to strengthen its own legitimacy. A dead institution could be revived as a political instrument, but not in the city where its authority had been rooted for five centuries.

Conquest also changed the Mongols’ own position. Prestige brought obligations that steppe mobility alone could not meet. The Ilkhanate required Persian bureaucrats, Muslim tax officials, Christian and Buddhist patrons, and urban revenue. Hulagu’s successors adopted parts of the administrative culture they had overrun. Ghazan Khan’s conversion to Islam in 1295 and his reforms under Rashid al-Din did not erase the violence of the conquest. They showed how a conquering house could move from destroying a caliphate to ruling through Islamic institutions and Persian fiscal practice. That later accommodation should not be projected backwards onto February 1258. The decision to permit some people to live and kill others belonged to the immediate needs of conquest, rank, religion, and command, while the city’s eventual usefulness did not determine whether it was spared. Nor should the massacre be explained as an eruption of undirected rage. Orders had to be transmitted. Units had to enter districts. Property had to be marked, gathered, and divided. Prisoners had to be assigned. Officials had to identify what could be restored. Violence on Baghdad’s scale required a victorious government before it required an angry crowd.

Juvayni’s account carries a troubling proximity. He could mourn the city’s destruction while legitimising the rule that destroyed it. His history presented the Mongol conquest as part of a providential sequence in which tyrants fell and new rulers emerged. Such writing was composed within a courtly world where survival depended on serving new masters and giving their victory a place in history. The need to justify conquest did not eliminate the evidence of bodies, empty houses, ruined institutions, and broken families. The record was shaped by power and still bore the weight of what power did.

The fate of Baghdad differed from Edessa in scale and in the conqueror’s relationship to the city. Zengi needed Edessa as a frontier stronghold and proof that a Muslim ruler could challenge the crusader states. Hulagu needed Baghdad as an imperial demonstration and later as a taxable centre. Both rulers used religious language. Both distinguished among inhabitants after victory. Neither could govern by destruction alone. The extent of killing changed with the political message required and with the institutions available to rebuild what had been damaged.

Baghdad’s archives were especially vulnerable because scholarship had been woven into the city’s political identity. Libraries, schools, mosques, courts, and palace offices connected memory to government. When the city was sacked, the destruction of books and scholars was experienced not merely as cultural loss but as the collapse of a public order. The famous image of ink in the Tigris endures because it gives material form to a historical break. Behind it stood more ordinary losses: a family unable to return to its house, a scribe without a patron, a shopkeeper whose accounts had been taken, a farmer whose canal no longer received the labour that kept it open.

No single number can carry that history. The date of surrender, the names of Hulagu, al-Musta'sim, and Juvayni, the routes from Iran, the walls around Baghdad, and Juvayni’s later appointment as governor bind it more securely. A city’s destruction is measurable in deaths, but also in the removal of offices that made ordinary life predictable. When those offices were rebuilt, they served a new sovereign.

The religious authorities of medieval Eurasia did not all respond to violence in the same way. Some sanctified conquest, some negotiated protection, and some tried to place armed men under rules reaching beyond the battlefield. The difference appears in three practices: the Peace and Truce of God in Latin Christendom, the Islamic aman, and Buddhist monastic protections over land and dependants.

At the Council of Charroux in Aquitaine in 989, bishops issued canons against attacks on churches, church property, and vulnerable people. The decrees belonged to a world where local lords, castellans, and armed retainers could seize livestock, burn villages, and fight over inheritance with little effective royal control. Religious sanctions gave bishops a way to identify forbidden targets and threaten excommunication. Later Peace and Truce of God movements extended restrictions to particular days, including Sundays and major feast periods. The rules tried to reduce private warfare by making the church a judge of conduct. Their protection was selective. The canons of Charroux defended churches, clerics, peasants, and goods placed under ecclesiastical protection, but the social order defining those categories remained intact. A knight could be condemned for attacking a church and blessed when fighting in a recognised campaign. The same institution that limited a lord’s raid could support a crusade against an external enemy. Constraint required a jurisdiction able to name violators; sanctification required a boundary around those whom violence could rightly reach.

The Islamic aman worked through a different legal form. A commander, ruler, or authorised individual could grant safe-conduct to a person or community, allowing entry into Muslim territory or continued residence under protection. The agreement might cover merchants, envoys, captives, or a defeated town. Its force rested on the obligation to honour a pledge once given, even when the protected person belonged to an enemy population. Protection was portable and contractual. It could be written, spoken, or conveyed by an authorised messenger, supporting diplomacy and commerce as well as mercy.

Buddhist monastic land protections operated through property and jurisdiction. In parts of China, Korea, Japan, and mainland Southeast Asia, monasteries received estates, tax exemptions, labour services, or immunity from certain officials. Charters and donor inscriptions placed fields, tenants, and religious buildings under sacred protection. The arrangement could shield dependants from military requisition or local seizure while allowing monasteries and rulers to accumulate wealth and command labour. A monastery might restrain one form of predation by becoming a recognised owner within another hierarchy of extraction.

The three practices do not form a scale from violent to peaceful religion. At Charroux, bishops tried to restrain castellans by defining protected persons and sacred time. Under aman, a ruler’s promise could interrupt vengeance and preserve movement across a frontier. In Buddhist estates, exemption could shelter land from soldiers while transferring obligations to a monastic lord. Religious authority constrained violence when it attached a binding rule to a named person, place, day, or property. It sanctified violence when it transformed an enemy category into a legitimate object of conquest. At Charroux, in the aman pledge, and on the protected estate, institutions decided who could be harmed.
