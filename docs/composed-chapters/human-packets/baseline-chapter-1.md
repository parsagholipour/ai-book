## Chapter 1: What Do We Mean by Human Aggression?

In 2011, archaeologists excavating at Schöneck-Kilianstädten in central Germany uncovered a mass grave containing the remains of people who had died violently. The grave was not a battlefield spread across a field, nor a single burial into which one body had later been placed beside another. Human remains had been deposited together in one concentrated place. The skulls carried injuries that drew the excavation towards a question more exacting than whether prehistoric people were violent. What kind of event could have produced these wounds, and what could the bones establish about it?

The archaeologists examined the injuries as marks left by particular encounters between bodies and hard objects. A skull fracture was not anger itself. It was the material consequence of an impact, preserved after the people who delivered and received it had disappeared. The placement of the bodies, the condition of the bones, and the pattern of damage allowed the excavators to reconstruct an episode of lethal violence. The reconstruction depended on the relation among several traces, since no surviving account described what happened. A stone tool, a blow, a fallen person, another blow: the sequence had to be inferred from what the ground retained.

The grave establishes collective death, but not the habits or intentions of every person connected with it. Its injuries do not show that the whole community settled disputes by force, or that the event was a war in the later sense of the word. The bones establish harm. The archaeologists’ reconstruction gives that harm a likely setting and sequence. At Schöneck-Kilianstädten, the interpretation remains tied to the injuries, the bodies, and their arrangement. That distinction is small in grammar and large in history. To say that one person struck another is to describe an act. To say that a community practised warfare is to describe a relation among groups, an expectation of conflict, and a means of organising people and supplies for repeated encounters. To say that a government carried out an atrocity is to identify a wider process in which officials, classifications, transport, confinement, weapons, and orders bring violence to a population. The skull does not make these distinctions for us. We have to make them before comparing it with a homicide register, a casualty roll, or a decree.

Human aggression may be defined as behaviour intended to cause harm to another person who wishes to avoid it. The harm may be physical, verbal, or psychological. Such breadth is useful because a threat and a blow can both seek submission, but it makes comparison hazardous. Anger is an emotional state, ranging from irritation to rage, and a person may feel it without harming anyone. Aggression is an outward orientation or act. Violence is narrower, involving severe physical harm. A shouted threat may be aggressive without being violent; a violent assault is aggressive even when its immediate motive is fear, profit, discipline, revenge, or obedience rather than rage.

A further distinction separates affective from instrumental aggression. Affective aggression is reactive: an insult, perceived injustice, or sudden threat appears to provoke an emotional response. Instrumental aggression turns harm into a means of securing a specific end, such as property or control. The two forms are not sealed compartments. A raider may feel fury while pursuing plunder. A soldier may fear an enemy while following a plan. A prison guard may use personal anger inside a system whose purpose is administrative control.

The Schöneck-Kilianstädten grave cannot tell us whether the attackers acted in anger or calculation. It places injuries within an archaeological reconstruction of collective death. That is already substantial. The evidence directs attention away from an imagined human essence and towards the conditions that made the blows possible: hard objects that could serve as weapons, a gathering of people, the treatment of bodies, and the decision to leave or place the dead together. The injuries belong to an event, not to an abstract population.

One person may injure another within a household, a friendship, a workplace, or an intimate relationship. Interpersonal violence occurs in relationships where one person seeks to gain or maintain power over another. Its immediate scene is often close and unequal. The aggressor may seek control over a particular person rather than advance the aims of a group. A household can contain affection, dependence, fear, and coercion at once. No army is required, and no public battle is present, yet harm can be sustained through access to money, shelter, children, or reputation.

Raiding has a different shape. It is a rapid military expedition directed towards a defined purpose, after which the raiders withdraw rather than occupy and administer the place they have struck. A raid may aim at plunder or at the capture of a particular person. Its speed does not make it merely spontaneous. Even a small party depends on recognised loyalties, knowledge of the terrain, expectations about reward, and a community capable of supporting or tolerating the expedition.

Warfare extends that organisation. It is the systematic waging of armed conflict by states or organised armed groups, using military operations to pursue political, economic, or territorial objectives. Battles are one part of warfare. Recruitment, taxation, transport, command, intelligence, supply, and the treatment of prisoners sustain the fighting. The battlefield concentrates violence, but the institution that brings people there may distribute coercion across villages, ports, workshops, courts, and households. The person who dies from a spear thrust and the official who requisitions grain are connected by a chain of decisions, even when the two never meet.

Law adds another register. Coercive law compels conduct through threats of punishment, restrictions on liberty, seizure of property, or danger to a person’s livelihood. Such pressure need not involve a physical attack at the moment it is applied. A government can prohibit speech, define disloyalty as treason, or make movement conditional on permission. The coercive force lies in the credible power behind the rule. A statute may look orderly in its language while depending on imprisonment, confiscation, or bodily punishment for obedience. This is why the early history of the Nazi regime cannot be understood only through the question of how much violence ordinary Germans felt in daily life. The regime created spaces in which many people encountered routine administration and others in which Jews, political opponents, and additional targeted groups faced escalating persecution. Civil liberties were restricted, treason was redefined, and loyalty was made enforceable through law. The same state that offered many citizens an appearance of public order subjected selected populations to terror, deportation, enslavement, and murder. Warfare and organised atrocity later widened the reach of this violence, but that widening followed earlier classifications and measures.

Organised atrocity is more than a large number of assaults. It is systematic violence against civilian populations, including genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and ethnic cleansing. Such violence has precursors. Dangerous speech and dehumanising descriptions identify a group as evil, inferior, or threatening. Discrimination and persecution establish unequal treatment. Armed conflict or political instability can supply the emergency in which exceptional measures become acceptable. The killing may be sudden, but the categories that make victims killable are usually prepared in advance.

At Schöneck-Kilianstädten, archaeologists worked backwards from wounds and the arrangement of a grave. In the twentieth century, officials worked forwards from categories, decrees, lists, and orders towards the destruction of people. The scale differs, the evidence differs, and the moral vocabulary cannot be flattened into one label. Both cases require attention to the setting in which a harmful act became possible. A weapon does not explain its own use. A wound does not identify the social purpose of the blow. At some stage, a person or an office has decided whose body may be attacked, abandoned, confined, or killed.

Thomas Hobbes began from a different kind of evidence. In *Leviathan*, published in 1651, he did not excavate a prehistoric grave or count wounds in a cemetery. He asked what human relations would be like in the absence of a common power able to keep people in awe. His state of nature is a thought experiment about people without a sovereign authority, not a report from a particular archaeological site. It remains useful here because it places fear, equality, anticipation, and actual fighting within a single argument.

Hobbes names three principal causes of quarrel: competition, diffidence, and glory. Competition drives people to invade for gain. Diffidence, or distrust, drives them to seek safety. Glory drives them to defend reputation, sometimes over a word, a gesture, or a perceived slight. Without a common power, no common rule can reliably settle disputes. Hobbes calls the result a state of war, and he carefully adds that war consists not only in actual fighting but in the known disposition towards it during the time in which there is no assurance to the contrary. His distinction places the argument beside Schöneck-Kilianstädten in an illuminating but uneven comparison. The grave contains actual injuries and deaths. Hobbes’s state of nature includes the expectation of violence, the fear that another person may attack, and the calculation that striking first may be safer than waiting. His “warre” is not simply a succession of blows. It is a condition in which the absence of assurance makes peaceful action precarious. People may spend much of the time without fighting and still live under the shadow of war.

The difference begins with the evidence. The grave records bodies that were injured and buried together. Hobbes records an argument about the insecurity produced when no authority can guarantee restraint. One source starts with an event and moves towards its setting; the other starts with a political condition and predicts the conduct it encourages. Neither supplies what the other lacks. The bones cannot reveal an expectation of attack, while Hobbes cannot establish that the people at Schöneck-Kilianstädten behaved according to his sequence of anticipation.

Fear does not by itself produce equal outcomes. Hobbes begins from a rough equality of ability: even the weakest may kill the strongest, whether by secret plotting or alliance with others. From this equality comes equality of hope in attaining ends, and from equality of hope comes conflict when two people desire the same thing. The argument is not that every person has equal strength or equal resources. It is that no natural difference guarantees safety. The powerful cannot assume that the weak are harmless, and the weak cannot assume that the powerful will refrain from using force.

In the grave at Schöneck-Kilianstädten, fear can be imagined as part of the circumstances surrounding the dead, but the archaeological reconstruction does not establish Hobbes’s sequence of anticipation. It does not show whether the attackers believed themselves threatened, whether the victims belonged to a neighbouring group, or whether the killing followed a dispute over land, food, status, or captives. Hobbes supplies a model of insecure relations; the grave supplies evidence of one lethal event. The model cannot substitute for the event’s context. Nor does Hobbes’s state of nature amount to a history of all societies before states. He writes as a political philosopher concerned with the grounds of government. His condition without a common power is an analytic construction, designed to show why people might authorise a sovereign rather than reconstruct the daily life of a prehistoric community. A society without a central state may possess councils, kin obligations, ritual sanctions, age grades, reputations, or rules for compensation. The absence of a Leviathan does not mean the absence of every restraint.

A society without a state in the European sense may still organise violence through kinship and obligation. A raiding party can move through a landscape whose members are bound by promises of shares, initiation, or the authority of elders. The target may be selected through a remembered feud or a political calculation. The attack may be violent, but it is not necessarily a war of all against all. It has a purpose, a group, and a boundary between those who take part and those who do not.

Hobbes nonetheless sees something that a narrow definition of violence can miss. The prospect of harm changes conduct before harm occurs. A village that builds a wall or a household that hides its grain is responding to expected violence even when no assault takes place. The fear may be induced by an actual enemy, a rumour, a law, or a ruler’s threat. Its historical importance lies in the choices it makes seem necessary. People surrender freedom for protection; officials demand resources for defence; neighbours become suspects; weapons move from emergency tools into permanent offices.

The sovereign in *Leviathan* is meant to end the uncertainty of private judgement by possessing enough power to enforce peace. Yet the same concentration of power can turn coercion outwards or inwards. A state can reduce private homicide while waging war against another state. It can suppress feuds while imposing forced labour. It can make roads safer for merchants while using courts and soldiers to dispossess a minority. Hobbes’s question about the need for a common power leads beyond the contrast between anarchy and order. We have to ask who holds the power, whom it protects, and against whom it is used.

Measured only by the existence of fighting, the distinction between a quarrel, a raid, a war, and an atrocity collapses. Measured by the institutions that select targets and sustain action, the differences become visible. A quarrel may end when anger cools or a settlement is reached. A raid may depend on a small armed group and a limited objective. Warfare requires continuing command and supply. Organised atrocity transforms legal categories, administrative procedures, and public language into instruments for attacking civilians. Fear occurs in all four settings, but it does not determine their scale or meaning.

Hobbes feared the condition in which no one could trust another person’s restraint. History presents an additional danger: a person may trust the restraint of a government that has decided he is outside its protection. The state can make violence predictable for some and compulsory for others. An account of aggression cannot stop with the passions of individuals. Fear may move a hand, but an office can direct thousands of hands towards a defined population.

A death count looks more definite than a theory of fear. Modern homicide registers appear to offer exactly what historical comparison needs: a number of unlawful killings within a population, a date, and a rate calculated against the number of inhabitants. Such registers are powerful because they turn scattered acts into a public series. They can reveal that estimated murder rates in England fell from about 24 per 100,000 in the fourteenth century to 0.6 per 100,000 by the early 1960s. The figures suggest a profound change in lethal interpersonal violence and invite an explanation involving courts, policing, urban government, weapons, social discipline, and altered expectations about retaliation.

The wording of the category does much of the work. A homicide register is not a list of every death caused by another human being. It is produced through a legal and administrative process that identifies some deaths as criminal homicides. A body may be discovered, a coroner may investigate, a constable may report, a prosecutor may classify, and a court may revise the account. Each stage can alter the number. Murder, manslaughter, justifiable killing, death in custody, and death in war do not necessarily enter the same column.

The office that counts is already making a decision about what kind of death it has found. A register might record a killing as murder because a court has established intent, while another death enters a different category because an official judged it justified. The number does not float above these decisions. Its apparent precision comes from the rule that permits one death to be counted and another to be placed elsewhere.

Battlefield casualty rolls use another arrangement. They may count killed, wounded, missing, captured, or dead from disease. They may distinguish officers from enlisted men, one regiment from another, or one engagement from an entire campaign. A battlefield death is not automatically a homicide in the category used by a civilian register, even though one person or group caused it. The roll belongs to an army that has a reason to know the number, a way of recording it, and an interest in describing the loss according to military needs.

The contrast between an English homicide rate and a count of global battle deaths illustrates the danger. One supplied estimate places global deaths in interstate wars at more than 65,000 per year in the 1950s and fewer than 2,000 per year in the 2000s. The figures point to a decline in one form of organised killing, but they do not measure all violence. Civil wars and deaths from displacement may be excluded or handled differently. A lower battlefield count can coexist with severe violence elsewhere, just as a lower homicide rate can coexist with coercive imprisonment or destructive state policy.

Numbers do not become unhistorical because categories shape them. The category is part of the evidence. When a government creates a homicide register, it declares that deaths within its jurisdiction should be made legible to an office. When a military prepares a casualty roll, it turns a lost body into a unit in the accounting of a campaign. The count reveals both an event and a public practice. It tells us how many deaths officials recognised under a rule, and it may show that the rule, the office, or the reach of the state changed.

The fall in recorded homicide in England is more than a story about people becoming less angry. It may reflect the declining acceptability of private vengeance, the expansion of courts and policing, changes in local governance, and a growing ability to intervene before a quarrel becomes lethal. Those developments can reduce interpersonal killing while strengthening the state’s monopoly over legitimate force. A man who once carried a weapon to settle an insult may face arrest, trial, and imprisonment. Violence has not disappeared; a different institution claims the right to use it and sets narrower conditions for its private use.

Battlefield numbers reveal a related transformation from the opposite direction. A state can gather men and weapons for war on a scale no household or feud can match. It can classify a death as a military loss, distribute the news through official channels, and compensate a family according to rank or service. The casualty roll gives the dead a place within an organisation, but it may leave civilian suffering outside the frame. The document is both a count and a boundary. This boundary complicates comparisons across time. A medieval chronicler may describe a battle’s dead in rounded language. A modern ministry may separate killed from missing and later amend the total. An archaeological assemblage may contain fractures and bodies without names. A homicide register may capture a death only after a legal decision. These sources do not form one ladder from ignorance to perfect knowledge. They are different instruments, each made for a purpose and each carrying that purpose into the number.

A count can still expose large change. The estimated English rate of 24 murders per 100,000 in the fourteenth century and 0.6 per 100,000 by the early 1960s are not meaningless because medieval and modern officials used different procedures. The scale of the difference demands attention. Its interpretation should remain tied to the offices that produced the figures and the practices that shaped the underlying violence. A decline may involve altered weapons, stronger courts, changing settlement patterns, improved dispute mediation, and a public less willing to accept lethal self-help. The number opens the question; it does not answer it alone. The same discipline applies to the Schöneck-Kilianstädten grave. Several skull injuries can establish a violent death event, but they do not generate a rate. Without a known population, a defined period, and a comparable sample, the grave cannot be converted into a statement about the prevalence of aggression. Its force lies elsewhere. It shows that the capacity for collective lethal violence existed among people whose society left no written explanation. It does not tell us how often such violence occurred or whether the group surrounding the grave lived under permanent fear.

The missing explanation becomes especially important for societies without written records. Archaeology can identify trauma, fortifications, burned settlements, mass burials, or weapon deposits. These traces may support an inference of violence, and patterns across sites may suggest raiding, conflict, displacement, or organised killing. Yet the inference moves from material effect to social meaning. A broken skull may have been caused by an attack, an execution, an accident, or a later disturbance, depending on its form and setting. The grave at Schöneck-Kilianstädten narrows the possibilities, but it does not give the dead their own account.

What violence disappears when no one writes it down? A household assault may leave no durable mark. A threat may alter a person’s life without injuring bone. A group may be expelled, impoverished, or compelled to labour while the settlement itself remains archaeologically intact. Written societies also lose such experiences, but their laws, registers, letters, and petitions sometimes preserve an echo. For unwritten societies, absence can mean that no harm occurred, that harm left no material trace, or that later observers have not yet learned how to recognise the trace.

The question is not whether archaeology can tell us anything. It can show injuries, deaths, destruction, and sometimes the organised conditions around them. The harder question is how far those signs permit us to move from violence that happened to violence as a social pattern. The answer will depend on repeated sites, secure dates, careful comparisons, and restraint before silence is turned into either peace or brutality. At Schöneck-Kilianstädten, the dead establish that people were killed. The history still has to determine who could be harmed, who organised the harm, and what kind of world made the grave possible.
