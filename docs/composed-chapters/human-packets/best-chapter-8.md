## Chapter 8: States, Slavery, and the Atlantic World

On 29 November 1781, Captain Luke Collingwood stood on the deck of the *Zong* while the crew brought enslaved people up from the hold.

The ship had left the African coast in September with more than four hundred captives aboard. She was bound for Jamaica, where the people in the hold were to be sold, but Collingwood had misjudged the distance. On 27 November, land was sighted and taken for Hispaniola. Two days later the error was discovered. The island was not Jamaica, and the ship lay hundreds of miles from the port for which it had been making. The water casks were nearly empty. Below deck, many of the enslaved people were sick with fever, dysentery, and scurvy. Some had already died.

Salt, bilge water, and sickness hung over the deck. The men opened the hatch and called down for more people. Those who could stand were brought up in groups and made to wait near the rail. During the crossing, their wrists and ankles had been fastened together; the irons had rubbed the skin raw. Others could not walk. They were carried upward while the crew worked around them.

Collingwood had been ill himself. In the account later given on his behalf, he said that he believed the water would not last until Jamaica and that the sick and dying captives were consuming what remained. The ship’s stores had been calculated against a voyage, and the captain now treated the people below as part of that calculation. The barrels, pumps, sails, provisions, and chains belonged to the same order of measurement as the human cargo. Water was being assigned against bodies, and bodies were being selected when the arithmetic no longer satisfied the voyage.

The first fifty-four were taken to the side.

No logbook survives to give their names, and no account preserves what they said. The crew pushed them over the rail. Some fell in pairs, still held together by their shackles. Others struck the water separately. They disappeared among coils of rope and scraps floating beside the ship.

The *Zong* sailed on.

On 30 November, another forty-two enslaved people were brought up. The officers later said that the killings were necessary because the ship’s water was running short. The sea was calm enough for the work. From the deck, the captives could see only open water, without land or sail. One by one, or in small groups, they were lifted to the rail and dropped.

A sick woman had been kept apart from the others below. The crew passed her upward with the rest. Her name was not entered. The insurers would later be told how many people had been thrown overboard, but the claim gave no account of who they were, what families had been separated, or how long each person had been confined in the hold.

On 1 December, another group was chosen. Twenty-six people were forced from the *Zong*. Ten more went into the water, according to the account presented at the trial: some were thrown, while others, seeing what was coming, leapt from the deck themselves. The count reached 132.

Afterward, the ship’s carpenter and sailors washed blood and filth from the planks. They hauled the empty shackles below and fitted the hatches over those who remained. More than two hundred enslaved people were still alive in the hold. The pumps continued to work, the sails filled, and the ship proceeded westward towards Jamaica.

When the *Zong* reached Black River, its owners claimed the dead as a loss covered by insurance. The underwriters refused payment. In March 1783, at the Guildhall in London, William Murray, Lord Mansfield, heard the case of *Gregson v. Gilbert*. The papers described the dead not by name but by number: 132 slaves, jettisoned at sea.

Mansfield had first heard the matter as an insurance dispute. The owners of the *Zong*, represented by John Lee, argued that Captain Collingwood had acted under necessity. The ship’s water was insufficient, the enslaved people were sick, and the loss had occurred through a peril of the sea. The underwriters’ counsel argued that the deaths followed a decision made by officers on the deck, with weather and wreck excluded as causes.

Mansfield directed a verdict for the insurers, although the surviving record leaves the legal reasoning less complete than later retellings often suggest. When the owners sought a new trial, he described the case as one of throwing goods overboard to save a ship. He also observed that a claim for the lives of enslaved people could not be separated from proof that the necessity had been real. The court treated the killings as an insurance question, testing whether the owners had met the conditions under which a maritime insurer might be made to pay.

The numbers gave the evidence its sharpest edge. Fifty-four on 29 November, forty-two on 30 November, twenty-six on 1 December, and ten more in the account presented afterward: a sequence of deaths converted into quantities that could be tested against water, distance, and the terms of a policy. The claim opened by asking whether the underwriters owed money for a sacrifice made at sea, leaving aside who the men, women, and children were.

The people killed had been carried from Africa as saleable property. Their deaths entered the court because the owners had assigned them a value and a commercial document had classified that value as recoverable. The ship’s chain of command and the court’s categories met in the claim.

The case acquired a different public meaning when Olaudah Equiano and Granville Sharp brought it before the reading public. Equiano, who had been enslaved as a child and later became a seaman and abolitionist, learned of the proceedings and sought information about the killings. Sharp, a London reformer who had already taken up the cases of enslaved people in England, used the *Zong* to expose the legal world that had made such a claim imaginable.

In 1783, newspapers and pamphlets reported the case amid arguments over the slave trade and parliamentary petitions. The campaign would eventually produce the 1788 Dolben Act, which regulated conditions aboard slave ships. The Act did not undo the commercial system that had carried the *Zong* across the Atlantic, but the publicity surrounding the case made its working visible: a captain could choose people for destruction, and the owners could ask a London company to indemnify them.

The men and women who died had no opportunity to give evidence at the Guildhall. The records preserve Collingwood’s account, the testimony of officers, the arguments of lawyers, and the arithmetic of the owners’ claim. Their silence was produced by the hierarchy that had confined them below deck, denied them standing as persons in the transaction, and allowed the owners to describe their deaths as jettisoned property.

Violence had travelled from the hold into the courtroom without ceasing to be organised violence. The captain’s decision was made on the Atlantic, but its consequences depended on ownership, insurance law, witness rules, and a judge’s understanding of necessity. Had the *Zong* been wrecked, the sea might have appeared as the cause. Instead, the court had to examine an act in which officers selected human beings for destruction and then sought payment for them. The judgment left the central crime to abolitionists, whose printed arguments named what the claim had tried to count.

By the time Mansfield heard *Gregson v. Gilbert*, English law had already given plantation society a broad Atlantic reach. Barbados had supplied one of its clearest early statements. In 1661, the island’s assembly enacted an Act for the better ordering and governing of Negroes. The statute treated enslaved Africans as a population to be disciplined, searched, punished, and inherited. It was not a private instruction for individual owners. The assembly prescribed the conduct of masters, the duties of officials, the penalties for resistance, and the consequences of a status attached to people and their descendants.

The Act described enslaved people as dangerous when left without supervision. It authorised severe punishment for movement, assembly, theft, and violence against whites. A person who struck a Christian could face execution; lesser offences could bring whipping, mutilation, confinement, or another bodily penalty. Owners were expected to provide food and clothing, but those clauses stood beside provisions protecting the owner’s authority to search a slave’s house, seize suspected fugitives, and recover people who had fled.

The statute made domination a public arrangement. A plantation owner did not have to negotiate each act of punishment with Bridgetown. Constables, magistrates, and owners received defined powers. Runaways could be pursued, assemblies of enslaved people broken up, and weapons confiscated. Those accused of rebellion could be tried under procedures unlike the protections available to free white subjects.

Barbados had changed rapidly before the Act was passed. During the 1630s, English settlers cultivated tobacco, cotton, and ginger with a mixture of European indentured labour and African labour. By the 1650s, planters were converting land to sugar. Sugar required mills, boiling houses, transport, and continuous field work, and it rewarded the planter who could make a large labour force work according to the crop’s timetable. The island’s enslaved African population grew while the proportion of white inhabitants declined relative to the people they controlled.

A planter in Saint Michael Parish could therefore draw on a legal authority that reached beyond the estate. The driver wielded the whip, but the assembly defined the offence, the magistrate authorised the procedure, and the owner’s claim to labour supplied the reason. The code’s violence was distributed through offices and local practice. Public power did not merely restrain private brutality; it gave private brutality a regular route through the colony.

The Act also separated enslaved people from protections associated with English Christianity. Baptism did not dissolve ownership. Conversion could coexist with bondage, so religious identity could not become a legal route to freedom. Later colonial statutes across the English Atlantic developed related rules. Virginia’s principle that a child’s status followed the condition of the mother turned a present coercion into an inherited one.

Barbados’s lawmakers helped turn an existing racial order into a durable one. Blackness became a sign of enslavability, punishment attached to that status, and white inhabitants received collective powers over the movement and bodies of enslaved people. A private estate thus depended upon public categories. Sugar profits could be calculated by a merchant in London, but their security rested on the island assembly’s definition of who could be beaten, detained, sold, and denied testimony.

The French Caribbean later supplied a related legal language. Louis XIV’s 1685 *Édit touchant la police des isles de l’Amérique française*, known as the Code Noir, required the baptism of enslaved people and ordered masters to supply food and clothing. It also regulated marriage, manumission, punishment, and sale, declared enslaved people movable property, and imposed corporal penalties for flight and assembly.

The French ordinance and the Barbados Act differed in their details and administrative settings. Both, however, placed plantation discipline inside royal law. Neither allowed the plantation to remain merely a private household. Across the Atlantic, the state made the enslaved person legible as property and made coercion available as an instrument of public order. That inheritance became visible in the machinery of a Jamaican estate. A sugar plantation did not turn labour into profit by the whip alone. Before a field was planted, an owner or absentee investor needed land, enslaved workers, draft animals, tools, a boiling house, and credit. London merchants advanced supplies against a future crop. Jamaican attorneys paid taxes and white craftsmen. Factors sold sugar in Kingston or overseas and entered the proceeds against the estate’s debts. The account book joined these transactions in columns.

An entry for a barrel of flour might stand beside a payment to a cooper and a valuation of an enslaved carpenter. The figures made unlike things commensurable. A person appeared as a capital asset, a skilled worker as an expected return, and a death as depreciation. Mortgages secured loans with land and enslaved people together. When an estate failed to meet its obligations, a creditor could seek possession through the courts and transfer a community of labourers with the mill and fields.

Credit gave plantation violence a future tense. The creditor was not present when an overseer assigned a task, yet the debt shaped the order because the crop had been promised before it existed. Sugar had to be cut and carried quickly. With the mill unable to wait for a convenient week, boiling continued through the night during harvest, and the estate’s calendar became a command.

At Rose Hall, Worthy Park, and other Jamaican estates, surviving books record provisions issued, work performed, punishments administered, and people who died or were sent away. Although the accounts leave the inner life of the labourers unrecorded, they show with precision how a plantation made bodies answer to debt and sugar prices. Drivers assembled gangs before daylight, assigned rows, counted tasks, inspected tools, and reported absences. The overseer converted the demands of the mill into orders that could be imposed on particular people.

Punishment entered the process at each point where labour failed to meet the estate’s measure. An overseer might order whipping for leaving a gang or breaking a tool. A driver carried out the sentence, while the estate book recorded an incident in discipline. Repeated punishment could produce injury, flight, or death; flight then justified patrols and rewards for capture. Jamaican laws against runaways gave private owners access to public force, so the search party, jail, and magistrate extended the plantation beyond its fence.

The system generated its own proof of necessity. When an enslaved worker met a daily task only under threat, the owner treated the threat as evidence that coercion was required. When sickness reduced output after overwork, the account book showed a loss to be managed rather than a warning about the labour regime. When an overseer demanded more hands during crop time, the creditor’s interest in repayment gave the demand weight. Profit did not wield the whip, but it gave the punishment a destination: cane cut, juice boiled, debt serviced, property preserved.

Across the Atlantic, the Portuguese plantation world followed a different route into the same human classification. In Brazil, sugar mills along the coast depended heavily on enslaved African labour. During the eighteenth century, the discovery of gold in Minas Gerais redirected people, credit, and shipping inland. Merchants at Rio de Janeiro supplied mining settlements with enslaved workers, food, tools, textiles, and animals. Gold moved outward; captives and provisions moved inward.

Minas Gerais was marked by dispersed mining settlements, river routes, urban markets, and shifting claims to gold. Saint-Domingue was marked by export estates whose mills and boiling houses required synchronised labour on a large scale. Both societies depended on Atlantic credit and racial slavery, but their violence took different administrative forms. In Brazil, the mule train, urban broker, and mining concession carried coercion inland. In the French colony, the plantation manager, ordinance, and militia bound it to the sugar estate.

French Saint-Domingue became the most productive sugar colony in the Caribbean before the Haitian Revolution. Its plantations operated through the Code Noir, later ordinances, Catholic institutions, colonial courts, and militia structures. Regulations specified work, food, religious instruction, punishment, and the treatment of fugitives, while local practice routinely exceeded the protections written into royal law. The colony’s wealth rested on an intense discipline that drew thousands of new captives from Africa each year.

Geography altered the form of domination. Law and commerce made both forms profitable, but they did not make them identical. Brazil’s mining economy dispersed coercion through routes and markets; Saint-Domingue’s sugar economy concentrated it around estates and mills. The difference shows where institutions entered the work of violence.

Toussaint Louverture understood this inheritance from within. Born into slavery at Bréda, near Cap-Haïtien, he became a coachman and later a manager before joining the uprising in Saint-Domingue in 1791. His letters from the 1790s move among several political languages: loyalty to France, opposition to royalism, defence of emancipation, and insistence upon military authority. He rose because he could command troops, negotiate with foreign powers, and restore production on estates damaged by war.

The 1801 constitution, promulgated under his direction, named Saint-Domingue a French colony but made Toussaint governor-general for life. It abolished slavery permanently, required equality before the law, and placed public order under a centralised executive. It also required agricultural labour to continue on plantations. Cultivators were tied to estates, military officers supervised work, and contracts limited movement.

Toussaint’s correspondence shows the pressure behind that choice. In letters to French officials, he argued that production was necessary to support the army and defend liberty against British, Spanish, and French intervention. To plantation labourers, he presented regulated and paid work as compatible with freedom. To commanders, he demanded obedience. The former slave who had confronted plantation power now used decrees, officers, and police to prevent the countryside from abandoning plantation agriculture.

A revolutionary government that had destroyed slavery inherited a colony whose ports, mills, and fields had been designed for coerced export production. Toussaint tried to make that inherited economy serve a black-led state. Napoleon Bonaparte tried to make the colony serve France again. The expedition commanded by Charles Leclerc arrived in 1802, arrested Toussaint, and deported him to France, where he died at Fort de Joux in April 1803. The constitution made emancipation a state principle; the French expedition showed how quickly an imperial state could turn military power against it.

Jean-Jacques Dessalines answered that expedition with a declaration on 1 January 1804. At Gonaïves, the act announcing Haitian independence described French rule as a system of violence and vowed that the former colony would never again accept domination. Its language was directed outward to France and inward to the people who had survived war, plantation labour, and re-enslavement.

The declaration made Haiti an argument in the form of a state. A society created by slavery had defeated the power that claimed to own it, and the victory altered the calculations of every slaveholding empire. The document did not ask European governments to recognise a moral principle in the abstract. It announced that people once treated as plantation property had taken political authority for themselves.

The effect reached the United States through the debate over the Louisiana Purchase in 1803. Napoleon’s defeat in Saint-Domingue weakened the French project in North America. The retrocession of Louisiana became less useful to Paris and more urgent to Washington. Thomas Jefferson and his cabinet debated constitutional authority, western expansion, and the purchase of New Orleans and the Mississippi basin.

The transfer doubled the territory of the United States, while the Haitian Revolution helped make it possible. A revolution against plantation slavery thus reshaped an imperial map whose later expansion strengthened slaveholding power. The same event that denied France a Caribbean colony opened land to an American republic whose institutions would carry slavery westward.

Economic incentives explain the plantation’s growth: sugar prices encouraged purchase, and credit rewarded expansion. They do not explain why Barbados lawmakers, French ministers, Jamaican magistrates, and American politicians gave coerced labour a racial and hereditary form. Profit selected the crop and encouraged the purchase of people; law fixed the person to the crop, while imperial ideology taught generations to see that fixation as natural. How far calculation explains slavery’s fusion with state power remains open, but any answer must account for the ledger and the statute.
