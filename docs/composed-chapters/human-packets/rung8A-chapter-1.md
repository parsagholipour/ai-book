## Chapter 1: What Do We Mean by Human Aggression?

The water-clock had already been opened when Socrates stood before the jurors in the court of the Heliaia, on the southwest edge of the Athenian Agora. Five hundred and one citizens sat on wooden benches behind a barrier, each holding a bronze voting disk hidden in his hand. Meletus, a young poet with a thin beard, had brought the charge. Anytus, a powerful leather-worker and politician, supported him; Lycon had joined them. The clerk read the indictment aloud: Socrates was accused of corrupting the young, of not believing in the gods of the city, and of introducing new divine beings.

Socrates wore no lawyer’s robe. His feet were bare, and his cloak had been folded around him in the ordinary way. He turned toward the jurors.

“Athenians,” he said, “I do not know how you have been affected by my accusers.”

They had called him a clever speaker who made the weaker argument appear the stronger. He had no prepared speech. More than seventy years old, he had never before appeared in court. The language of the courtroom was not his language, he said, though the jurors had heard it from him in the streets, workshops, gymnasium, and marketplace. He began with the older accusation, which had no place on the written indictment. Aristophanes had shown him in a basket, studying the clouds and teaching young men to cheat their fathers. Socrates pointed toward the wooden image of the juror’s voting urn and said that these stories had taken hold of the city long ago. They were harder to answer than Meletus because the men who spread them were not present.

The clerk called Meletus forward. Socrates asked him who improved the young.

“The laws,” Meletus said.

“But who improves them?”

The jurors shifted on the benches. Meletus named the judges. Socrates asked who improved the judges, and Meletus answered that the whole city did.

“Do all the Athenians improve them, and I alone corrupt them?”

“Yes,” said Meletus.

Socrates looked toward the water-clock. A narrow stream ran through the bronze tube into the vessel below. He compared the matter to horses: not everyone improved horses, but trainers and a few skilled men did; most people harmed them by handling them without knowledge. Meletus had accused him of corrupting the young deliberately, Socrates said. Yet no one would willingly live among people made worse by him, since bad companions could injure those who kept them.

Meletus had also said that Socrates believed in no gods. Socrates asked whether a man could believe in things connected with spirits and yet believe in no spirits at all. Meletus faltered. The jurors began to murmur. One official struck the floor with his staff.

Socrates did not bring his sons into court to plead for him. Other defendants had filled the benches with children, wives, and crying relatives. The jurors had sworn to judge the charge, he said, not to grant a favour. His own work was to go among the citizens and ask whether they cared more for money than for wisdom and the condition of their souls.

When his speech ended, the jurors cast their first votes. Each man took his bronze disk to the urns: one for acquittal, one for conviction. The result was narrow. Socrates was guilty. The clerk announced 280 votes for conviction and 221 for acquittal.

Meletus proposed death.

Socrates could have asked for exile or a fine. Instead he said that a man who had spent his life urging Athens toward virtue deserved to be maintained at public expense in the Prytaneum, where the city fed its Olympic victors and honoured guests. The jurors shouted. He amended the proposal: he could pay one mina, and his friends Plato, Crito, Critobulus, and Apollodorus would stand surety for thirty.

The water-clock was filled again. The jurors voted. This time the bronze disks returned a larger majority for death.

Socrates turned toward the men who had condemned him.

“Now the hour of departure has arrived,” he said, “and we go our ways—I to die, and you to live. Which is better, only God knows.”

At Çatalhöyük, between about 7400 and 6000 BCE, houses rose and were rebuilt on the Konya Plain of central Anatolia. Their walls were repeatedly plastered, their floors swept, and their entrances reached by ladders from the roofs. People buried relatives beneath those floors, often in crouched positions, then returned to the rooms above them to cook, sleep, mend tools, and receive visitors. A child might lie beneath a platform where an adult later sat. The settlement had no separate cemetery dividing the dead from domestic life. Its houses held both.

The burials were not uniform. Some individuals were placed alone; others were gathered into bundles after the flesh had decayed and reburied. Excavations directed first by James Mellaart in the 1960s and later by Ian Hodder’s teams uncovered hundreds of bodies, many incomplete because bones had been moved or removed. A skull could be taken from one burial and inserted into another. Red ochre appeared in some graves, while beads or shells accompanied particular bodies. The repeated handling of remains suggests that death did not end a person’s place within the household. At Çatalhöyük, relationships could be revised through the treatment of the dead.

Plastered walls above and beside the burials carried paintings and reliefs whose meanings remain unsettled. One room displayed rows of red marks; another held a pattern Mellaart interpreted as a plan of the settlement. Several rooms contained large horned forms often called bulls. The images were not simple records of the daily diet. Residents hunted wild cattle and kept sheep and goats, but the prominence of horns may have connected feasting with memory, danger, and prestige in ways the walls no longer explain. A painting from a level usually dated to the seventh millennium BCE has been read as showing human figures near headless bodies or animals. Later scholarship has questioned whether it represents a battle, a hunting scene, or something more ritual and abstract.

The buildings make interpretation harder. Çatalhöyük had no palace, temple, or broad public square of the kind familiar from later cities. Houses were closely packed, with shared walls and access across the roofs. Some rooms contained more elaborate installations: modeled breasts concealing animal horns, benches raised above graves, and painted surfaces renewed many times. These differences may indicate distinctions of status, age, household history, or ritual role, but they do not reveal a ruling class. Nor does the absence of a palace mean the absence of power. A family that controlled access to a room, a burial, or a store of grain possessed influence without leaving a written decree.

Several skeletons bear healed fractures, blunt-force trauma, or other damage, and some wounds occurred before death. A few skull injuries have been discussed as signs of repeated blows, perhaps from thrown clay balls or other projectiles. The pattern is suggestive without being decisive. A fracture tells us that force entered a life; it does not tell us whether that force came from an assault, a fall from a roof, an accident during work, or a conflict between settlements. The body records violence more readily than intention.

Healing supplies another kind of evidence. Bone that knitted shows that the injured person survived, was fed, and was sheltered long enough to recover. Someone made room for that body. At Çatalhöyük, the settlement that produced skull trauma also produced careful burials, renewed plaster, and rooms in which the dead remained beneath the living. Harm and care belonged to the same household.

The wall imagery has encouraged the claim that permanent communities brought warfare into human history. The chronology does not support so simple a conclusion. Collective violence appears in several Neolithic contexts, but evidence before cities and states is scattered, and the word warfare carries expectations formed by later armies. A group defending a field, a household avenging a killing, and a territorial force seeking submission may all use weapons without creating the same political world. Çatalhöyük provides no army list, campaign account, or monument naming an enemy. It provides houses, bodies, paintings, and the wear of repeated occupation. That combination resists the division between a peaceful village and a violent city. People at Çatalhöyük hunted animals whose horns later appeared in interior installations, handled human remains, and lived densely enough for neighbours to be unavoidable. Some suffered injuries; others survived them. None of this proves a culture of violence, and the absence of fortifications proves no culture of peace. Aggression must be separated from anger, and both from the organised forms that governments could count, authorise, and repeat.

Anger is an internal state. It may follow insult, fear, pain, jealousy, or frustration, and it may end in speech or withdrawal. Aggression is behaviour intended to harm another person who wishes to avoid that harm. The intention is often inferred rather than observed. A blow delivered in rage and a calculated threat used to extract obedience belong to the same broad category, although one is reactive and the other instrumental. That distinction clarifies the courtroom where Socrates stood. His questions irritated the jurors, and his refusal to plead in the expected manner may have felt provocative. Irritation was not assault. The jury’s decision was coercive because the city claimed the right to impose a penalty, but the verdict was not an angry scuffle between Socrates and Meletus. A legal institution had converted accusation into procedure, procedure into a vote, and a vote into the threat of death.

Interpersonal violence is narrower: the intentional use of physical force or power by one person or a small group against another, with a substantial likelihood of injury, death, psychological harm, deprivation, or impaired development. A household beating and a street stabbing fit within it. So may withholding food by a person who controls a dependent, even when the body shows little trace. Aggression can be verbal or psychological; violence points to force or power capable of serious physical or social damage. The terms overlap without becoming identical.

Raiding changes both scale and purpose. It is a swift group attack on a settlement, convoy, farm, or rival community, usually intended to seize goods, livestock, captives, or people before a sustained counterattack can form. Killing may occur, but it is often one instrument among others. In the precolonial Philippines, accounts from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries describe communities where raiding for captives existed alongside household feuds, headhunting, trade, and seasonal work. A captured woman could be forced into agricultural labour; a young man could be taken as a slave or sold onward. The attack joined interpersonal violence to an economy of possession.

A raid does not become warfare merely because it uses weapons. Warfare is organised lethal aggression between autonomous political groups. The groups may be states, chiefdoms, confederations, or communities without central government, provided that the fighting is sustained or recurrent enough to involve collective decisions, recognised participants, preparations, and objectives beyond one private attack. A Mongol cavalry raid could belong to a larger campaign; a Viking assault on a monastery could remain a raid; repeated attacks between neighbouring communities could occupy the uncertain ground between feud and war.

Coercive law works through an authority that attaches punishment to disobedience and reserves force for officials who enforce the command. A fine, confiscation, beating, imprisonment, or execution can compel conduct. The person who obeys need not feel anger toward the magistrate, and the magistrate need not hate the person punished. The harm is organised because a court, council, police force, prison, or army has defined an offence and made its penalty enforceable.

The case of Apuleius shows why an accusation cannot be read as a simple report of aggression. In the *Apologia*, delivered in North Africa in the second century CE, Apuleius defended himself against charges that he had used magic to secure the affection of Pudentilla, a wealthy widow whom he had married. His accusers assembled suspicious details: verses, medical knowledge, the purchase of fish, and an episode involving a woman who suffered what Apuleius described as an epileptic fit while seeking advice for an earache. Lynn Thorndike later observed that Apuleius presented the defence as one of philosophy as well as reputation. The court had to decide whether learning, marriage, status, and unusual conduct could be converted into a criminal charge.

Organised atrocity is more extensive still. It describes large-scale, systematic violence against civilians, including genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and ethnic cleansing, though the phrase itself is not a single legal offence. Its defining feature is not only the number killed. A massacre may be sudden; an atrocity process ordinarily involves classification, preparation, permission, transport, confinement, incitement, or the deliberate removal of restraints. Clerks register people, police gather them, soldiers guard them, and officials distribute orders. The offices that sort populations and police subjects can make violence reach people who never meet the men who harm them.

The categories overlap. A raid may contain assaults, warfare may include raiding, and a state can turn a local conflict into atrocity by naming an entire population as an enemy. These distinctions ask what happened, who decided, how many people were involved, and what record the event should have left. Without them, a blow in a courtyard and a deportation organised across provinces become one undifferentiated impulse.

The traces differ because the acts differ. A single injury first enters the record through a body. A massacre enters through bodies gathered together. A state punishment enters through an office that names the condemned and records the penalty.

A healed fracture in a skeleton from Çatalhöyük may be the residue of a blow, a fall, or a workplace accident. The bone can show location, severity, and duration. A sharp-force mark on a rib or a depressed fracture in a skull can narrow the possibilities, especially when similar wounds occur across a group. Yet the individual history remains partly hidden. The body preserves the physical consequence, not the argument that preceded it, the speaker who threatened, or the neighbour who intervened. Osteologists must compare patterns rather than turn one damaged bone into a complete narrative.

A mass grave creates another kind of evidence. At Talheim in southern Germany, usually dated to about 5000 BCE, the remains of at least thirty-four people were placed together in a pit. Many had injuries consistent with blows from stone axes or adzes, and the bodies included men, women, and children. The arrangement and injury pattern have often been interpreted as a massacre connected to Neolithic conflict. The grave reveals concentration, selection, and lethal force applied to a group. It does not name the attackers or explain whether the victims belonged to one household, a settlement, or a group moving through the landscape.

At Jebel Sahaba in the Nile Valley, a cemetery dated to roughly 13,000–11,000 BCE contains dozens of individuals with projectile injuries and embedded stone fragments. The graves indicate repeated violence, perhaps connected to competition during severe climatic conditions, but the attackers left no campaign account. Talheim and Jebel Sahaba are separated by thousands of years and different ecologies. Set beside Çatalhöyük, they show variation in scale and survival rather than a universal ladder from simple aggression to organised war.

Legal execution records carry a different authority because an institution speaks through them. The Old Bailey proceedings, published for London trials from 1674 onward, record indictments, testimony, verdicts, and sentences. A person hanged at Tyburn in the eighteenth century appears not only as a body but as a named defendant processed through a court. The record may preserve the charge of murder, the words attributed to witnesses, the jury’s verdict, and the judge’s sentence. The killing has been classified before it occurs. It is violence inflicted by the state under a rule that claims public legitimacy. That classification can conceal as much as it reveals. The court’s vocabulary decides which injuries count as murder, which deaths count as lawful punishment, and whose testimony deserves belief. A thief condemned at the Old Bailey and a prisoner killed by soldiers may both die by state force, while only one death enters a formal execution record. The archive bears the imprint of institutions that had the power to name, preserve, and destroy.

What disappears when no one builds a grave, keeps a register, or leaves a durable wound? Çatalhöyük’s plaster survives because rooms were abandoned and buried; wooden tools, spoken threats, and acts of intimidation do not. A state archive may preserve the sentence passed on a prisoner while omitting the hunger, beating, or forced labour that brought the case before the court. Regions with long bureaucratic traditions appear more densely violent partly because their officials wrote more of what they did.

Comparison must live with that imbalance. The skull, the pit, and the execution register are three kinds of historical remainder, shaped by an event and by the conditions under which evidence survived, rather than three readings of one universal quantity called aggression. Which unrecorded injuries, vanished raids, and unburied dead would alter the picture if they could speak? The past has left no single scale, but a scatter of traces made by unequal lives and unequal powers.
