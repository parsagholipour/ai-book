## Chapter 9: Gunpowder, Bureaucracy, and Mass Mobilization

>
> — Oliver Cromwell, *Cassell's Illustrated History of England/Volume 3/Chapter 5*

Oliver Cromwell was still mounting the rise above Broad Moor when the king’s guns opened. It was Saturday, 14 June 1645, and a white mist lay over the fields near Naseby. Cromwell’s horse stood twelve ranks deep along the parliamentary right, the men’s buff coats dark at the shoulders, their matchcords tucked ready in their hands. Beyond them the ground fell towards the hedges and cottages of Sulby. In the hollow behind, the foot regiments waited beneath their colours. Fairfax had placed the infantry in the centre, Skippon among them, while on the left Ireton’s horse faced Prince Rupert. The king’s army stretched across the opposite ridge: red and blue coats, upright pikes, and cannon showing black between the battalions.

A trumpet sounded. The royalist foot came down the slope.

Cromwell saw the movement before the smoke hid it. He lifted his hat and pointed with his sword. The regiments under his command moved forward at a walk, then broke into a trot. Their pistols were not fired together. Files opened, horses pressed through the intervals, and carbines spoke in separate cracks until drifting grey covered the front. Royalist horse met them hard. Men fell beneath the hooves. A rider in a gilt helmet went down on his back and was trampled before he could rise.

On the other side of the field, Rupert’s charge struck Ireton’s wing and drove it from its ground. The parliamentary left gave way towards Naseby village. Ireton was wounded in the thigh and face, and the royalist horse swept past him towards the baggage and the country beyond. Some of the king’s men began to plunder the wagons. Others chased fugitives as far as the houses.

After his first success, Cromwell re-formed on the ridge, drawing his squadrons together while the royalist infantry pressed upon Fairfax. The king’s foot had broken through part of the parliamentary line. For a few minutes the two armies were mixed, pikes and musket butts striking at arm’s length, colours leaning and vanishing in the powder smoke. Sir Thomas Fairfax rode into the press with his hat on the point of his sword. Skippon, bleeding from a musket wound, was carried among the soldiers and called them back into their ranks.

Near the king, the royalist reserve waited. Charles watched from horseback, with Prince Rupert’s brother Maurice beside him. His infantry had advanced too far and opened gaps between the battalions. Cromwell brought his horse round the flank, passed the captured guns, and struck them from behind.

Royalist soldiers turned to see the blue-sashed cavalry coming through the smoke. Their pikes faced Fairfax; their backs were open to Cromwell. The first rank fired without orders, and the bullets went high. Cromwell’s men rode in, discharged their pistols, and used sword and hatchet among the files. A colour-bearer dropped beneath a horse. The standard fell, was lifted, and fell again.

A royalist officer called for quarter. No one heard him.

Cromwell’s regiments crossed the field in a widening line. They passed the king’s guns, where the artillerymen had abandoned their pieces, and reached the mass of foot gathered around the royal standard. The standard guard closed about it. Fairfax’s infantry came on from the front, pikes lowered. Between the two attacks the royalist line folded inward.

Charles moved towards the standard, but Lord Carnarvon seized his bridle. The king’s attendants urged him away. Around them men threw down muskets and ran towards the rear, while parliamentary horse cut at those who tried to form again. Rupert returned from the village at a gallop and found the battle lost.

The royalist foot began to yield along the whole ridge.

Cromwell rode among his men, his face and sleeves black with powder. An officer beside him raised his sword and shouted for the enemy to be pursued. Cromwell reined in long enough to answer him. The words were written afterwards in Cassell’s Illustrated History of England: “Sir, this is none other but the hand of God, and to Him alone belongs the glory.” Then he turned the squadrons towards the fleeing army.

The road to Constantinople ran differently from the field at Naseby. Cromwell’s squadrons had needed powder, horses, and pay, but an English army could still move through a landscape whose villages and committees supplied it unevenly. Mehmed II brought to the Bosporus a force that had to feed itself before it could batter the city. On 6 April 1453, when the Ottoman camp settled before the land walls, carts were already moving, animals were being counted, and labourers were calculating the grain required by the gun crews.

The city stood at the end of a peninsula guarded on its western side by the Theodosian Walls, a double line of masonry and earthworks extending for roughly six kilometres from the Golden Horn to the Sea of Marmara. The inner wall rose behind a broad moat and an outer wall, with towers placed to observe and fire along the approaches. Constantine XI commanded a garrison far smaller than the circumference demanded. Venetian and Genoese volunteers joined Greek soldiers, and Giovanni Giustiniani Longo took charge of the principal defence, but the walls had been built for an empire with more men than the diminished Byzantine government could provide.

Mehmed’s army was not simply a crowd surrounding an old city. Engineers, infantry, cavalry, miners, sailors, and artillerymen depended on one another. The sultan had prepared roads and shipping routes, collected provisions, and assembled cannon of several sizes. Among the specialists was Orban, a Hungarian or Transylvanian founder whose precise origin remains uncertain but whose service to Mehmed is recorded in the siege tradition. Later accounts say that he first approached Constantine’s government and then offered his knowledge to the Ottoman ruler when Byzantine resources could not meet his price.

Orban’s great bombard was cast at Edirne in the winter before the siege. Later descriptions make it perhaps eight or nine metres long, capable of throwing a stone ball weighing several hundred kilograms, though the dimensions reported by chroniclers grew with the reputation of the weapon. Its importance lay less in one spectacular measurement than in the work required to use it. The bombard was difficult to move, load, and fire, and slow to reload. Timber sledges, oxen, roads, powder, stone shot, and men had to bring it before the walls.

When the bombard fired, the effect was physical and administrative. Its projectile struck masonry that had resisted generations of siege engines, while defenders repaired the damage before another assault. Ottoman gunners selected stretches of wall rather than scattering ammunition across the circuit. The largest piece may have discharged only a few times each day, with a pause while its chamber cooled and its crew prepared another charge. Smaller cannon maintained pressure elsewhere. The wall was attacked as a sequence of assigned tasks: mark a weak point, bring forward shot, clear rubble, protect the crews, and send infantry when the breach became practicable.

Constantine’s defenders answered with repairs and sorties. They filled craters with baskets, stones, and timber, raised barriers behind damaged sections, and concentrated Giustiniani’s men where the bombard had opened gaps. The defenders had no equal supply of heavy artillery, but they knew the gates, ditches, and firing positions. Each night converted the day’s damage into another night’s labour.

The Golden Horn presented a separate problem. A chain stretched across its entrance, preventing Ottoman vessels from entering the harbour, where Byzantine and Italian ships could support the walls. Mehmed ordered ships hauled overland from the Bosporus, around Galata, and into the sheltered water beyond the chain. Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts describe wooden rollers and greased tracks working under cover of darkness. Boats had to be built and dragged, the route cleared, and the operation protected from observation and attack.

The siege lasted seven weeks. Disease, hunger, fatigue, and desertion threatened the Ottoman force as much as Byzantine missiles did. Food stores diminished inside the city, walls required constant repair, and the Genoese commander was wounded. On 20 April, a small Christian fleet fought its way towards the harbour against a much larger Ottoman force. The ships survived, encouraging the besieged, but the encounter also showed how little room remained for outside help.

Mehmed’s army tightened its works as the bombardment continued. Trenches and fascines approached the ditches. Guns were repositioned. Miners attempted to pass beneath the walls while defenders dug countermines and fought in the dark below ground. The Ottoman command distributed labour across these operations because the army was organised around the sultan’s authority and supplied through channels extending beyond the camp. The great gun could not create a breach by itself. Men had to remove earth, carry ammunition, guard the working parties, and preserve enough strength for the final assault.

By the last week of May, the damage near the Roman gate of St Romanus had become severe. The old walls had not become useless; they had become a contested workplace. Ottoman cannon struck, Byzantine crews rebuilt, and every repair narrowed the time before the next attack. On the night of 28 May, the besieged attended services and prepared for what they understood would be the final assault. Mehmed’s army divided its attack into waves so that fresh troops could follow exhausted men and pressure could be applied at more than one point.

The assault began before dawn on 29 May. Irregular troops went forward first, followed by more disciplined formations. Defenders repelled attacks at several places, but the long contest had reduced their numbers and exhausted their reserves. Giustiniani was wounded and withdrawn, damaging morale at the most dangerous moment. Ottoman troops entered through a breach and, according to the standard accounts, through the Kerkoporta, though the exact sequence remains disputed. Constantine XI died in the fighting. By afternoon, Mehmed possessed the city.

The capture was violent, but it was not an unbounded eruption. Soldiers were permitted a period of plunder under customary rules, while particular buildings, skilled workers, and valuable installations received protection when the sultan judged them useful. Mehmed entered Constantinople, renamed and rebuilt it as the centre of an Ottoman imperial order, and converted the conquest into a capital. Artillery had overcome the walls because an office, a camp, a treasury, and a ruler had kept thousands of people at work beside it.

The surviving Ottoman records are less dramatic than the bombard. A register rarely describes the instant when masonry fell or a soldier crossed a parapet. It counts iron, charcoal, wages, transport, and repair. In those counts, artillery appears as a recurring expense.

The Tophane district on the northern shore of the Golden Horn became associated with cannon founding under Ottoman rule, especially after the conquest. Its workshops drew on founders who prepared moulds, smiths who worked fittings, carpenters who built carriages, and clerks who entered materials and payments. Registers from the imperial arsenal and related offices record quantities of copper and tin, deliveries of charcoal, the employment of masters and apprentices, and the issue of shot. A gun existed in the record as a sequence of materials and obligations before it existed on a battlefield.

Bronze artillery required more than molten metal. Founders needed a mould strong enough to hold the charge, fuel capable of sustaining high heat, and a method of cooling the casting without destroying it. Iron guns raised different problems of forging and durability. A defective barrel could burst and kill its crew, while a successful one still required powder, a carriage, and trained personnel. Inventories joined weapons to their surroundings. A cannon without shot was entered beside the shot it lacked; a damaged carriage generated a request for timber; powder stored in a damp room became a loss charged to someone’s account.

The register’s language turns danger into administration. A delivery might be divided among named depots, with each amount assigned to a purpose. Saltpetre, sulphur, and charcoal were handled separately before being processed into gunpowder. Powder magazines near Constantinople required guarded storage and controlled access because fire could destroy a stockpile and an unauthorised issue could arm a mutiny. The city that held the sultan’s palace and arsenal also held the ledgers connecting a mine, a workshop, and a fortress.

An entry for a master founder could contain the practical outline of his life: his name, his wage, the material assigned to him, and the officer responsible for his work. Payment was a form of command. So was requisition. When the state ordered charcoal from a district, it transferred the burden of military production to woodcutters, carriers, and villagers who might never see the gun they enabled. Taxation and manufacture met in the furnace.

The Tophane records show that standardisation was partial. Cannon varied in bore, weight, and intended use. Large siege pieces were difficult to transport and might remain with a fortress for years; smaller field guns could move with an army. A register distinguishing calibres and repairs allowed an arsenal to learn through accumulation: what had been cast, what had burst, what had been issued, and which workshops required correction. This paper world gave uncertainty a place to be managed. The Ottoman state could not make every casting sound or every powder store dry, but it could name a responsible official, record a shortfall, and order another delivery. At Naseby, Cromwell attributed success to God while asking Parliament not to discourage the soldiers who had fought. In the arsenal, success had to be attributed to labour, material, and payment because a future campaign depended on finding them again.

The Ottoman fiscal system supplied artillery without making every province equally governed. Tax farming allowed the treasury to assign revenue collection to individuals who paid in advance or undertook to deliver a specified sum. Collectors sought returns from agricultural districts and customs points, and their local influence made extraction possible. The arrangement gave the central government money quickly while leaving room for abuse, bargaining, and evasion.

Tokugawa Japan developed a different relationship between central rule and weapons after Tokugawa Ieyasu’s victory at Sekigahara in 1600. The shogunate did not abolish armed power. It divided it. Daimyo retained domains, stipends, castles, and retainers, but their movements and construction projects were watched. The alternate-attendance system required major lords to spend time in Edo and maintain costly residences there. Money, roads, and hostageship restrained the possibility of independent war.

The Japanese case is often reduced to a story in which firearms arrived and were then suppressed. The arquebus entered Japan through Tanegashima in 1543, and Japanese smiths soon produced local copies. During the sixteenth century, firearms were used extensively by Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and their enemies. At Nagashino in 1575, Oda and Tokugawa forces deployed ranks of arquebusiers against Takeda cavalry, though the familiar image of uninterrupted volley fire simplifies a more complicated battle. Guns remained in arsenals and domain stores after unification.

What changed was political permission. Hideyoshi’s sword hunt of 1588 ordered weapons collected from peasants, presenting the measure as part of a project to separate cultivators from warriors and secure labour for a great Buddhist image. Under the Tokugawa shogunate, status distinctions, castle regulations, travel controls, and licensing practices limited the private circulation of arms. Sword edicts associated with the shogunate and domains regulated who might wear swords and how weapons could be possessed. The governing question was which household could turn a weapon into political force.

In the Ottoman domains, the treasury depended on military reach across a land empire exposed to Habsburg, Safavid, Russian, and Mediterranean rivals. Tax farms, timar obligations, customs revenues, and provincial administration supported cavalry, infantry, fortifications, and naval forces. The Janissaries became a permanent corps, and firearms increasingly joined their equipment. Constantinople’s arsenal was connected to an empire that needed weapons in Belgrade, Baghdad, Cairo, and along the Black Sea. Its administrative problem was expansion and defence over distance.

Tokugawa rulers faced a different problem after decades of civil war. They inherited a country where armed households and autonomous lords had made violence politically profitable. Regulation could succeed because the shogunate combined surveillance with a settlement of status and land. Daimyo, unlike conquered peasants, were incorporated as ranked lords whose privileges depended on compliance. The result was an armed ruling class within a society where access to weapons and military action was increasingly controlled.

The comparison is not Ottoman permissiveness against Japanese pacification. Ottoman officials also licensed, collected, and restricted weapons, especially in cities and unstable provinces. Tokugawa domains continued to manufacture guns, train with them, and maintain stores. The difference lay in the fiscal and political uses surrounding the weapon. Ottoman artillery belonged to a state obliged to project force outward and bargain with provincial intermediaries. Tokugawa firearms belonged to domains subordinated within an order designed to prevent them from combining their resources against one another.

A tax farmer in Anatolia and a domain official in Edo therefore handled different military pressures. The first converted local revenue into a payment sustaining imperial war; the second converted military potential into obedience and ritualised attendance. One system widened the state’s ability to fight across frontiers. The other narrowed the opportunities for domestic war without eliminating the prestige of arms.

Gunpowder left both results open, since the same technology could support siege, conquest, policing, status distinction, or controlled display. Its consequences depended on who collected revenue, who maintained workshops, and who was allowed to command armed men.

The debate over the military revolution has often begun with weapons and ended with states. Geoffrey Parker argued that improved artillery and trace italienne fortifications forced European rulers into larger armies, heavier taxation, and more elaborate administration. The Ottoman assault on Constantinople supports part of that sequence: the cannon mattered because a government could feed its crews and sustain a siege. Jeremy Black has questioned the idea of a single, Europe-centred revolution unfolding at one pace, stressing variation in warfare, state formation, and military practice.

The Ottoman and Tokugawa cases support a narrower conclusion. Technology changed the reach and cost of violence more readily than it changed the motives of rulers or soldiers. Mehmed wanted Constantinople before Orban cast his bombard; Tokugawa rulers wanted political submission after firearms had become familiar in Japan. Guns enlarged what their governments could organise. They did not choose the target, raise the tax, classify the subject, or decide when restraint served power. Those decisions remained political, and the records of the arsenal, the tax office, and the weapons edict show who turned a weapon into mass harm.
