## Chapter 8: States, Slavery, and the Atlantic World

In 1789, Parliament and the British public were shown a plan of the slave ship *Brookes*, and the violence of the Atlantic trade appeared as a problem of measurement. The drawing presented a side elevation and several decks crowded with small rectangles, each marking the allotted space of an enslaved African. The ship had been designed to carry 454 people, although the diagram associated with the regulated trade showed 482 bodies packed into compartments below deck. A human being became a length, a width, and a place in a grid. The plan’s power came from its plainness: no waves, cries, names, or bodies in motion, only timber, geometry, and confinement calculated by the state.

The image followed parliamentary efforts to regulate the trade rather than abolish it. The 1788 Dolben Act limited the number of enslaved people a British vessel could carry according to its tonnage, and the *Brookes* plan showed what compliance looked like. Its compartments were more spacious than conditions that had often prevailed before regulation, but the improvement sharpened the horror. Even under a legal limit, the ship’s interior arranged hundreds of people as freight. At sea, this was an ordinary voyage undertaken after a government had decided that the trade could continue if its cargo received sufficient room. That evidence supports the strongest account of the traffic as a market. Liverpool merchants invested in ships, credit, insurance, and cargoes. Customs records registered vessels leaving the Mersey, entering African ports, and returning with sugar, rum, ivory, and human beings. Firms connected with William Davenport recorded purchases on the African coast and sales in the Caribbean. Ships sailed because merchants expected a return; captains calculated provisions because voyages had costs; owners sought compensation when people died because the law recognised financial loss. Commerce directed the violence from the voyage’s first outfitting. Yet commerce alone cannot explain the rectangles. A market may price an object, but it cannot authorise a captain to seize people, carry them across an ocean, or recover money through a court when they die. The *Brookes* required Parliament to define the permitted load, customs officers to clear the vessel, naval power to protect British shipping, colonial governments to receive the survivors, and courts to enforce claims over them. Someone had to decide that a person could be entered into a calculation of tonnage.

Nor were the rectangles passengers choosing berths. They represented people captured, purchased, or delivered under force, separated from communities whose languages and political ties the purchasers did not need to recognise. The deck plan made bodies interchangeable because the trade had already made them legally and commercially equivalent. The crew needed to know how many people it could confine, feed, guard, and sell. It did not need to know who they were.

Liverpool’s records place the diagram within a larger operation. Between the 1750s and the abolition of the British trade in 1807, merchants from the port financed hundreds of voyages. Their papers listed casks of water, barrels of flour, firearms, iron goods, beads, and cloth beside the expected number of captives. The same voyage carried trade goods outward, enslaved Africans to Jamaica or Barbados, and plantation produce homeward. When mortality reduced the expected return, the loss entered correspondence as a business problem. The arithmetic was real, but law, armed force, and racial classification made it possible.

The 1789 image became an abolitionist weapon because it turned an unseen interior into a public object. William Wilberforce and the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade used such representations to challenge the distance between British consumers and Atlantic suffering. A reader in London could trace the lines of the deck with a finger and imagine the compression beneath it. The plan condemned the trade while revealing how government, commerce, and maritime discipline had joined to produce it.

A different record begins with a name.

Olaudah Equiano wrote that he was born in Essaka, in what is now southeastern Nigeria, and kidnapped with his sister as a child. In *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano*, published in London in 1789, he described their separation, the households through which he passed, and the journey to the coast. His account moved from village life to a ship’s hold, from Virginia to the Royal Navy, and from plantation labour to Atlantic commerce. The narrative restored sequence to a life that the ship plan reduced to a count. His first recorded purchaser was Michael Pascal, a British naval officer who called him Gustavus Vassa. The renaming made possession ordinary. Equiano served aboard naval vessels during the Seven Years’ War, learned to read and write, and became familiar with navigation, trade, and seafaring discipline. In 1763 Pascal sold him to Robert King, a Quaker merchant in Montserrat. King allowed him to earn money through small trading ventures. On 10 July 1766, after Equiano had accumulated the required sum, he bought his freedom for forty pounds.

The sum carried several histories. Equiano could earn because maritime commerce created a narrow opening for a skilled and trusted worker. He could buy freedom because a slaveholder agreed to recognise money earned by an enslaved man. He remained vulnerable because another owner might have refused the transaction or taken his savings. Freedom was neither a gift nor the product of courage alone. It depended on a property regime that allowed a human being to be bought and sold, and on commercial practices that permitted a small accumulation toward release.

After 1766, Equiano travelled widely. He worked in the Mediterranean, visited the Arctic during an expedition in search of a Northwest Passage, and lived for periods in London, where he joined religious and abolitionist circles. He became a Methodist and later associated with evangelical reformers who linked conversion to public campaigns. His experience as a sailor gave him a language familiar to merchants and naval officers. He knew the smell of a ship’s hold, the accounting of provisions, the hierarchy of a crew, and the threats governing an Atlantic crossing. His authority rested on detail. Equiano recalled the terror of seeing the sea for the first time, the confinement of the hold, the smell of sickness and excrement, and the despair of people who believed they were being taken somewhere from which there was no return. He described African societies as politically and socially complex, answering the European claim that enslavement rescued people from savagery. He also recorded the religious arguments he encountered in Britain, where Christian language condemned cruelty while merchants continued to profit from it.

Equiano’s testimony belonged to a campaign. He petitioned Parliament, circulated his book, corresponded with Granville Sharp, and supported the abolitionist effort associated with the Society of Friends and later with Wilberforce. When he presented accounts of the Middle Passage, he converted private injury into evidence before readers with political influence. His life showed that the enslaved were authors, traders, sailors, believers, and witnesses, not anonymous units beneath the *Brookes*.

Print made that testimony public. The first edition of *The Interesting Narrative* appeared in London in 1789, the same year the *Brookes* plan circulated. One document offered a measured interior; the other offered a remembered interior. Equiano’s name resisted interchangeability. The boy called Gustavus Vassa, the sailor who bought his freedom, and the author addressing the public were one person moving through different legal statuses.

The plantations that consumed this labour did not organise coercion in the same fashion. Jamaica and Barbados belonged to the British Caribbean; Potosí belonged to the Spanish American mining world. Their different commodities produced different institutions.

Barbados was colonised by English settlers in 1627. During the 1640s, planters shifted toward sugar, a crop requiring land clearance, mills, boiling houses, skilled processing, and a large labour force working to a strict seasonal rhythm. By the later seventeenth century, the island’s fields and factories were tied to London merchants, Bristol insurers, and Atlantic shipping. Sugar could be stored, pledged, exported, and converted into political influence. The plantation was a farm and factory, but also a household, prison, and jurisdiction governed by men who owned land and labourers.

The Barbados Slave Code of 1661 gave this regime a durable legal form. Enslaved Africans became inheritable property, were denied ordinary legal standing, and were exposed to whipping, mutilation, and death. The code ordered Christian instruction while denying enslaved people the protections Christian status might imply. It made racial difference a presumption of law. A white person’s testimony and a Black person’s testimony did not carry equal force, and a slave-owning magistrate could help decide the fate of the people whose labour enriched his class.

Jamaica developed on a larger and more mountainous scale. The English seized the island from Spain in 1655, and sugar expanded rapidly after the late seventeenth century. Estates were extensive, transport was difficult, and enslaved communities established themselves in the interior, where Maroon groups resisted colonial control. The First Maroon War, fought in the 1720s and 1730s, showed that plantation owners required military power as well as law. Treaties with Maroon communities in 1739 and 1740 recognised a political fact the slave code could not erase: colonial rule depended on negotiation when armed suppression became too costly.

On a Jamaican sugar estate, work began before sunrise. Gang labour placed groups under overseers who measured progress through the day. Cane had to be cut, hauled to the mill, crushed, boiled, and clarified before the juice spoiled. A delay in the fields became a loss in the boiling house. The calendar of sugar connected the whip to the creditor’s expectation, the mill to the ship’s departure, and exhaustion to the price quoted in London.

Potosí operated through another labour system. After silver was discovered at Cerro Rico in 1545, the city grew into one of the largest urban centres in the Americas. The viceregal government reorganised older Andean labour obligations through the mita, requiring Indigenous communities to send rotating contingents to the mines and associated industries. The mita did not make every worker hereditary property in the same legal sense as British Caribbean chattel slavery. It did compel communities to supply labour under colonial command, separate men from families, expose them to dangerous underground work, and transfer the value of Andean labour into imperial and private hands.

Legal status shaped the violence. In Barbados and Jamaica, the planter claimed a permanent right over the enslaved person’s body, descendants, movement, marriage, and sale. At Potosí, Spanish officials and mine owners drew labour from named Indigenous communities through tribute and rotation. A *mitayo* might return home after his term, though illness, debt, distance, and death often prevented it. The colonial state claimed labour from a community rather than treating each labourer as inheritable estate property. Coercion was severe in both settings, but its unit differed: the Caribbean plantation concentrated ownership in the master’s household, while the mita worked through village authorities, *corregidores*, recruiters, and viceregal officials.

Production widened the difference. Sugar required continuous processing and punished delay with immediate spoilage. Silver mining involved shafts, tunnels, mills, mercury amalgamation, transport, and uncertain extraction. Potosí therefore depended on engineers, mine owners, royal officials, Indigenous intermediaries, and merchants supplying food and tools. The mountain drew workers into an urban economy; Barbados and Jamaica organised enslaved people into estates policed by patrols and armed forces.

Religion and law diverged without becoming humane. The Spanish Crown declared Indigenous people royal subjects and issued legislation concerning their treatment, including the New Laws of 1542. Local interests evaded, amended, or overrode those protections, but subjecthood created a field of litigation and petition. British Caribbean law more openly defined Africans and their descendants as property. French colonies operated under the 1685 Code Noir, issued under Louis XIV, which required Catholic baptism and regulated manumission while authorising corporal punishment and reinforcing the master’s control.

Resistance followed the structure of each system. Jamaican Maroons fought from forested terrain and forced treaties from the colonial government. On Caribbean plantations, enslaved people slowed work, broke tools, fled, formed communities, poisoned animals, and revolted. In the Andes, Indigenous communities petitioned, negotiated quotas, hid workers, and resisted officials. The Túpac Amaru rebellion of 1780–1781, directed against a wider structure of colonial taxation and rule, revealed the anger produced by forced labour, tribute, and administrative abuse.

The sugar plantation and the silver mine were both built on organised coercion, but they produced different social worlds. Barbados made racial slavery a compact island regime governed by planter assemblies and patrols. Jamaica combined plantation expansion with frontier war and Maroon autonomy. Potosí extracted labour through an imperial system that preserved Indigenous communities while subordinating them to mines and tribute. Their differences identify who owned the worker, who recruited him, which court heard his complaint, and whose wealth the labour sustained.

Capture did not begin with a ship.

In West Africa, people entering Atlantic commerce came through wars, raids, judicial enslavement, kidnapping, debt, and seizure by rulers or armed entrepreneurs. No single African market or state controlled the trade. Ouidah on the Bight of Benin, Luanda in west-central Africa, and Bonny in the Niger Delta connected different political economies to European merchants. African rulers negotiated prices, duties, and access; brokers arranged transactions; European captains waited offshore or operated through forts and factories. Firearms altered the balance of power in some regions, but African states retained their own aims and conflicts. The trade was Atlantic in reach and locally organised at every shore.

An armed group first had to secure a captive and bring that person from the interior to a ruler’s court, merchant’s compound, or coastal market. There the captive might be held for days or months and exchanged for cloth, metal goods, alcohol, guns, or ammunition. Local currencies and credit shaped the transaction. A merchant depended on suppliers expecting future deliveries and on authorities who could close a port, change a duty, or redirect trade.

At the coast, captains and surgeons examined age, sex, apparent health, and strength. Names became journal entries. A person from the interior became “a boy,” “a woman,” or “a prime man,” terms that converted biography into a category useful to a purchaser. The captain calculated how many could be carried, how much water they required, and how many provisions the voyage would consume. This was the arithmetic later displayed by the *Brookes*.

Provisioning the Middle Passage required flour, beans, rice, salted meat, water, and sometimes African food purchased near the coast. The quantities were set against a voyage whose duration remained uncertain. Wind, currents, storms, sickness, resistance, and delay changed the plan. Captains kept journals of deaths, illness, punishments, and daily distribution. The hold appeared as a managed environment because management kept a valuable cargo alive long enough to sell. People were forced on deck for exercise, washed, inspected, and sometimes fed through devices when they refused food. Maintenance and terror worked together.

When disease spread, crews separated the sick, fumigated spaces, altered rations, or discarded bodies. The sea could erase evidence, but logs registered mortality as dates and numbers. On the *Zong* in November 1781, Captain Luke Collingwood and his officers ordered 132 enslaved people thrown overboard during the voyage to Jamaica. The owners later sought payment from insurers, claiming that the deaths were a jettison of cargo made necessary by lack of water. The dispute reached London in 1783. Lord Mansfield ruled that the case concerned the policy’s terms, not the criminal prosecution of those who had killed the people. Granville Sharp’s campaign helped turn the affair into abolitionist evidence.

The Zong records show a person forced aboard ship becoming a claim governed by maritime law. Insurance represented the shipowner’s interest, the insurer assessed risk through the language of “loss,” and the court translated deliberate killing into a question about maritime peril. Mansfield’s judgment did not authorise every killing, but the hearing exposed a legal world in which human value could be argued through cargo doctrine.

Survivors were sold into plantation society. A Jamaican or Barbadian buyer inspected teeth, limbs, age, and apparent skill. The price entered the estate ledger beside cattle, tools, buildings, and imported goods. Payment might be made in cash, on credit, or through a merchant advancing funds against the next sugar harvest. If a worker died, fled, or resisted, the loss affected the estate’s accounts. If the worker had children, the owner’s claim reached the next generation.

Plantation credit connected the field to the countinghouse. London and Bristol merchants supplied clothing, iron, barrels, tools, and food on terms secured by future crops and enslaved people listed as collateral. Mortgage deeds could include land, mills, livestock, and hundreds of men, women, and children. A hurricane, rebellion, or fall in sugar prices threatened the planter and the creditor who had lent against the estate. Pressure travelled down the chain as longer hours, harsher punishment, and closer surveillance.

The Barbados code gave overseers and magistrates a framework for answering resistance. Enslaved people could be whipped, branded, mutilated, or executed; movement and gatherings were restricted; white inhabitants were drawn into patrols. The court did not stand outside the relation between master and enslaved person. Judges, jurors, constables, and legislators frequently belonged to the class that owned plantations. Law distributed coercive duties across free society and made slavery a public order.

The French Code Noir followed a related logic. Its provisions required baptism and Catholic instruction, regulated treatment, and allowed manumission under defined conditions. At the same time, it confirmed the master’s power, punished flight and assembly, and made racial status inheritable. Religious incorporation and property status were made to coexist.

Courts protected contracts as well. A merchant claiming that a planter had failed to pay for provisions could enforce the debt against the estate. A planter claiming that an enslaved person had been damaged or lost at sea could seek compensation through insurance law. When colonial officials ordered an expedition against fugitives, public funds and private interests merged. The government that punished an uprising could borrow from merchants whose security rested on plantation property.

At the final stage, commodities returned to Europe. Sugar loaded at Kingston, Bridgetown, Port Royal, or smaller harbours travelled to warehouses in London, Bristol, Nantes, Amsterdam, or Liverpool. Refiners processed it, shopkeepers retailed it, and households consumed it in tea, preserves, and medicines. A court ruling, a credit ledger, a ship’s ration list, and a plantation punishment belonged to one system because each made the next transfer possible. That chain could be interrupted. Enslaved people escaped from markets, refused food aboard ship, preserved languages and religious practices, slowed plantation work, formed Maroon communities, destroyed equipment, and rebelled. The Haitian Revolution, begun in Saint-Domingue in 1791, defeated armies, abolished slavery in the French colony, and created Haiti in 1804. Its violence was organised through plantations, towns, military units, religious networks, and changing alliances.

The chain ran in two directions. Credit, law, and shipping carried coercion toward the enslaved; resistance carried costs toward owners, insurers, governments, and consumers. A plantation could be profitable only if people worked. People worked under threat because courts and patrols backed the threat, and courts and patrols acted because the colonial state treated plantation property as a public interest. The market moved through these links, but it did not create them.

Some histories begin with demand: European consumers wanted sugar, tobacco, coffee, and cotton; merchants responded; Africa supplied labour; plantations expanded. The sequence is correct as far as it goes. Sugar prices encouraged investment, and profits encouraged more land, ships, and captives. But a price cannot compel a person to cut cane, and a contract cannot make a child inherit a parent’s enslavement unless a government defines that person as property and supplies force to defend the definition.

The Atlantic plantation political economy joined powers that reinforced one another. Land seizure gave colonists fields. Maritime power protected routes and ports. Credit supplied goods before harvest. Colonial assemblies wrote slave codes. Courts enforced mortgages and insurance claims. Militias pursued fugitives. Metropolitan governments granted monopolies, chartered companies, regulated trade, and fought rival empires. Planters used the resulting wealth to purchase offices, influence legislation, and present their interests as the interests of the colony.

Barbados makes the process unusually clear because its small size concentrated the evidence. By the late seventeenth century, sugar estates covered much of the island, and the planter assembly possessed a direct interest in plantation discipline. The 1661 code addressed individual cruelty as part of its role as a constitutional document for a slave society. It defined who could testify, who could bear arms, who could move without permission, who could be punished, and who belonged among the governed without belonging among citizens. The code made racial hierarchy a routine of policing.

Jamaica required a broader apparatus. Its plantations were scattered, its interior offered refuge, and its enslaved population repeatedly challenged colonial rule. After the First Maroon War, the treaties of 1739 and 1740 recognised Maroon communities while obliging them to return future fugitives and help suppress revolts. The colonial government used war and diplomacy together, granting limited autonomy to one armed population to preserve the subjection of another. Sugar demand alone cannot explain such a settlement. It shows a state sorting communities according to the security and labour needs of plantation society.

Potosí provides a necessary contrast. Silver connected the Andes to Spain, Mexico, China, and European finance, while the mita supplied labour to one of the early modern world’s most important mining centres. The Spanish Crown’s categories remained different: Indigenous people were subjects of the king, theoretically protected from enslavement, yet still required to provide tribute and labour. Officials defended the mita as an inherited Andean obligation or regulated public service. The result was coercion without the exact legal form of Caribbean chattel slavery. This difference has shaped debate over whether racial ideology preceded economic exploitation or developed through it. In *Capitalism and Slavery*, Eric Williams argued that profits from plantation slavery and the slave trade helped finance British industrial development, and that abolition followed changing economic interests rather than a sudden moral awakening. His account shifted attention toward balance sheets, ports, credit, and accumulation. It also presented the Atlantic economy as a connected structure rather than a collection of local cruelties.

Williams remains powerful where he places wealth and political economy at the centre, but slavery cannot be explained by profitability alone. Sugar production existed before British dominance; coerced labour had several legal forms; and planters defended slavery even when particular estates lost money. A plantation could be economically inefficient and politically indispensable. Slavery’s value lay in control of land, labour, credit, status, and colonial government, not merely in the return from a field.

Seymour Drescher’s *Econocide* challenged the idea that British emancipation followed because slavery had become unprofitable. West Indian slavery remained commercially significant when Parliament abolished the British trade in 1807 and later ended slavery in most British colonies in 1833–1834. Political mobilisation, religious dissent, slave resistance, and changing imperial priorities worked alongside profitability. The disagreement reveals that economic value becomes historical power through political institutions, while political decisions are shaped by conflicts over wealth.

The racial-law historiography adds another dimension. Barbara Fields warned against treating race as an explanation floating above historical practice. Racial ideology was produced through the social relations of slavery: people learned to understand coercive labour through racial difference, and that language then justified the system. Winthrop Jordan, Edmund Morgan, and other historians traced how colonial law, Protestant thought, labour discipline, and ideas about African ancestry converged. Europeans did not possess a timeless hatred that automatically produced plantation slavery. Lawmakers and owners made racial categories useful to the defence of property.

Virginia demonstrates the process in a setting different from Barbados. During the seventeenth century, colonial law attached African descent to perpetual servitude. Children inherited their mother’s status; conversion to Christianity did not guarantee freedom; interracial marriage was restricted; and punishment followed racial classification. These decisions responded to labour conflicts, elite fears, and the need to separate poor Europeans from enslaved Africans after events such as Bacon’s Rebellion in 1676. Race hardened through law because it solved a political problem for a ruling class.

The *Brookes* plan and Equiano’s narrative expose the same conflict from opposite directions. The diagram required the category of slave cargo to make bodies countable. Equiano’s book broke that category open with memory, geography, religious thought, and a name. Abolitionists placed testimony beside shipping data, mortality tables, diagrams, and legal cases. Numbers challenged claims of necessity; personal narratives challenged claims of African incapacity; legal arguments challenged property in persons.

The Zong case exposed the limits of moral language inside a commercial court. In March 1783, Mansfield instructed the jury that if throwing enslaved people overboard had been necessary to save the ship, the owners might recover from insurers. The first hearing did not establish a moral judgment about the killings. Insurance doctrine had defined human beings as insured property, and the court addressed the dispute in those terms. Sharp’s campaign insisted that the deaths were murder rather than ordinary maritime loss. The court’s categories contained violence until abolitionists forced them into political controversy.

The Code Noir exposed a related contradiction. Louis XIV’s ordinance of 1685 required enslaved people in French colonies to receive Catholic instruction and baptism. It regulated marriage, burial, food, and punishment, and recognised limited routes to manumission. Yet it confirmed the master’s authority and prescribed severe penalties for flight, assembly, and disobedience. The state claimed to Christianise the enslaved while preserving the conditions under which they could be beaten, sold, and inherited. Law did not merely restrain violence; it sorted violence into permitted and prohibited forms. That sorting shaped public life among free Europeans. A white sailor assaulting another white man might face prosecution; an overseer whipping an enslaved worker could claim discipline. A merchant who failed to honour a debt could be sued; an enslaved person resisting sale could be imprisoned or killed. The system taught judges, creditors, patrols, and legislators where sympathy was expected and where suffering counted as an operating cost.

The market explanation narrows further when it encounters reproduction. Children born to enslaved women increased an owner’s property without a new African purchase. The rule *partus sequitur ventrem*, established in English North American colonies during the seventeenth century, made the mother’s status decisive. This legal invention transmitted bondage through descent and converted the relation between mother and child into an economic mechanism.

Caribbean demographic conditions made the mechanism more brutal. In many sugar colonies, mortality was so high that natural increase did not sustain the enslaved population. Merchants continued importing captives because labour, disease, punishment, and malnutrition consumed lives. Barbados, Jamaica, and Saint-Domingue depended on the Atlantic trade to replenish their workforces. A market supplied people, but law made supply permanent by attaching status to descent and denying the enslaved control over family separation.

Enslaved people also built institutions their owners neither intended nor controlled. Churches, burial societies, markets, kin networks, naming practices, and fugitive communities preserved forms of life under conditions designed to prevent autonomy. Maroon settlements defended territory through arms and treaties. In Saint-Domingue, religious gatherings, plantation networks, and military organisation helped turn resistance into revolution. The plantation system organised aggression, but it never fully organised the people who endured it.

State support reached beyond the colonies. Naval wars protected or disrupted shipping lanes; diplomatic treaties opened ports; chartered companies received privileges; customs houses collected duties; and Parliament debated regulations treating the trade as a national interest. Britain’s victories over France and Spain in the eighteenth century enlarged its commercial reach, while Liverpool, Bristol, and London became linked to Caribbean plantations through finance and shipping. By permitting private violence at a distance, the empire made it strategically useful.

National wealth concealed the source of accumulation. Sugar duties filled public revenues; merchants invested profits in urban property and manufacturing; banks and insurers developed around Atlantic commerce; and politicians defended colonial interests in Westminster. Slavery did not single-handedly finance industrialisation. British industry had multiple sources, and plantation wealth varied across sectors. The Atlantic economy nevertheless widened the resources available to merchants, states, and consumers, while enslaved labour lowered commodity costs and transferred risk away from those who performed it.

A market coordinates exchange through prices, contracts, and competition. Atlantic slavery used all three, but its essential conditions were imposed through sovereignty. Armed authorities seized and sold captives. Colonial governments recognised ownership in people. Courts enforced credit claims against bodies and land. States protected trade and classified deaths within insurance law. Commodity prices mattered at every stage, but they operated inside a legal order that had already decided who could be compelled.

Historians who follow the money from Liverpool to Barbados and from Potosí to Seville identify something indispensable. The sugar plantation and the mine were enterprises dependent on investment, accounting, labour extraction, and distant consumers. Yet the ledger changes when a person appears as collateral. That entry joins economic calculation to a political decision about the body. The figure is a price because a court will help an owner control a person.

A plantation owner could borrow against enslaved people because law promised that the claim would survive movement, marriage, injury, and inheritance. A shipowner could insure a human cargo because maritime courts recognised a property interest. A magistrate could punish a runaway because the state treated unauthorised movement as theft from the master. Parliament could regulate the bodies aboard the *Brookes* because it accepted the premise that government might set conditions for transporting people who had no right to refuse. Separate offices worked toward one order. That order was never complete. In August 1791, enslaved people in northern Saint-Domingue began a rebellion that spread across plantations and destroyed the assumption that military force could secure colonial labour forever. France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1794, partly under the pressure of revolution and war; Napoleon restored it in 1802 in several territories; Haiti defeated the French expedition and declared independence in 1804. Abolition, imperial strategy, racial ideology, and enslaved resistance collided, and economic interests did not dictate one outcome.

British abolition followed another path. Parliament abolished the slave trade in 1807, but slavery continued in the colonies until the Slavery Abolition Act of 1833, with emancipation taking effect in 1834 and an apprenticeship system delaying full freedom for many until 1838. Compensation went to slaveholders, not to the people who had been enslaved. The arrangement preserved the state’s recognition of property claims even as it ended them. Plantation society survived emancipation through wages, contracts, landlessness, and imperial control.

Olaudah Equiano died in 1797 and did not see the 1807 act. His narrative belonged to the political world that made abolition thinkable. When he described the hold, the sale of children, and the contradictions of Christian Britain, he joined memory to public action. A man entered in an owner’s ledger had become an author of national judgment.

The *Brookes* plan had a double life: created to display regulation, it was used to expose cruelty. The Code Noir was created to regulate slavery and became evidence of the state’s role in maintaining it. The Zong case was heard as an insurance dispute and became abolitionist memory. Their later meanings depended on readers, campaigns, courts, and institutions reopening what earlier officials had closed.

The Atlantic world does not reveal an unchanging human appetite for aggression. It reveals what happened when maritime power, colonial seizure, racial law, plantation labour, commercial credit, and imperial rivalry were assembled across an ocean. Barbados became a sugar society through land claims, labour decisions, legislation, and investment. Jamaica’s rulers combined plantation discipline with war against Maroon communities. Potosí’s silver economy depended on another imperial labour structure. The differences are where explanation begins.

A person captured in the interior of Africa, carried through Ouidah or Luanda, confined beneath a ship, sold in Kingston, whipped in a Jamaican field, pledged to a London creditor, and recorded after death in an estate book passed through a world larger than any owner’s will. That world held together because officials and merchants repeatedly made compatible decisions. It came apart when captives resisted, sailors testified, abolitionists circulated diagrams, courts exposed their own categories, and revolutions turned property into a target.

The ledger shows value; the plan shows confinement; Equiano’s narrative restores a life; the Zong records show a court translating killing into loss; the Code Noir shows a king’s government making domination durable. Each record belongs to a different office, but each answers the same practical question: who may be harmed, and whose claim will the law protect? In the Atlantic plantation world, the answer was built into ships, statutes, credit, courts, and armies long before it was felt in any single act of violence.
