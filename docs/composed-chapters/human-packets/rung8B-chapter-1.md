## Chapter 1: What Do We Mean by Human Aggression?

Njal Thorgeirsson stood in the Law Council at the Althing with the cords of his cloak drawn tight against the wind. Behind him the booths spread across the lava, their walls of woven cloth and packed turf bright with shields, saddle blankets, and the red coats of men who had ridden from the south. Beyond them, the Law Rock rose black and uneven above the plain. On its other side stood the men who had killed Gunnar Hamundarson.

Gunnar had died at his own farm after being made an outlaw. He had stayed when he might have gone, unwilling to leave Hlíðarendi. When the attackers came, he shot one from the window and wounded another before they broke into the house. The saga says that Hallgerd refused him a lock of her hair for a bowstring, and that Gunnar’s bow failed. Njal had urged him to leave Iceland; now he had come to the Thing to bring the case for Gunnar’s sons and kindred.

The killing had been done by Starkad and his companions, men bound by marriage and interest to the families with whom Gunnar had quarrelled. They stood in the crowd near the Rock, friends around them. Starkad wore a blue cloak and carried a spear. He watched Njal without lowering his eyes.

When the Lawspeaker called the court to order, Njal moved forward. Men pressed close behind him, some standing on stones to see. Someone held Gunnar’s shield, split and blackened at the rim, though the saga does not say whether it had been brought as proof or only as a token. The cases were spoken aloud in their turn: disputes over grazing, debts, injuries, and killings. Each required witnesses, a lawful summons, and a place at the proper court. A wrong word could break a case as surely as a broken bow.

Njal named Gunnar. He told the court that Starkad and the others had gone to Hlíðarendi with hostile intent, entered the house, and taken Gunnar’s life. He set the suit against them and called for the judgment due under the law. His voice was not loud. Men nearest him leaned together to catch it, then repeated the words until the charge had passed through the crowd.

Starkad’s friends answered that Gunnar had been outlawed and that the men who went against him had acted within their rights. They spoke of the outlawry first and of the dead man afterward. Gunnar had refused to leave Iceland, they said. He had chosen to remain. The killing was therefore not murder but the enforcement of a sentence.

Behind Njal, Gunnar’s kindred shifted. His brother Kolskegg stood among them, broad-shouldered and silent. Gunnar’s sons were old enough to understand the names being used for their father and young enough to watch the men who had used them. One had a hand on the rim of a shield.

Njal asked the court to hear witnesses to the attack. Men came forward and gave their names. They had seen the party ride to Hlíðarendi and the house surrounded. They had heard the fighting within. One spoke of the arrow Gunnar had shot through the wall; another told how the attackers had struck at the roof and doors. The facts were laid down one beside another, not as a story but as pieces required to make a case stand.

The court listened. The Lawspeaker sat at the Rock, his knees drawn up beneath his cloak. Sun showed on the helmets of men gathered above him. Horses moved among the booths, and from farther down the slope came the smell of boiled meat and damp wool. A raven lifted from the stones, flew low over the assembly, and settled on a tent rope.

Njal then asked for a settlement. He named the sum that should be paid for Gunnar’s life and required guarantees from the men who had taken part in the killing. The opposing kin refused. Their refusal passed from mouth to mouth, and the men behind Starkad closed ranks. No one drew a weapon. The law permitted the pressure to gather without yet becoming a fight.

Gizur the White came through the crowd. An old ally of Njal’s and a powerful man at the Thing, he spoke privately with the parties, first with Njal, then with Starkad and his supporters. The men stood in a half-circle around him. He reminded them that Gunnar was dead, that the suit was properly brought, and that a feud could be carried farther than any man intended.

The answer was still refusal.

Njal turned towards the Rock. The court remained in session, and the case stayed before it. He raised his hand, and the witnesses stepped forward again as the judgment was called. The names of the accused were spoken. The suit was entered. Then Gunnar’s family went back through the booths, carrying his shield between them.

The shield went back through the booths, but the case did not end there. At Towton, six centuries later, the dead were brought before another kind of court: not one that could impose compensation or outlawry, but a trench, a laboratory, and a public audience asked to decide what the bones meant.

In March 2011, archaeologists working near the village of Towton in North Yorkshire reopened the question of the mass grave discovered during building work in 1996. The grave lay beside the medieval church, in ground associated with the aftermath of the battle fought on 29 March 1461 between the houses of York and Lancaster. A mechanical excavator had exposed human remains in a pit, and the first investigation recovered the skeletons of at least thirty-eight men. Their bodies had been placed together, some apparently without the ordinary care given to a parish burial. Without turning the pit into a battlefield photograph, the later work made the objects and bones available for a more exact account.

The skeletons were male, mostly young adults, with a smaller number of adolescents. Their bones carried wounds made by swords, knives, arrows, and other edged weapons. Several injuries had healed, showing that some of the men had survived earlier violence before dying at Towton. Others had been struck repeatedly around the head. One skull had a circular opening associated with a powerful blow; another showed cuts that had entered the bone at different angles. Damage was concentrated in the upper body, especially the head, rather than spread evenly across the skeleton. That pattern is consistent with close fighting in which an exposed or fallen man was attacked from above.

The names of the men are gone. The bones preserve the force and direction of particular blows. A cut across the back of a skull suggests that its bearer was turning or fleeing; a wound to the face indicates that an assailant stood in front of him; a puncture in a rib may have been made by a narrow weapon entering the chest. These are observations about bone, not sentences spoken by a witness. The archaeologist records the shape of the break, the presence of remodelling around an old wound, the condition of the surface, and the position in which each skeleton was found. Reconstruction begins only after those observations have been made.

Towton was a place where armed men were brought together by competing claims to the English crown. Edward, Duke of York, met the Lancastrian army in snow and wind on a field north of the village. The battle followed years of political conflict, shifting allegiance, imprisonment, and earlier fighting. Men struck one another at close quarters within a campaign organised by magnates and supplied by households, estates, towns, and royal claims. Their wounds were personal in the moment. The encounter that produced them was not.

Contemporary accounts describe Towton as exceptionally bloody, and later chroniclers gave casualty figures in the tens of thousands. The numbers vary sharply. The Burgundian chronicler Jean de Wavrin reported that the battle lasted for much of the day and that the dead were numerous; the English chronicler William Gregory supplied a figure of 28,000, a number often repeated. Archaeology cannot turn those reports into a complete count. It can show that at least some of the dead suffered severe sharp-force trauma, that several had survived earlier injuries, and that the bodies were gathered and buried together after the battle.

The public interpretation in 2011 drew these findings into a story of the battle’s violence. Visitors were shown that the skeletons belonged not to an abstract medieval population but to men who had endured distinct attacks. A healed fracture spoke of an earlier episode; a fresh cut marked the last one. The grave was treated as evidence for the intensity of Towton, while the surrounding landscape and written accounts supplied its political setting. The interpretation worked because each kind of evidence answered a different question. The bones indicated what happened to bodies, the chronicles described what people believed had happened, and the landscape located the fighting within a settlement that had to manage the dead. Yet aggression covers more than one act. An archer loosing at an advancing line, a man striking a fallen enemy, a commander ordering a position held, and a lord requiring service from a tenant belong to the same history without being identical behaviours. The skeletons disclose lethal violence, but they do not by themselves tell us whether a combatant fought willingly, feared punishment, sought payment, defended a household, or believed the Yorkist or Lancastrian cause just. The causes of a blow cannot be read directly from the fracture it leaves.

A burial can make violence visible by concentrating its remains. It can also make one kind of violence easier to see than another. Towton offers no comparable record of threats, hunger, forced recruitment, sexual coercion, or the destruction of homes beyond what other sources preserve. The bones are eloquent about weapons. They are silent about the arrangements that placed weapons in men’s hands. That silence is where historical method begins. An injury is not yet a rate, a battle is not yet a society, and a grave is not yet a measure of human nature.

Aggression is behaviour intended to harm another person who does not wish to be harmed. It may be physical, as at Towton, or psychological, as in a credible threat intended to make someone submit. Anger is an emotional state—irritation, fear, humiliation, or rage—not the behaviour itself. Violence normally refers to physical force, so a punch is both violent and aggressive while an insult may be aggressive without being violent. A soldier who fires because he has been ordered to may commit an aggressive act without feeling anger.

Intent complicates the definition. Someone may shove another in a crowded doorway without wishing to injure him. A hunter may kill an animal without committing interpersonal aggression. A magistrate may order a whipping as lawful punishment, while the victim experiences the force as coercion. The same movement carries different meanings according to its object, setting, and recognised purpose. Historical records often identify the act more readily than the intention. A coroners’ roll might record that a man died after being struck with a staff without preserving whether the blow began as an assault, an attempt at restraint, or a quarrel that escalated.

Interpersonal violence occurs between individuals or within relationships, including domestic assault, homicide, sexual violence, and threats used to secure obedience. Organised violence is carried out by a group whose members are recruited, directed, supplied, or disciplined for that purpose. Raiding usually involves a swift attack to seize goods or captives, followed by withdrawal; warfare involves sustained conflict between organised groups, often with plans to hold ground or defeat an opposing force. A raid can form part of a war, and a war can be fought through thousands of individual acts.

Coercive law adds another layer. A statute, court, prison, tax demand, or threat of confiscation can compel conduct by making refusal costly. The coercive power of a state becomes especially important when private acts are classified, investigated, and punished. The Laws of Manu, compiled in India probably between the second century BCE and the third century CE, assigns the king responsibility for punishment and describes social order through ranked duties and penalties. In George Bühler’s translation, the ruler is told that punishment protects the world and that a king who fails to punish those deserving punishment brings disorder upon himself and his realm. That text sorts force into categories while also praising it. The king’s punishment is represented as public duty; a lower person’s retaliation may be treated as disorder; a breach of caste discipline is placed within a hierarchy of penalties. A private blow and a royal sentence can both cause pain, but the text gives them different political statuses. The compiler describes the authority he wants a king, judge, and subject to recognise, without recording every assault in India. His document shows how aggression becomes legible when a society names the person entitled to use force and the person entitled to receive it.

Historians move from an act to a pattern through classification. In skeletal analysis, trauma is commonly sorted by its timing. Antemortem trauma occurred before death and may have healed. Perimortem trauma occurred around the time of death, when bone still had some elasticity. Postmortem damage occurred after death through burial pressure, excavation, animals, tools, or later disturbance. The distinction is technical but consequential: a cut made in battle and a crack made by a twentieth-century machine may occupy the same skull while belonging to entirely different histories.

Researchers also classify the form of injury. Blunt-force trauma produces crushing, depression, or fracture; sharp-force trauma leaves cut marks or narrow wounds; projectile trauma may produce an entry point or distinctive fragmentation. At Towton, the concentration of sharp-force injuries to the skulls supports a reconstruction of violent close combat, especially when the marks occur with other signs of fresh injury. It does not require every wound to have been made by a sword, nor establish that every dead man was killed on the field rather than during pursuit or after capture.

From individual bones the analyst calculates prevalence. Prevalence is the proportion of the observed group showing a particular condition, such as evidence of trauma. Incidence is the number of new cases occurring within a defined population during a defined period. A medieval cemetery may permit an estimate of trauma prevalence among the excavated dead, but rarely a true incidence rate, because the living population, period of exposure, and total number of deaths are uncertain. Confusing the two makes a graveyard appear to report a society’s annual violence when it reports only the remains selected by burial and survival.

Casualty counts have a similar problem. If a chronicler says that 28,000 men died at Towton, the historian must ask who supplied the number, when it was written, what purpose it served, and whether it was copied from an earlier account. A large number may express horror, praise a victory, or lend grandeur to a ruler’s cause. Muster rolls, prisoner lists, tax records, parish burials, and archaeology can test such a claim, but they do not count the same population. A battlefield estimate counts the dead attributed to an event; a burial register counts people entered by a clerk. Their denominators differ before their totals are compared.

Court records create another route from acts to patterns. In medieval England, the coroner investigated sudden and suspicious deaths on behalf of the Crown. The resulting rolls might name the dead person, the suspected killer, the place, the weapon, the witnesses, and the verdict. A roll from Warwickshire could describe a man killed in a village street with a staff or knife, while a London inquest might identify a servant found dead near a river and list the people last seen with him. Such entries provide dates, names, and circumstances that archaeology often lacks. They also reflect procedure. The coroner’s jury was called because a death had entered a legal category requiring inquiry. A quarrel in which nobody died might leave no comparable trace. A death concealed from officials might be absent. A killing committed by a powerful person could be transformed by witnesses, negotiated away, or recorded under another description. The roll is evidence both of violence and of a system’s decision to count one death as a public matter.

A historian reads the record in steps. First comes the literal entry: who died, where, when, and by what stated means. Next comes comparison with other entries from the same jurisdiction and period. Then the historian estimates how much of the population and how many kinds of event the record covers. Only afterward can the record contribute to a claim about homicide, the social position of victims, or the role of weapons. A list of killings may show that knives were common in recorded assaults; it cannot by itself show that knives caused most assaults in the whole population. The same discipline applies when a source describes a society elsewhere. Medieval English coroners’ rolls and early modern reports from the highlands of New Guinea may both contain accounts of people killed by other people, but they were made for different purposes and under different conditions. A comparison that ignores those conditions produces a false similarity.

In an English coroners’ roll, the death usually enters through an official summons. The record names a village, manor, road, alehouse, or field. It may identify a knife or staff and state that the accused fled or was arrested. The entry belongs to a legal process in which homicide is classified against the king’s peace. Its details are shaped by the questions asked by the coroner and the answers given by a jury.

Early European visitors and later anthropologists reporting from New Guinea encountered conflict organised around villages, clans, marriage alliances, compensation, revenge, and the taking of captives. Reports from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries describe ambushes, raids, defended settlements, and cycles of retaliation, but outsiders wrote them, their presence altered what they observed, and their categories did not always match local ones. A report of “war” might combine a planned attack with a feud; a statement that a village was peaceful might mean that no fighting occurred during the observer’s stay.

The English record makes an individual killing unusually precise because a royal officer required an inquiry. The New Guinea report may make collective conflict more visible because an outsider noticed a raid or public ceremony while domestic assaults and disputes remained outside the account. England may therefore appear more homicidal in one dataset and New Guinea more warlike in another, although the difference partly reflects the path by which each event reached paper.

The cases still differ in important ways. The English roll places killing within a territorial jurisdiction that claimed authority over roads, households, and bodies. The New Guinea account more often describes violence negotiated among groups whose political relationships were distributed through kinship, exchange, and local leadership rather than concentrated in a single court. That contrast changes who could authorise force, who could demand compensation, and who had the power to preserve an account.

Recorded violence and lived violence are not the same quantity. A widow in a Warwickshire inquest, a clan elder arranging compensation in the New Guinea highlands, and a man buried after Towton all encountered harm, but the surviving documents make their experiences commensurable only in limited respects. We can compare weapons, victims, settings, and outcomes when the evidence permits. We should not turn unlike records into a universal ranking of societies.

The open question remains stubborn because preservation is itself historical. A society with many surviving legal rolls may look more violent than one whose disputes were settled orally. A battlefield with deep, protected deposits may appear bloodier than a dispersed conflict whose dead were buried in ordinary graves. The number of recorded homicides depends on the population at risk, the years covered, the jurisdiction’s reach, habits of reporting, and the survival of documents. Without those denominators, a total floats free of the people it is supposed to describe.

Towton’s thirty-eight skeletons cannot measure the violence of fifteenth-century England, just as a run of English inquests cannot measure every quarrel, beating, or threat. Yet neither should be dismissed as merely incomplete. The bones establish injuries; the rolls establish cases; the reports establish observations made under particular conditions. Greater recorded violence may mean more violence, better preservation, or a state more determined to name and prosecute it. Which answer fits depends on who counted, whom they counted, and what power stood behind the count.
