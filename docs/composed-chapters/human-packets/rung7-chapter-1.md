## Chapter 1: What Do We Mean by Human Aggression?

> “The Reform Movement was agitating the whole country at this period, and political excitement led to political riots, burning of buildings, and general orgies of massacre and destruction.”
>
> — Women's suffrage: a short history of a great movement, *Women's suffrage: a short history of a great movement/Chapter 1*

Henry Hunt stood on a chair in St Peter’s Field at noon on Monday, 16 August 1819, holding his hat in one hand. Beside him, a white hat hung from a pole. The open ground between Mount Street and Peter Street was filled with men in Sunday coats, women in bonnets and shawls, and children raised onto shoulders. Above them, banners moved in the heat: REFORM, UNIVERSAL SUFFRAGE, ANNUAL PARLIAMENTS. From the edge of the field came the clatter of hooves and the shouted orders of men trying to make a passage through people who had walked in from Manchester, Oldham, Saddleworth, Middleton and Stockport.

Hunt raised his arm. Those near the hustings saw him clearly; those farther away watched the movement and waited for the signal to pass through the crowd. The meeting had been called to demand parliamentary reform, and its organisers had arranged it with the care of a procession. Families walked together. Bands had played on the roads into town. The women of the Manchester Female Reform Society had come in their own ranks, wearing white ribbons and carrying a banner. Mary Fildes, the society’s president, stood near the hustings with her husband, John, and their child.

Since morning, the magistrates had been gathered in a house on Mount Street. From its windows they looked down towards the field and across the roofs beyond it. William Hulton, chairman of the local magistrates, watched the meeting as it grew. He sent for the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry, part-time cavalry drawn largely from shopkeepers, clerks and manufacturers in the district. The men waited in Byrom Street with sabres at their sides, their horses restless in the heat.

At about half past one, the magistrates signed the order for Hunt and the other speakers to be arrested. Joseph Nadin, the deputy constable, entered the field with a body of special constables. The crowd opened unevenly before them. Hunt saw hats and staves moving towards the hustings and told the people to stand firm and keep order. On the platform, Samuel Bamford of Middleton heard someone call for the cavalry.

The yeomanry came first. They entered from the south in a confused line. Officers waved their swords while the horses slipped and turned among the bodies packed around the platform. Captain Hugh Birley shouted for a way through. Those closest to the hustings backed into one another. Some raised their hands. Some cheered, believing that the cavalry had come to protect the meeting. The mounted men forced themselves into the crowd, striking with the flats and edges of their sabres. One man fell beneath a horse. Another climbed onto the hustings and seized at a bridle.

Mary Fildes was thrown down, and her banner went over.

The Manchester magistrates’ report said that the meeting had become dangerous and that the cavalry had acted to preserve the peace. The Times, describing the event afterwards, reported that the military had moved upon the people and that many had been cut and trampled. Witnesses spoke of men riding with drawn swords through a crowd that had nowhere to run. The account later printed in Women’s Suffrage: a Short History of a Great Movement stated that women as well as men had been ridden down by the cavalry.

Hunt remained on the platform until the soldiers reached it. He took off his hat, bowed towards the officers, and gave himself up. John Saxton, editor of the Manchester Observer, was arrested beside him. The banners collapsed into the crowd. A drummer continued to beat after the musicians had scattered, and the sound seemed to come from below the horses.

Richard Carlile, standing near the platform, saw the yeomanry cut at the people and tried to pull a woman clear. William Jolliffe, a cavalry officer, later said that he had seen no deliberate slaughter. The dead and injured lay across the field in the tracks left by the horses. John Lees of Oldham, who had fought at Waterloo, was carried away with a sabre wound and died on 9 September. William Dawson, a labourer, was struck down in the press. Mary Warren, a married woman from Manchester, was killed. The precise number wounded was never settled.

By two o’clock, St Peter’s Field was nearly empty. The cavalry had pushed the crowd towards Peter Street and the surrounding lanes. People carried the injured into houses and public houses; others ran with blood on their clothes. The banners had been torn down. Hunt was taken away under guard, and the arrested speakers followed him through the town.

The last people were driven from St Peter’s Field. The yeomanry remained among the broken sticks, trampled hats and fallen flags.

The order that sent the cavalry there had passed from the magistrates’ house to Joseph Nadin, from Nadin to the special constables, and from the constables into the bodies of men and women who had come carrying banners. The sabres belonged to individual riders. The decision to put riders among the crowd belonged to an office. At Peterloo, some cavalrymen may have felt frightened or insulted, but the order did not require each rider to hate the people he struck. The force of the attack came from the authority that assembled the men, armed them, and directed them into the field.

At the East London hearing of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Nomonde Calata was asked to speak about the killing of her husband, Fort Calata. The hearing took place in 1996, in a country still governed by the memory of the apartheid state and by the recent work of its security police. Her husband had been one of the four men later known as the Cradock Four. On 27 June 1985, Matthew Goniwe, Fort Calata, Sparrow Mkhonto, and Sicelo Mhlauli disappeared while travelling from Cradock towards Port Elizabeth. Their burned bodies were found near the coast the next day. The Commission examined how a government had made the deaths of four men possible and who had given that possibility a legal and administrative shape.

Calata sat beside other witnesses and faced the commissioners. The record identifies her as a widow, a mother, and a resident of Lingelihle township in Cradock. She described the pressure that had gathered around her family before the killing: surveillance, intimidation, the presence of police, and the political work that had made her husband a target. Fort Calata had been a lawyer’s clerk and an organiser in the United Democratic Front. Goniwe, a teacher and civic leader, had been removed from his post and placed under restrictions after leading opposition to the local council. Their work was public. The state’s interest in stopping it was public too, though it was expressed through offices, reports and men who came to the house.

The testimony moved between the ordinary and the bureaucratic. Calata spoke of visits to the house and of men asking questions. She described the fear that settled over the family when Fort was taken away. The Commission heard evidence about security officers, informers and surveillance reports. It also heard about meetings in which opponents of apartheid were discussed as threats to be neutralised. The language of the state reduced people to categories: unrest, intimidation, black-on-black violence, terrorist influence. Calata’s account returned them to names and relationships. A husband left the house. A child waited. A family learned that the government had not merely failed to protect its citizens but had treated some citizens as obstacles to be removed.

When she began to weep, the hearing changed. The commissioners allowed her time. Her cry, caught by television cameras, became one of the most recognisable sounds of the Commission. It was not an account in the narrow sense, because no sequence of names and dates can contain the instant in which a person understands that the institution before which she is speaking has helped produce the loss she is describing. The cry followed testimony about police power, political surveillance and the state’s treatment of opposition. Grief was present, and so was accusation.

The Commission’s work depended on holding those together. It collected individual testimony while examining the institutions that had made violence durable: the South African Police, the security branches, the military, local administrations and the political leadership that authorised emergency rule. Fort Calata was selected through a system that watched, classified, detained and passed information upward. Men carried out the physical act, but the killing acquired its reach from offices, files, vehicles and commands.

Anger and aggression separate most clearly when one is present without the other. Anger is an internal feeling of displeasure or antagonism; aggression is behaviour directed towards causing harm to someone who wishes to avoid it. A woman may feel rage and restrain herself. A clerk may sign an order without visible anger. The distinction is visible at Peterloo, where the order that brought cavalry into St Peter’s Field did not depend on each rider sharing the same emotion. It is visible at East London, where the security apparatus did not require every policeman to hate the people he helped classify and pursue. It required obedience, classification and the protection of official secrecy.

Interpersonal violence, raiding, warfare, coercive law and organised atrocity belong to one history of force, but they are not interchangeable. A husband who beats his wife and a government that disappears opponents both use force or the threat of force. The identity of the target, the authority behind the act, the resources available to the perpetrator and the response expected from witnesses change what the act does. Raiding is swift and mobile, aimed at goods, captives or disruption before the attackers withdraw. Warfare is organised lethal conflict between autonomous groups. Coercive law compels conduct through a threat backed by an office. Organised atrocity turns classification into a method of killing civilians.

The state deserves special attention because its offices police the state’s own subjects. A feud may kill two families. A magistracy, police department or military command can mark thousands of families as suspect and give armed men authority to enter their homes. The files created by those offices help decide where force will be applied, to whom, and under what name. The records of the apartheid security state make this visible because the surviving testimony and commissions of inquiry preserve both the human loss and the vocabulary used to justify it.

A burial preserves a different kind of decision. At Talheim, in what is now Baden-Württemberg, a pit held the remains of at least thirty-four people killed about 5000 BCE. The grave was found in 1983 near the village of Talheim and studied through the bones, soil and arrangement. Men, women and children lay together. Most had suffered blows to the back or side of the skull. The wounds were made by hard implements, probably stone axes or adzes, and many were fatal. The bodies had not been placed in an orderly cemetery. They had been thrown together in a shallow pit and covered.

The skulls are the central evidence. Blunt-force trauma appears repeatedly at the rear and left side of the cranium, locations expected when victims are struck from behind or while trying to flee. The injuries do not resemble one accidental collapse or a single duel. They record repeated impacts inflicted on several people, including children. The assemblage has been interpreted as the remains of a small farming community or related group attacked and killed in one episode. The perpetrators are absent from the pit. Their purpose is absent too.

Talheim belongs to the early Neolithic period, when farming communities had been established in central Europe for generations. People cultivated cereals, kept cattle and pigs, built houses, stored food and maintained connections across settlements. The grave resists the image of agricultural life as the simple arrival of peace. Farming altered the objects and places around which people lived. Land, harvests, animals and stored grain could be defended or seized. A community with fixed fields had something different to lose from a mobile band, and a community with houses and graves could be attacked in a way that left an archaeological signature.

The bones show that the dead were not merely exposed to danger. Someone used a weapon against them at close range. A person struck once in a fight and a person struck repeatedly after being surrounded may share a category in a database, but their situations were not the same. At Talheim, the injuries suggest an event in which victims were controlled long enough for attackers to kill many of them. The concentration of trauma around the head implies deliberate targeting. The pit then concealed the bodies or removed them from the inhabited landscape.

Who were they? The grave’s population has invited attempts to answer through age, sex, isotope analysis and genetic evidence. The victims appear to have included people connected by kinship or residence, although the precise social group remains debated. Some have proposed that the attackers came from another settlement and that the episode belonged to a conflict over land or resources. Others have warned against turning a pattern of wounds into a complete narrative of invasion. The evidence supports a violent collective event. It does not identify the speech made before it, the grievance carried into it, or the person who gave the first order.

Within that boundary, the grave’s arrangement shows choices. The bodies were brought together. If the dead were first left where they fell, they were later gathered into one place. The attackers possessed tools capable of breaking bone, knew how to use them, and acted with enough coordination to kill people of different ages. The grave records violence as a social act even though it cannot preserve a written command.

Thirty-four deaths in one pit require a larger account than a quarrel, an accident or a single ambush. The victims may have been attacked at a settlement, on a road or during an attempt to escape. Their killers may have wanted cattle, land or revenge. Each possibility connects the blows to a social world, but none can be selected merely because it fits a familiar story about prehistoric violence.

A healed fracture found elsewhere in the Neolithic record carries a different temporal mark. It says that a person survived an earlier injury and lived long enough for bone to mend. Talheim’s lethal wounds say that survival did not occur for those struck in the pit. Neither mark supplies a universal measure of aggression. One community may sustain many nonfatal assaults and bury its dead separately; another may experience a single attack that kills nearly everyone present. The soil preserves outcomes, not a scale on which all societies can be placed.

The Talheim grave also separates weapons from the institutions that direct them. A stone axe is an implement used for woodworking, but it can split a skull. Its presence does not prove a warrior class, a standing army or a political centre. The weapon became part of organised violence because people acted together and because a group had decided that another group could be harmed. The axe is evidence of capacity. The grave’s arrangement is evidence of action. Neither identifies the authority that ordered the attack.

Written records make that decision easier to see, though never neutrally. The medieval London eyre rolls were produced during royal judicial visits to the city and county. They collected pleas, accusations, presentments and judgments under the authority of the crown. Homicide appears beside appeals, theft and disputes over jurisdiction. A killing that might once have remained within a household or neighbourhood was translated into a legal case with named parties, sworn jurors, fees, warrants and punishments.

Napoleon Chagnon’s published accounts of Yanomami communities present a different record of conflict. Working among villages in the Amazon from the 1960s onward, Chagnon described raids, club fights, accusations of sorcery, deaths, marriage alliances and the political standing of men who had participated in killing. His accounts made violence central to arguments about social organisation and reproductive success. The cases were gathered through field observation, interviews, genealogies and reports from participants whose interests shaped what they told him.

London’s rolls and Chagnon’s cases both turn violence into information, but they do so through different observers. The eyre clerk asks what happened under the categories of royal law. The Yanomami ethnographer asks who fought, why a village divided and how a history of conflict affected marriage and authority. In London, the document’s immediate purpose is jurisdiction. In Chagnon’s work, the purpose is ethnographic explanation. One case is carried into an office; the other passes through conversation, observation and publication.

A London roll might name a body found in a lane, the person accused and the jurors who presented the matter before recording the judgment. That form makes the crown visible even where the accused are not powerful. The record does not merely count blows. It places a death inside a hierarchy that claims the right to investigate and punish. The violence is interpersonal at the moment of injury and institutional when the killing becomes a matter of royal jurisdiction.

Chagnon’s village conflicts show a different distribution of power. A raid could be organised by men from one settlement against another, with captives, women and revenge shaping its objective. The authority to fight was dispersed among kin groups and leaders rather than concentrated in a royal court. Prestige could attach to a killer, while obligations created by marriage and descent could draw communities towards retaliation or alliance. Violence entered political life without passing through a state archive. Neither record represents all violence in medieval London or among Yanomami communities. The rolls preserve what reached royal law; Chagnon preserved what could be learned in particular villages through a particular observer. The difference between them is the evidence. London’s violence was increasingly made legible through a central court that claimed jurisdiction over local killing. Yanomami conflict, as Chagnon described it, was organised through relations among villages, kin, marriage and reputation. The first record reveals the expansion of a judicial office. The second reveals conflict distributed through social ties that could provoke retaliation and also restrain it. The same act can occupy different historical categories. A killing in a London lane might be prosecuted as felony. A death in a village feud might demand compensation, retaliation or a new marriage settlement. A raid might be condemned by one community and praised by another. Classification is not decoration placed on violence afterwards. It determines who may answer for it, who may profit from it, and whether the dead are remembered as victims, enemies, criminals, ancestors or examples.

What disappears when the record is thin? The missing settlement records of prehistoric Anatolia leave the question sharpened rather than settled. Houses, walls, burials, storage pits and broken tools survive unevenly across the Neolithic landscape, but the arguments that joined one settlement to another are gone. A fortified wall may indicate fear, property, ceremony or a practical response to animals and weather. A burned house may record accident, abandonment, assault or ritual closure. The absence of weapons proves little where wood, hide and bone have decayed.

Documentary silence is closer to home and no less difficult. Domestic violence often entered archives only when a neighbour complained, a church court intervened, a hospital treated an injury or a police officer wrote a report. Many households had no reason to believe that an institution would protect the injured person. Others knew that reporting could bring eviction, imprisonment, shame or the loss of children. The quiet archive may contain a history of unrecorded harm, but it cannot be converted into a precise number by imagination.

The unanswered question concerns how much of violence’s shape was determined by the conditions under which anyone was permitted to record it. A skull, a court roll and a widow’s testimony each preserve a decision, but they preserve different decisions by different hands. The history of aggression begins by keeping those differences in view.
