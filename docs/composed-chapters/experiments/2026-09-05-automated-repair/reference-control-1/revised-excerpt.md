## Chapter 6: Frontiers, Faiths, and Political Fragmentation

The catalogue begins with shores, roads, walls, and harbours. Lindisfarne, an island monastery off the coast of Northumbria, entered the written record of Viking violence through the attack traditionally dated to 8 June 793. Alcuin of York, writing soon afterward, treated the assault as a calamity striking a Christian community, while the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* preserved the terse memory of raiders arriving after ominous signs in the sky. The monastery’s wealth was portable: vessels, precious fittings, books, and people could be seized and carried away. Its isolation reduced the number of defenders and increased the value of surprise. Men who had crossed the sea in vessels designed for speed, shallow water, and return could reach a place chosen for prayer and turn it, in a morning, into a source of captives and objects.

Viking raiding required more than a ship at the water’s edge. Crews had to be assembled, provisions secured, coasts remembered, and the gains divided when the ship returned. Agriculture set limits to the season, while tides and weather shaped the journey. A leader who brought back silver, captives, or ecclesiastical treasures could strengthen his household and attract new followers. The same maritime skill that carried a war band to Lindisfarne could carry traders to a market, settlers to a river valley, or envoys to a king. The ship widened the field of action; political relationships determined what its cargo meant.

Across the Sahara, protection gathered around a different geometry. A caravan leaving Sijilmasa or crossing toward the lands south of the desert depended first on wells and guides, then on agreements that kept travellers moving together when the horizon offered few fixed points. Maghrebi merchants and desert communities developed practices of escort, payment, warning, and retaliation around these routes. Protection could be claimed by a lineage, a local ruler, a confederation, or a group controlling access to water. A caravan’s safety rested on terms that could be renewed, broken, or enforced through the threat of violence.

Camels carried salt, cloth, metals, and other goods in trains whose length and composition changed with the route and the political climate. At Taghaza, where salt extraction supported long-distance exchange, the resource linked labourers, merchants, and armed protectors to places far beyond the mines. A convoy did not move through an empty wilderness. It passed among communities that knew the wells, the tracks, and the value of delay. A raiding party might seek animals and merchandise; a protector might demand payment for preventing that raid; a ruler might present the collection of dues as legitimate passage. The road’s commercial purpose gave armed men something to seize and gave them an argument for being paid.

Along the eastern Roman frontier, Byzantine writers used the term *akritai* for men associated with the borderlands, though the word gathered different social realities into one literary figure. In the tenth-century poems concerning Digenes Akritas, the frontier hero appears as a warrior of mixed cultural inheritance, defending the Christian Roman border while inhabiting a world of intermarriage, local rivalries, raids, and negotiated loyalties. The poem is not a payroll or census. Its value lies in showing how the frontier could be imagined: neither a clean line between two peoples nor a zone in which settled law simply ended, but a place where military service, landholding, kinship, and religious identity pressed against one another.

The Byzantine system relied on fortified places, military districts, tax obligations, local landowners, and soldiers whose service was tied to the survival of villages as well as to imperial command. At Amorion and along the Taurus approaches, walls and passes gave geography a political shape. Garrisons watched roads, messengers carried warnings, and inhabitants could be moved, recruited, taxed, or abandoned according to the fortunes of war. Arab and Byzantine forces raided across the frontier for captives, livestock, and prestige; emperors and emirs also made truces, exchanged prisoners, and arranged boundaries of use. Defense combined force with information and accommodation because the frontier could not be held by walls alone.

The ports of the Indian Ocean completed the catalogue by making rivalry mobile without requiring armies to occupy a continuous frontier. Kilwa on the East African coast, Aden at the entrance to the Red Sea, Hormuz in the Persian Gulf, and Calicut on the Malabar coast each depended on ships, pilots, customs, warehouses, credit, and rulers able to turn maritime position into revenue. Merchants carried textiles, spices, ivory, gold, horses, and other goods between regions whose political calendars did not coincide. A port’s prosperity could attract protection from a sultan and predation from a competitor.

When the Portuguese fleet commanded by Vasco da Gama reached the Malabar coast in 1498, Calicut was already a major commercial centre under the authority of the Zamorin. The Portuguese sought access to the spice trade, but they also brought armed ships and a willingness to make commerce answer to cannon. The conflict that followed involved Muslim merchants, Hindu rulers, Portuguese captains, and competing ports, each pursuing advantage through embargo, alliance, naval attack, or control of anchorage. A harbour was therefore a commercial site and a military instrument. Whoever controlled its customs house or anchorage could decide who was allowed to trade.

A raider and a soldier are often placed at opposite ends of a historical story. The raider is imagined as mobile, opportunistic, and socially loose; the soldier as settled, disciplined, and attached to a state. The cases resist that arrangement. Viking seasonal raiding needed organisation before the ship left shore and after it returned. Byzantine warfare depended on settlements that could feed, inform, and shelter fighters. Sahelian raiding operated within commercial systems that also required recognised protectors. Mobile raiding and settled warfare were connected positions within economies of force, not opposite social types.

The settlement of Vikings in places such as Dublin and York altered the relation between raid and rule. A town with a market and harbour could become a base from which raids were launched, a place where captives and goods were exchanged, and a centre that required defences of its own. The winter camp described in accounts of the Great Heathen Army in England was not simply a fleet pulled onto a beach. It was a place of storage, negotiation, recruitment, and political pressure. Men who raided could become rulers, while rulers could continue to raid. Residence did not end mobility; it gave mobility a more durable political base.

Byzantine frontier settlements show the reverse movement. People living near the border were not merely passive victims of armies crossing their fields. Villages, estates, forts, and market towns supplied grain, animals, labour, and intelligence. A raid could carry off inhabitants, but it could also be answered by a counter-raid from the same frontier. Military service gave some men standing and land while exposing their households to retaliation. The *akritai* of literature condensed these conditions into the figure of a heroic defender, but the practical border included farmers, tax collectors, local magnates, clergy, captives, and families divided by allegiance.

The frontier was inhabited because it was productive and strategically necessary, not because the state had solved the problem of danger. Byzantine emperors could reinforce a pass or rebuild a fort, but a defended road also directed movement and made the surrounding villages valuable to an enemy. A settlement might receive protection from Constantinople and still suffer when an army needed supplies. The fort and the farm belonged to one system, and that system distributed protection and exposure unevenly among the people who sustained it.

Sahelian caravan raiding makes the same relation visible under conditions that older European categories often obscure. A desert raider did not operate outside commerce as its pure enemy. The raider understood the value of the route because the route carried wealth. A group that attacked caravans could later bargain for protection, settle near a water source, or use political alliances to make its demands regular. Merchants, pastoralists, rulers, and armed escorts could change places across a lifetime. Their conduct depended on access to animals, wells, information, and patrons.

Accounts of trans-Saharan trade often emphasise exchange and the spread of Islam, while accounts of raiding emphasise predation. Neither alone describes the route from Sijilmasa toward the Niger bend. Payment for safe passage could be understood as a customary due by one party and as extortion by another. A ruler who punished raiders might be defending merchants, asserting control over a tax base, or competing with a neighbouring ruler for the same traffic. Violence entered the price of movement through political decisions about who could charge, who could protect, and whose claim would be enforced.

The temptation to divide societies into raiding peoples and peaceful peoples comes partly from the language of sources. A monastery records an attack; a court chronicle records a campaign; a merchant describes a dangerous road. Each record selects a crisis and gives it a moral vocabulary. The Viking is made barbarous, the frontier warrior loyal, the caravan protector necessary, and the port ruler commercial or tyrannical according to the author’s position. Those judgements are evidence of political meaning, but they are not neutral descriptions of whole populations.

Economic pressure alone does not explain the pattern. Scarcity could make animals, land, or trade more valuable, but whether scarcity produced migration, bargaining, raiding, taxation, or war depended on who could command men and define a lawful claim. Technology widened reach: the longship moved quickly along coasts and rivers, while the camel made desert traffic possible. Neither device supplied a motive by itself. Leaders, households, merchants, and states made each technology dangerous by attaching rewards and obligations to its use.

The First Crusade carried these entanglements into the eastern Mediterranean, where religious promises, princely ambition, commercial calculation, and military necessity became inseparable in the aftermath of conquest. Jerusalem in 1099 was not only a sacred destination. It was a city with neighbourhoods, markets, water systems, workshops, religious institutions, and inhabitants whose lives were reorganised by the arrival of an army claiming divine sanction.

When the crusading army entered Jerusalem in July 1099, the surviving accounts describe a siege followed by the capture of the city and extensive killing. Latin authors such as Raymond of Aguilers presented the event as a victory granted to the crusaders, and their descriptions of bloodshed helped turn the capture into a sign of divine favour. Muslim and Jewish communities experienced the conquest as dispossession and death. The Latin army’s assault passed through walls and streets, but its consequences reached households that had not chosen the expedition and could not negotiate with the men who claimed the city.

The names most often attached to the conquest are Godfrey of Bouillon, Raymond of Saint-Gilles, Tancred, and Robert of Normandy. Behind them stood men who had travelled from different regions of Europe, joined for different reasons, and expected different rewards. Some sought remission of sins, some land or status, some protection for fellow pilgrims, and some the chance to profit from conquest. A single religious banner gathered those motives without making them identical. Once Jerusalem was taken, the language of pilgrimage had to coexist with the work of governing a populated city.

The new rulers distributed property, installed Latin clergy, and defended the territory against surrounding powers. The Holy Sepulchre became a centre of Christian kingship as well as devotion. Jerusalem’s Muslim and Jewish inhabitants were killed, expelled, or subjected to new conditions, while Christian communities of different rites negotiated their place under Latin rule. Conquest did not simply replace one administration with another. It altered access to homes, markets, shrines, and protection, and gave violence an institutional form that continued after the army’s departure.

News of Jerusalem moved outward through letters, sermons, oral reports, and returning participants. For those who had not seen the city, the event could be remembered as a miracle, a massacre, a warning, or proof that enemies had been defeated. Such memories affected recruitment and resistance. A city’s capture became an argument used in later campaigns, while people displaced by it carried knowledge of Latin methods and intentions to other towns. Armies moved through the Levant, but the meaning of their violence travelled through stories.

Antioch revealed a different difficulty. The city was captured in June 1098 after a long siege marked by hunger, disease, desertion, negotiation, and assault. Bohemond of Taranto, Raymond of Saint-Gilles, and other leaders had entered the expedition with shared enemies but competing plans. Once Antioch fell, Bohemond claimed the city, while other leaders argued over promises, leadership, and the obligation to continue towards Jerusalem. The conquest created a prize whose possession threatened to divide the coalition that had won it.

Antioch’s population had already lived through the turbulence of Seljuk expansion, Byzantine recovery, local rule, and the arrival of the crusaders. The citadel, walls, gates, markets, and surrounding agricultural land gave the city a value no devotional description could exhaust. Control of Antioch meant access to routes between Anatolia and Syria, to the Orontes valley, and to a port at Saint Symeon. A lord who held it could tax movement and withstand attack. A population that survived the siege found itself inside a new political experiment whose rulers needed both revenue and allies.

The crusaders’ victory at Antioch did not end danger. Kerbogha of Mosul arrived with a large army, and the crusaders, still divided and weakened, faced encirclement inside the city. The discovery of the Holy Lance, described by Raymond of Aguilers and others, helped generate the religious confidence needed for the army’s sortie and victory in June 1098. Hunger made action necessary; belief helped make a risky action acceptable and coordinated. Material desperation and sacred interpretation worked together in the decision to fight.

Afterward, Bohemond remained in Antioch, while other leaders continued south. The principality that developed there depended on fortresses, tribute, alliances, local administrators, military households, and relations with Armenian and Syrian Christian communities. Latin rulers could offer protection against one enemy while imposing new burdens or provoking another. Christians who had suffered under previous regimes did not thereby become a single political community with the conquerors. Religious affinity could assist negotiation, but it did not erase differences of rite, language, rank, or interest.

The displaced communities of the Levant carried the aftermath beyond the captured cities. Some inhabitants fled before or during sieges; others were expelled, enslaved, or moved through military and commercial networks. Captives taken in Jerusalem, Antioch, and later campaigns could be sold, exchanged, ransomed, or retained as labourers. Families were separated along the same routes that carried grain, letters, and troops. A community displaced from a city did not vanish from history, but the sources often record its absence more clearly than its later life. Those who remained had to deal with altered taxes, courts, landholding, and armed protection. A village near Jerusalem might supply food to the new kingdom and fear raids from neighbouring Muslim powers. A Christian community near Antioch might gain room to worship under one ruler and lose influence under another. Jewish survivors and refugees entered other towns or joined networks of trade and kinship. “The inhabitants” hides these differences, just as “the crusaders” hides the disputes among the men who claimed the conquest.

The aftermath changed the surrounding political field. Muslim rulers in Damascus, Aleppo, Mosul, and Egypt confronted Latin enclaves that had not existed before the expedition. Their responses combined military resistance with negotiation, rivalry, and the search for allies. Zengi’s capture of Edessa in 1144 later became a powerful symbol in Latin Europe, while Nur al-Din and Saladin built authority partly through the promise of opposing the crusader states. The violence of 1099 supplied political language to rulers who came after it.

Saladin’s entry into Jerusalem in October 1187, after the battle of Hattin, was shaped by the memory of the earlier massacre. The surrender arrangements allowed many inhabitants to leave through ransom or payment, though the process was unequal and not everyone escaped easily. Latin churches were converted or reassigned, while some Christian shrines and communities were accommodated. The contrast with 1099 mattered to Muslim and Christian writers, but it should not be turned into a simple proof of moral progress. Saladin was managing legitimacy, prisoners, property, and the response of a city whose capture had strategic as well as religious value.

The people of Jerusalem experienced conquest as a change in the terms under which ordinary actions were possible. Who could open a shop, hold land, cross a gate, worship in a shrine, or claim the protection of a judge depended on the ruler of the moment. The First Crusade attached violence to new titles, new settlements, and new claims about rightful possession. A sacred destination became a capital, and the capital became a prize in arguments that continued long after the first army departed.

Among the most searching documents from the later crisis of Islamic political order is a fatwa associated with Ibn Taymiyya and the Mongol rulers who had entered the lands of the Abbasid caliphate. Ibn Taymiyya, a scholar from Damascus who died in 1328, addressed a problem that military victory alone could not settle: how should Muslims judge rulers who professed Islam but continued to govern according to the *yasa*, the law attributed to Chinggis Khan, and who attacked Muslim territories?

The document’s force lies in its conditional structure. Ibn Taymiyya did not treat political violence as legitimate merely because an enemy had been defeated or because a ruler claimed the Muslim name. He examined conduct, law, allegiance, and the relation between profession and rule. The Mongols had converted in significant numbers, and some Mongol leaders presented themselves as Muslims. Their armies nevertheless invaded Syria, threatened Damascus, and maintained political practices that Ibn Taymiyya regarded as incompatible with the obligations of Islamic governance. His argument turned on legitimate authority. A ruler was not made rightful by genealogy, military success, or a public declaration alone. Rule required obedience to the law of Islam and protection of the Muslim community. When a regime claimed Islam while judging by another code and warring against Muslims, Ibn Taymiyya could classify it as an enemy requiring resistance. The category did not depend on the ruler’s ancestry. It depended on the relation between belief, law, and political action. This was dangerous language because it could direct violence inward. The Mongol rulers were no longer simply outsiders beyond the religious community. Their partial adoption of Islam made the question sharper: could a ruler be treated as an unbeliever, or as an illegitimate aggressor, when he professed the faith but violated its political obligations? Ibn Taymiyya’s response gave legal and theological form to the judgement that profession without lawful conduct was insufficient. His text made authority answerable to a standard higher than the ruler’s declaration.

“Profession without lawful conduct was insufficient.”

The fatwa should be read alongside the circumstances in which it was produced. Damascus was not a schoolroom detached from war. The Mongol invasions had displaced populations, damaged towns, and placed scholars and rulers under pressure. Ibn Taymiyya participated in the public debates of his time and urged resistance. The document’s religious reasoning was therefore also a response to an immediate political emergency, though it cannot be reduced to personal anger or patriotic speech. Its terms connected battlefield decisions to a jurisprudential argument about the obligations of rulers and subjects.

The earlier frontier cases clarify the stakes. The Byzantine *akritai* represented defence at a border where identities overlapped, while Ibn Taymiyya confronted a regime whose religious identity was disputed from within the community it ruled. In both cases, authority was tested by conduct under pressure. A wall, a military title, or a profession of faith did not settle who could command obedience. The question was whether protection, law, and allegiance were joined in a manner recognised as legitimate.

Ibn Taymiyya’s use of precedent and legal reasoning also constrained the violence he could justify. He was not writing that every political disagreement permitted killing. He distinguished among enemies and considered the consequences of fighting them. The Mongols’ actions, their laws, and their attacks on Muslim lands supplied the grounds for resistance. Religious vocabulary sharpened political conflict through categories, conditions, and inherited arguments rather than through an undifferentiated call to rage.

The document records a struggle over names. “Muslim,” “Mongol,” “ruler,” “enemy,” and “legitimate authority” were not neutral descriptions; each carried consequences for obedience, war, and punishment. A community could be urged to resist a ruler because resistance was presented as a duty when rule had broken its lawful purpose. The same vocabulary could narrow the space for compromise. Once an opponent was placed outside legitimate authority, negotiation became more difficult and coercion easier to defend. Yet the Mongol world did not divide neatly between faith and force. Mongol rulers governed Muslim cities through officials, tax collectors, military commanders, and local notables. Muslim scholars served under rulers they disliked or criticised. Merchants continued to seek profit across boundaries that armies treated as hostile. A ruler might be condemned in a fatwa and still be encountered in a market, a treaty, a tax office, or a diplomatic mission.

The fatwa belongs to the history of aggression as a close record of how religious judgement can organise political action. It supplied a vocabulary through which a community could identify unlawful rule, distinguish nominal affiliation from legitimate conduct, and authorise resistance. That vocabulary addressed Mongol armies, Syrian towns, displaced people, and the threatened authority of local rulers. Belief gave the conflict a moral shape; institutions and armies gave the shape force.

## Chapter 7: Steppe Powers and Connected Worlds

The Mongol armies entered a political field that settled rulers often described too simply. To call the steppe a place of raiders was to miss the work required to make mobile power durable: households had to attach themselves to leaders, pasture had to be allocated, fighting men counted, and victory converted into obligations that survived a season’s movement. *The Secret History of the Mongols* begins not with an empire already formed but with a child whose position is repeatedly made insecure by decisions made around him.

Temüjin was born, according to the *Secret History*, while his father Yesügei was returning from a raid against the Tatars. The child was said to have arrived with a clot of blood in his hand, a sign that marks his future connection with killing and rule. The surrounding details are less ornamental. Yesügei arranged Temüjin’s betrothal to Börte of the Onggirat while the boy was still young, then was poisoned by Tatars before he could secure his family’s place among the tribes. Temüjin’s mother, Hö’elün, was left with children and dependants whom the Tayichi’ut abandoned beside the Onon River.

The family survived by gathering roots, catching fish, and hunting small animals. The *Secret History* gives these hardships a domestic scale: the children disputed over food, Bö’orchu joined Temüjin after finding one of his stolen horses, and Jamuqa became first a sworn companion and later an enemy. A horse could be taken; a marriage could be broken; brothers could become rivals over a marmot or a fish. The narrative does not describe an abstract “Mongol character.” It follows a household whose prospects depended on kin, livestock, marriage ties, and the willingness of stronger men to recognize its claims.

Temüjin’s first important acts were acts of attachment. He sought the protection of Toghoril, the Kerait ruler known in the *Secret History* as Ong Khan, renewing the alliance formed through his father’s earlier relationship with him. After Börte was abducted by the Merkit, Temüjin called on Toghoril and Jamuqa for help. The rescue was a military action, but the marriage became politically useful because Börte belonged to the Onggirat and because the alliance gathered men connected by obligation rather than by a single settled territory. A household joined a larger coalition; the coalition gained a cause and a marriage; its leader could demonstrate authority through distribution.

Jamuqa’s friendship with Temüjin developed into formal unions, and the *Secret History* presents their separation as a question of political style. Some men preferred Jamuqa’s established rank and ceremonial precedence. Others moved toward Temüjin because he promoted followers according to service and loyalty rather than preserving every privilege of lineage. The account may be shaped by the victorious ruler’s memory, but it repeatedly returns to named followers and particular rewards. Jelme, Subedei, Muqali, and Bo’orchu appear not as interchangeable instruments but as men whose careers became evidence for the coalition Temüjin was building. He punished betrayal with unusual severity. The defeat of the Tayichi’ut, the destruction of the Tatars, and the struggle with Toghoril and the Keraits brought killings, enslavement, and incorporation. The *Secret History* presents Temüjin’s decisions as responses to treachery and old enmity, while showing that conquest required the breaking of rival leaderships. The Tatars who had killed Yesügei were connected to the personal history through which Temüjin understood obligation. The campaign against them became a policy to prevent their nobles from restoring power, and the order to kill adult males joined private memory to collective violence.

Defeated groups supplied soldiers, artisans, herders, interpreters, wives, children, and specialists. Some leaders were executed; others were rewarded; ordinary households were redistributed among new units. Decimal groupings—tens, hundreds, thousands, and larger formations—made military service legible to the commander and loosened older loyalties by placing men under officers who were not always their former kin leaders. Family ties remained, but they now answered to a wider chain of command.

At the quriltai of 1206, after the defeat of major rivals, Temüjin received the title Chinggis Khan. The *Secret History* remembers the gathering as the culmination of his ascent, but the title did not create the coalition from nothing. It named a settlement among men who had survived wars, benefited from redistribution, or accepted that no rival could assemble comparable force. Chinggis Khan’s authority rested on rewarding followers, assigning commands, regulating succession, and directing violence outward. It also rested on a story in which hardship, loyalty, betrayal, and providential signs made the new order intelligible.

The narrative’s intimacy can obscure the scale of what followed. A boy abandoned near the Onon became the ruler of a coalition able to move beyond the eastern steppe, but the path between those conditions was neither a simple expression of temperament nor an automatic result of nomadic life. It involved the destruction of competing elites, the recruitment of defeated people, the management of herds, and a political vocabulary in which fidelity to the khan could supersede an older loyalty. The *Secret History* gives us Temüjin’s face at the centre of this process; the names, motives, and losses of the thousands around him are less fully recorded.

A mobile coalition became most formidable when it ceased to rely on immediate presence. The Mongol *yam*, the relay system used to carry messengers, orders, intelligence, and sometimes officials across long distances, extended the khan’s reach beyond the speed of a single mounted party. Stations were supplied with horses and provisions, and local communities could be required to support the traffic. A command sent from Karakorum or from a moving court passed through successive riders, each covering a segment before handing the message to another. The order reached a distant commander because the empire had divided movement into obligations.

The system was not a road laid across empty grassland. A relay route depended on inhabited places and extracted resources. Horses had to be available, fodder gathered, guards assigned, and obligations recorded. The same empire that allowed merchants and envoys to cross great distances could compel households to maintain a service they had not freely chosen. Communication was a practical convenience and a form of rule: communities carried the court’s demands because the court had made their labour answerable to it.

At Ayn Jalut in 1260, the Mongols met an opponent that could not be subdued by speed alone. The Mamluk forces of Egypt, led by Sultan Qutuz and the commander Baybars, confronted the Mongol army in Palestine after Hülegü had withdrawn much of his force toward Iran. The battle took place near springs and grazing country in the Jezreel Valley, where concealment, water, and timing shaped movement. Baybars is associated with a forward force that drew the Mongols into pursuit before the main Mamluk army appeared. Qutuz then committed the larger force, and the Mongol commander Kitbuqa was killed.

The contrast with the *yam* lies in the different scale at which information operated. A relay could coordinate across distance, but it could not guarantee equal numbers, timely reinforcement, or reliable local allegiance in every theatre. News of Hülegü’s withdrawal altered the balance in Syria. The Mongols had conquered cities rapidly, yet the local political field remained unsettled, and the Mamluks could present themselves as the force capable of resisting an army whose advance had seemed unstoppable. At Ayn Jalut, mounted warfare met divided commands, distance, and an enemy prepared to control the moment of engagement.

Baybars understood that the Mongols had to be made to commit themselves. A force able to retreat in good order and draw an enemy across open ground was difficult to defeat, but a pursuing army could be channelled towards an ambush or separated from support. The Mamluk victory did not end Mongol power in the region. It stopped the advance into Egypt and helped secure the Mamluk sultanate’s position in Syria. Its force came from a military society studying Mongol habits and adapting its own command structure against them.

The two cases belong together because both depended on information, though they used it differently. The *yam* carried knowledge through a chain of stations; Ayn Jalut turned knowledge of Mongol expectations, numbers, and movements into a tactical advantage. The Mongols’ strength was organizational before it was merely physical. The Mamluk response succeeded because organization could be learned, concealed, and directed back against them. A relay widened the khan’s reach; a concealed detachment narrowed the enemy’s choices.

Ayn Jalut has sometimes been treated as a boundary between an unstoppable Mongol conquest and settled Muslim resistance. The surviving episode supports a narrower judgment. The battle shows that the Mongol army could be defeated under particular circumstances, especially when its forces were divided and its opponent controlled the moment of engagement. It does not show that the Mamluks possessed a universal answer to Mongol warfare, or that mounted power had ceased to matter. The advance into Egypt had been redirected, not erased.

The relay horse and the concealed Mamluk detachment belonged to different scales of war. One connected a court to distant commanders; the other altered what an enemy thought he saw on a single morning. A rider carrying an order, a station maintaining spare mounts, and a commander withholding the main body joined force to knowledge. Both systems enlarged the consequences of delay, misjudgement, and isolation.

The speed of a steppe campaign began long before an army crossed a frontier. It began with horses. A Mongol household might possess several mounts for each fighting man, not because every horse served the same purpose but because animals had to be rested, replaced, and matched to different tasks. One horse could carry a rider during a march while another bore equipment and a third remained fresh for pursuit. The army’s apparent ability to move without stopping came from stopping its individual horses in rotation.

The herd depended on pasture. Families moved animals between seasonal grazing grounds, using knowledge of water, shelter, forage, and weather. Such movement was not random wandering. It was a schedule shaped by the condition of grass and by the needs of sheep, goats, cattle, camels, and horses. When a coalition gathered for war, its camps and departure had to fit this cycle. An army that concentrated too early could exhaust the pasture near its camp; one that left too late could meet winter without sufficient fodder.

Pasture rotation supplied the first stage of a campaign. Households spread animals across available grazing areas, preserving strong horses for later movement and keeping remounts within reach. A dry summer, a blocked water source, or the loss of a winter pasture could reduce military options months before battle. Economic pressure entered warfare through animals and grass, but it became destructive when a khan could compel households to move, contribute mounts, and accept the risks of a campaign decided elsewhere.

The second stage was assembly. Men arrived with their own horses, weapons, and provisions, but they did not simply form a crowd. Decimal units placed soldiers under officers, combined contingents, and distributed orders. The reorganization associated with Chinggis Khan could detach fighting men from older clan structures and place them in a command hierarchy that followed the khan. A man who had once answered chiefly to a lineage head could now be counted in a thousand commanded by a different leader. Coordination improved as obedience became more direct.

The third stage was reconnaissance. Mongol forces sought information from scouts, merchants, envoys, captives, interpreters, and people who knew the roads ahead. Before invading a settled state, they could gather knowledge of river crossings, fortified towns, harvests, political divisions, and the loyalties of local rulers. Trade routes helped carry intelligence because merchants already moved between regions and understood the value of accurate reports. Diplomatic demands tested an opponent’s response as well. An embassy could carry a message of submission while revealing something about the court that received it.

Intelligence had human carriers and local costs. A guide might be compelled to lead a column through difficult ground. A captured official might identify a road or explain the relationship between a governor and his sovereign. A merchant might exchange information for protection. A city’s rival might welcome the Mongols to weaken a neighbouring ruler. The campaign advanced through threats and unequal bargains made by people who were not all Mongol and did not share the same interests.

Once the route had been chosen, remounts converted information into endurance. Riders changed horses before an animal was exhausted, allowing a message or scouting party to cover great distances with fewer pauses. The main army could carry or gather replacement animals as it moved, though this depended on captured herds, local pasture, and successful supply arrangements. The familiar image of a rider who never seems to tire describes an animal system. Human stamina mattered, but the campaign’s rhythm was set by the repeated substitution of rested horses.

The composite bow made the moving horse a platform for force. Constructed from wood, horn, and sinew, and held under tension, it could be shorter and more manageable on horseback than a large infantry bow while retaining considerable power. A mounted archer could approach, shoot, turn away, and return without presenting a fixed target. The bow required training and care: sinew and glue were vulnerable to damp conditions, and the weapon had to be protected from weather. Its effectiveness came from design joined to skilled use, suitable animals, and a tactical system that allowed the archer to choose distance.

An attack could proceed in stages. Scouts located the enemy and reported its position. A forward group tested the response, perhaps feigning retreat. Mounted archers harassed the formation, seeking disorder rather than immediate hand-to-hand combat. Other units moved towards a flank or rear. Fresh horses sustained the pressure, while officers used signals and messengers to coordinate changes in direction. If the enemy broke, pursuit became the most lethal phase; if it held, the Mongols could withdraw, regroup, and seek another angle.

The procedure sounds smooth because the sources and later reputation emphasise success. In practice, every stage could fail. Grass might not support the herds, a river could rise, or scouts might misread a fortified position. Humidity could damage bows, and a coalition member might refuse an order when the expected reward no longer seemed sufficient. At Ayn Jalut, Mongol tactical habits met a Mamluk command that understood the danger of pursuit and used concealment against it.

Horse herds also made conquest economically demanding. An army consumed animals through battle, disease, exhaustion, and the ordinary movement of thousands of people. The demand for pasture could bring Mongol forces into conflict with other mobile groups before they reached a city. Seizing livestock weakened a rival household while enlarging the means of continuing the war. Herds were food, transport, wealth, and future military power at once.

The campaign’s speed rested on a chain that began with ecology and ended in command. Pasture rotation kept animals alive; household obligations supplied them; decimal units organised men; intelligence located weakness; remounts sustained movement; and composite bows made distance useful. Remove any link and the army changed character. A bow without a trained rider was an object. A horse without pasture was a short-term resource. Information without a command structure was rumour. The same chain carried more than violence. Merchants, artisans, translators, envoys, and clerics travelled along routes secured by Mongol power, even when that security was imposed through taxation and threat. The road that brought an order to a commander could bring a merchant to a market or carry a craftsman to a new city. Conquest widened exchange by joining territories previously divided by hostile rulers. It also widened extraction. A community that had never seen a Mongol army might still be required to furnish horses, food, labour, or tax. That double use leaves a question that the surviving administrative evidence sharpens rather than resolves. When Persian officials recorded taxes for the Ilkhanid rulers, when Yuan administrators adapted Chinese fiscal practices, and when Rus' princes collected tribute under Mongol oversight, were these parts of a deliberate design for integrating Eurasia, or were they local bargains made by rulers who needed revenue and populations that needed room to survive?

The Persian Ilkhanid administration did not simply reproduce the institutions of the Mongolian steppe. In Iran, Mongol rulers governed through Persian bureaucrats, tax registers, urban officials, and established practices of land revenue. Rashid al-Din, who served the Ilkhan Ghazan and later Öljeitü, belonged to a bureaucratic world in which governing provinces and taxes required written expertise. Ghazan’s reforms sought to regularise taxation and restrain abuses that damaged cultivation and emptied villages. Such measures served the ruler’s revenue, but they also depended on local officials and landed interests whose cooperation could not be assumed.

A decree could announce a policy; its effect depended on who collected it. If tax demands exceeded what a district could produce, cultivators fled, irrigation declined, and the treasury eventually suffered. If a local notable secured exemption, the burden shifted to others. Persian administration joined Mongol military authority to inherited practices, but the resulting order was negotiated through unequal relationships. The Ilkhans wanted a productive Iran. Their officials, commanders, and local intermediaries did not always agree about how production would be secured.

The Yuan dynasty offers a different settlement. Kublai Khan adopted the title of a Chinese emperor, established his capital at Dadu, and governed through institutions associated with the Chinese imperial state while preserving distinctions among groups within the empire. The examination system was not restored in its earlier form immediately or uniformly, and Mongol, Central Asian, northern Chinese, and southern Chinese personnel occupied different positions in the hierarchy. The dynasty used Chinese taxation, grain transport, postal routes, and administrative offices because these systems could govern dense agricultural populations at a scale that mobile court practices alone could not manage. This was adaptation, but it was not surrender. The Yuan court maintained Mongol privilege, military colonies, and links to the wider imperial family. Its capital received goods and people from across Inner Asia, while officials reconciled steppe political traditions with Chinese expectations of dynastic rule. The result was neither a pure Mongol system nor a simple continuation of the Song state. It was a government assembled from inherited offices, conquered resources, court priorities, and compromises made in the provinces.

Rus' tribute arrangements show another pattern. The Mongol conquest did not replace every Rus' prince with a Mongol governor. Princes such as Yaroslav of Vladimir and later Alexander Nevsky sought recognition from the khan, received patents of authority, and collected payments from their territories. The tribute, often called *vykhod*, was gathered through local rulers and officials, while Mongol representatives could supervise censuses and revenue. The relationship imposed subordination without requiring direct administration of every town.

For a Rus' prince, recognition from the khan could strengthen his position against rivals. For the khan, the prince was useful because he could extract revenue and maintain order at lower cost than a permanent occupation. The bargain was coercive, and Mongol punitive expeditions remained a threat when payments failed or resistance spread. Its operation nevertheless depended on local political competition. A prince could turn service to the khan into an instrument against another prince, just as the khan could use competing princes to preserve control. These cases do not align neatly on a scale from tolerance to oppression, or from direct rule to independence. In Iran, Mongol rulers employed Persian fiscal knowledge while trying to protect revenue from destructive exaction. In China, the Yuan court adopted major institutions of imperial administration while retaining a hierarchy that favoured Mongol power. In Rus', khans governed through princes whose authority was both enabled and constrained by tribute. Each settlement connected military conquest to existing political forms, but each connection was made in a different place by people with different leverage.

The language of integration can conceal the violence that made connection possible. Roads became safer for some travellers after conquest because competing rulers had been defeated; the same roads carried taxes, prisoners, and military orders. Caravan trade expanded where merchants could rely on imperial protection, yet protection was purchased through levies and obedience. Religious communities sometimes received charters or exemptions, while other populations endured massacre, enslavement, or forced movement. A connected world was not a peaceful world. It was a world in which decisions made at one centre could reach people far away.

Was that world planned from the beginning? Chinggis Khan’s coalition required deliberate choices about loyalty, command, marriage, punishment, and distribution. The *yam* required sustained provisioning. Officials in conquered regions had to recognise that one form of rule could not simply be carried intact from the steppe into every agricultural province. These facts suggest purposeful adaptation. They do not establish that a single blueprint for Eurasian integration guided every Mongol ruler, or that later arrangements followed an original imperial intention.

Local bargains leave their marks because they are uneven. A Persian vizier could make a reform serve the Ilkhan’s treasury and his own administrative interests. A Yuan official could translate a Mongol demand into Chinese fiscal categories. A Rus' prince could present tribute as submission while using the khan’s confirmation to defeat a domestic rival. None of these actors controlled the whole empire. Their choices could preserve a conquered order, redirect it, or make it more burdensome than the court had intended.

The uncertainty is specific. Mongol rulers repeatedly joined mobile military power to settled administration, while officials and local rulers reshaped that connection. Similar practices alone cannot tell us whether their resemblance came from a central programme or from practical solutions discovered in separate regions. The empire may have been deliberate in its appetite for revenue and allegiance while remaining contingent in the forms through which those demands were met. Its unity was made by commands, roads, armies, and titles; its everyday life was made by bargains.

## Chapter 8: States, Slavery, and the Atlantic World

In March 1662, the lawmakers of Virginia entered a short rule into the colony’s records: “Whereas some doubts have arisen whether children got by any Englishman upon a negro woman should be slave or free, Be it therefore enacted and declared by this present grand assembly, that all children borne in this country shall be held bond or free only according to the condition of the mother.” The sentence begins with doubt and ends with inheritance. Between those two points, it changes a child’s status from a matter that might be argued in court into a condition assigned at birth.

The words were economical. A child born in Virginia would not be judged by the father’s identity, freedom, or circumstances of conception. The mother’s condition supplied the rule. If she was enslaved, the child was enslaved; if she was free, the child was free. The Latin phrase later used for this principle, *partus sequitur ventrem*, means that the condition of the child follows the condition of the womb. Virginia’s assembly attached legal status to reproduction and made the future labour of children available to the same people who claimed the mother.

“Some doubts had arisen.” The doubts came from a society in which English men, African women, baptism, service, purchase, and coercion had brought different forms of dependence into contact. Earlier assumptions about English hereditary status did not settle every case in a colony where people of different legal and racial positions lived together, or where an enslaved woman might bear a child fathered by a free man. The assembly answered that uncertainty by making maternity decisive.

A father’s refusal to acknowledge responsibility could no longer interrupt an owner’s claim over the child. The law did not ask whether the father was known, whether he was free, or whether he had exercised force. Nor did it make him answerable for the child’s condition. The burden travelled through the mother, whose legal subordination became the channel through which slavery reproduced itself.

There was a plain economic advantage. Owners did not have to establish paternity before claiming a child’s labour. They could count future workers through the status of enslaved women. A plantation household, a court, and a buyer could treat a pregnant woman not only as someone already held in bondage but as the bearer of another person whose legal condition had been fixed before birth. The statute made generation a source of property. It also separated sexual relations from the legal consequences that English patriarchy might otherwise have imposed on free men. A free father did not transmit freedom to his child, but neither did the law make his own status vulnerable to the child’s condition. The mother’s body carried the legal cost. Such a rule could stabilise a society in which white men exercised sexual power over enslaved women while preserving the boundary of white freedom.

A court applying the rule would need no account of intention. It would not have to decide whether a mother had consented, whether a father had promised marriage, or whether a child resembled one parent more than another. The record of the mother’s condition was enough. Birth became an administrative event through which names, ages, ownership claims, and transfers could follow. That compression of human circumstance into a legal category gave the statute its force. It made slavery durable without requiring each generation to be captured abroad. The Atlantic trade brought people into Virginia, but the colony’s law made enslaved women and their children part of a local reproductive system. Violence at capture and sale was joined to violence in the household and the field, then carried into the court and the inheritance ledger. The law did not replace those forms of force. It connected them across generations.

The statute also altered the meaning of race. Skin colour did not by itself establish the principle; the words concerned the mother’s condition. But as slavery became increasingly associated with African descent, hereditary status and racial classification strengthened one another. A rule framed around bondage helped produce a society in which Blackness was treated as evidence of enslavability and whiteness as evidence of freedom. Law did not merely reflect that association. It repeated it in births, sales, punishments, and property disputes until a social hierarchy appeared natural because it had been made ordinary.

Virginia’s 1662 enactment belongs beside the ships, markets, and plantations of the Atlantic world, not beneath them as a technical detail. It shows a state narrowing the space in which status could be contested. The assembly did not abolish uncertainty from colonial life; it directed uncertainty away from owners and toward enslaved families. A free father could not free his child. A child could inherit bondage without having committed any act. A woman’s captivity could become a lineage.

The principle was portable because it was simple. It could be used in a plantation economy, a court hearing, an estate inventory, or an argument over whether a child belonged to one owner or another. Its simplicity concealed the work required to enforce it: surveillance of women, recognition of ownership, punishment of flight, and adjudication of claims. A sentence in a statute became a social order only when people with weapons, offices, money, and legal standing made it effective.

Barbados makes the pressure visible. Sugar required land cleared, cane planted, cut, crushed, boiled, and transported. The crop imposed seasons and deadlines. Mills and boiling houses ran according to the pace of harvest, while heat, exhaustion, injury, and disease made the work difficult to sustain. A plantation that expanded acreage needed workers to make expansion profitable, and a mill represented an investment that could not be left idle during harvest.

The Royal African Company illustrates the difference. Its supply networks depended on forts, ships, contracts, credit, port officials, merchants, and armed protection. Captives were not delivered to Barbados by an invisible market. They were seized, sold, marched, confined, loaded, transported, inspected, and resold through connections joining African rulers and traders, European companies, colonial buyers, and metropolitan authorities. Each link could alter the price, the risk, and the number of people moved. The company’s chartered position and shipping resources helped make the trade more regular and extensive than isolated private purchases could have made it.

The network did not produce one uniform African experience. Captivity could result from war, raiding, judicial punishment, debt, kidnapping, or political bargaining, and African polities made different choices under changing pressures. European merchants did not control every event on the African coast. Varied African participation does not dissolve the structure of Atlantic enslavement. European colonial economies created the demand, European shipping carried most captives across the ocean, and colonial law converted purchased people into inheritable property.

Barbados gave imported labour a local political shape. Plantation owners needed the colonial government to define who could be enslaved, discipline workers, restrict movement, regulate testimony, and punish resistance. Without such rules, an owner’s claim over a captive remained vulnerable to flight, collective action, competing claims, and moral or religious challenge. The plantation was a workplace, but it was also a jurisdiction sustained by law.

The code did not merely tell workers what to do. It told white residents what they could do to Black people and told officials which injuries would be treated as private discipline rather than public crime. A market could purchase labour, but only government could turn the buyer’s claim into a protected right to compel work across a lifetime and pass that claim to heirs. Economic motive required a legal vocabulary of property and a political vocabulary of racial belonging.

Comparisons with other labour systems therefore require care. Indentured servants, convict labourers, conquered peasants, and military dependants could all be coerced, but they did not occupy identical conditions. Some forms of service had terms, recognised routes to release, or rights that could be invoked against an owner. Hereditary racial slavery closed those routes for an entire population and made family formation serve the owner’s future supply of labour. The Virginia statute clarifies what a plantation market by itself leaves unexplained.

Ideas about belonging supplied another part of the answer. Colonists who called themselves English could define freedom as a status attached to whiteness, Christianity, landholding, or political membership while treating Africans as outsiders whose presence authorised control. Religious arguments varied. Some Christians condemned cruelty; others accommodated slavery or helped justify it. African descent became useful as a category because it could be inherited, seen, and administered. It marked a population for exclusion from privileges that colonial governments claimed to protect.

Profit intensified these distinctions, but profit did not dictate them in one unavoidable form. Barbados planters could have confronted labour scarcity through wages, limited contracts, immigration, or smaller holdings. They instead developed plantation slavery within an Atlantic world that supplied captives and governments that protected property claims. Economic pressure became organised harm through those choices.

At Stono, the pressure moved in the opposite direction. The surviving South Carolina testimony is hostile, fragmented, and concerned with punishment, but it records an event that owners could not reduce to an accounting problem. On a Sunday in September 1739, enslaved people gathered near the Stono River, southwest of Charleston. Sunday gave some enslaved workers distance from plantation supervision, while the colony’s militia obligations could be less immediately concentrated. The men and women who assembled acted within a landscape of roads, plantations, waterways, churches, and armed patrols.

The testimony reports that the rebels first attacked Hutchinson’s store, killed the two white storekeepers, and took guns and ammunition. At Stono’s bridge they raised a banner and moved along the road. The records describe a group calling for liberty and hoping to reach Spanish Florida, where Spanish authorities had offered freedom to enslaved people who escaped from English colonies and accepted Catholic baptism. That destination gave the march a political direction. Flight could mean movement toward another jurisdiction.

The group grew as it proceeded. Testimony gathered afterward described houses burned and white inhabitants killed, though the numbers and sequence vary across accounts. Some enslaved people joined; others did not. The rebellion did not represent every Black person in South Carolina, and the surviving statements cannot reconstruct the words or motives of each participant. They do show an organised movement that collected weapons, used the road, sought recruits, and understood the colony’s relation to Spanish Florida.

Lieutenant Governor William Bull encountered the march with a small party of white men. The colonial accounts say that the rebels saw them and withdrew into the woods rather than immediately engaging. Bull and the men with him returned toward the area around the Edisto River, raising the alarm and gathering militia. As word spread, the colonial response acquired the advantages of a plantation government: mounted men, local knowledge, armed settlers, and the authority to command others into pursuit.

The confrontation came later that day. The militia attacked the rebels, killed some, and captured others. The surviving testimony says that several rebels were taken alive and that others escaped into the woods. The march had covered ground, but it had not reached Florida. The records are more interested in the colony’s recovery of control than in the final decisions of those who fled, and the fate of every participant cannot be followed.

South Carolina then answered rebellion with law. The colony tightened patrols, restricted movement, and increased punishments. The Negro Act of 1740 sought to limit gatherings, trade, education, and activities officials believed could support collective resistance. Its language converted fear into regulation. A Sunday march by armed people became evidence for monitoring ordinary movement; the possibility of literacy or assembly became a matter for criminal control.

The testimony records two kinds of action. It shows rebels choosing a road, a store, weapons, a banner, and a destination. It also shows officials translating an uprising into a wider programme of surveillance. What began as a local revolt altered the legal conditions of thousands who had not taken part. Collective punishment extended the reach of the event beyond its participants.

The Stono records preserve the colonial government’s view most clearly. They name places, officers, weapons, deaths, arrests, and punishments. They say less about the conversations through which people decided to join or remain behind. Still, silence in testimony does not turn the rebellion into a reflex. The appeal of Spanish Florida, the seizure of guns, and the attempt to move toward a border indicate planning shaped by knowledge of law and geography. The rebels acted against a system that treated their labour as property, using the narrow openings that system left.

The road from Stono leads into a wider catalogue of resistance, though no catalogue can flatten the differences among its entries. In Jamaica, Maroon communities formed through the flight of enslaved people into mountainous interiors, where forests, ravines, and distance from plantation roads offered protection. Their survival required food, leadership, warning systems, knowledge of terrain, and negotiation with colonial officials. Jamaican Maroons fought British forces and resisted attempts to bring them back under plantation control; treaties later recognised forms of autonomy while also placing limits upon them. A settlement in the hills was both an escape from the plantation and a political community compelled to bargain with the state it had defied.

In Suriname, people who fled Dutch plantations established Maroon societies along rivers and in forested regions beyond easy colonial reach. Plantations clustered along navigable waterways, while the interior demanded different skills and forms of cooperation. Raids could recover weapons or goods, but endurance depended on cultivation, hunting, kinship, ritual, and the transmission of knowledge. Treaties with Dutch authorities acknowledged that colonial governments could not easily destroy these communities. Recognition came after violence and retained the memory of violence.

Brazil’s quilombos varied in size, duration, and political composition. The word names communities associated with people who escaped slavery, but a quilombo was not simply a camp of fugitives. Communities had to make fields, defend approaches, receive newcomers, arrange authority, and maintain relations with surrounding populations. Palmares became the most famous example because it endured for decades and required repeated Portuguese expeditions to attack it. Its history joins flight to state formation: those who escaped had to build enough order to survive the armed campaigns directed against them.

Work slowdowns operated within the plantation rather than beyond it. A worker could perform a task slowly, break a tool, misplace an object, feign illness, damage a crop, or preserve energy for life outside the owner’s schedule. Such actions were difficult to record because owners described them as laziness, disobedience, or theft. The language of the plantation obscured the worker’s intention, yet the recurring struggle over pace shows that labour was never simply extracted. Every field and boiling house contained negotiations over time.

Flight could be temporary or permanent. A person might leave to find a spouse, visit kin, seek food, escape punishment, or reach a Maroon settlement. A colonial notice describing a fugitive usually listed scars, clothing, skills, speech, and the name of an owner, turning the person into a description for recapture. Those details also reveal mobility. People knew roads, rivers, occupations, languages, and settlements beyond the plantation. A runaway was not only absent labour. The act challenged the owner’s claim that a person could be located, returned, and compelled at will.

Petitions made another use of the language of power. Enslaved people in French Saint-Domingue addressed complaints and requests to colonial authorities, invoking baptism, marriage, family separation, illegal sale, excessive punishment, or rights said to follow from royal law and Christian practice. A petition entered a world of writing controlled by officials, but it did not become meaningless for that reason. Its authors had to state a grievance in terms that a court or administrator might recognise, presenting themselves as people entitled to judgment.

A claimant might ask that a family not be divided, that a sale be cancelled, or that an owner be restrained from a particular abuse without demanding the immediate abolition of slavery. Such a request could defend a life while leaving the larger structure intact. Survival often required partial claims. The absence of an abolitionist argument in a petition does not show acceptance of the petitioner’s condition; it shows the narrow field in which a response seemed possible.

The 1662 statute remains small enough to quote in a breath. Its few lines joined the intimate fact of birth to the fiscal needs of a colony and the racial order of an empire. At Stono, people tried to break that connection with stolen weapons and a road toward freedom. In the mountains, forests, settlements, workshops, courts, and households of the Atlantic world, others broke it by inches, by seasons, or by preserving lives the law had declared available for use.
