## Chapter 1: What Do We Mean by Human Aggression?

In September 1991, two hikers found a human body protruding from melting ice in the Ötztal Alps, near the border between Austria and Italy. The body was not recent. It belonged to a man who had died about 5,300 years earlier, during the Copper Age, and whose preservation in ice retained skin, hair, clothing, equipment, and an unusually detailed record of injury. He became known as Ötzi, after the Ötztal Alps. Walter Leitner and his colleagues, in *The Iceman’s Last Days*, could reconstruct much about the damage done to him. They could not recover the explanation that the people around the killing might once have given.

The examination begins with the arrow wound. A flint arrowhead had entered Ötzi’s left shoulder from behind and remained lodged near the scapula. The injury damaged the subclavian artery, and the resulting blood loss would have been fatal. A cut on the right hand, probably made several days before death, had begun to heal. There was also evidence of a blow to the back of the head, though its relation to the death was less certain than that of the arrow. Ötzi lay on his back in a rocky hollow, with his left arm drawn across his chest and his legs extended. His equipment included a copper axe, a flint dagger, a quiver, unfinished arrows, a bow that was not yet complete, and a birch-bark container.

Someone released the arrow, or caused it to be released, and the projectile entered Ötzi’s body from behind. The direction and force of the wound make an attack the clearest description of the event. The arrow records one person’s harmful action against another, with burial and accidental bruising excluded as explanations.

The reason for the shot remains unknown. The assailant may have acted alone, or with others. The attack may have followed a quarrel, an ambush, or a pursuit that ended among the rocks. Ötzi may have been killed in an encounter whose larger purpose disappeared with the people who took part in it. No inscription identifies an enemy, and no settlement record names a dispute. His final position cannot distinguish an execution from a flight that ended in the hollow.

The healed cut on his right hand complicates the sequence without explaining it. A hand wound can be consistent with defence, but it may also have resulted from work, a fall, or contact with a sharp tool. Because the cut had begun to heal, the injury and the arrow wound were not necessarily part of one continuous episode. His equipment raises another question. A copper axe was valuable in the Alpine world, and its presence might suggest that the killing was not a simple robbery. Yet an attacker could have had another purpose, and an object left with a body does not prove that it was never taken, recovered, or deliberately returned.

Leitner’s study reconstructs bodily sequence rather than a finished narrative. Ötzi had sustained an earlier injury, carried equipment across a high Alpine landscape, and received the arrow wound that killed him. His body then remained in the rocky hollow, where snow and ice preserved it. The study can connect the arrowhead, the damaged artery, and the fatal outcome. It cannot recover the social organisation behind the shot.

The forensic record narrows possibilities; it does not turn possibilities into motives.

Ötzi’s final position also resists a confident account of what happened after the arrow struck. His left arm crossed the body, and the corpse rested in a shallow depression rather than in a constructed grave. Snow and ice altered the surroundings over centuries, while pressure and movement within the glacier affected the body and its belongings. Archaeologists can identify the place of death and the condition in which the body was discovered more securely than they can identify every movement between injury and preservation. The evidence does not establish whether he collapsed immediately, whether another person moved him, or whether natural forces shifted him after death.

Aggression begins at a smaller scale than organised violence. If aggression means behaviour directed toward causing harm, Ötzi’s arrow wound supplies strong evidence for it. One person used a weapon against another, producing injury and death. Anger need not be added to the description. Anger is an internal state, ranging from irritation to rage, and the arrow cannot reveal whether the shooter felt any of those emotions. A person may harm another through fear, obedience, calculation, habit, or command.

The wound establishes behaviour; it does not establish feeling. Nor does it settle the question of social organisation. One person can shoot another in a dispute that involves no enduring group structure. Several people can act together without forming a state or army. A community may approve, condemn, or ignore a killing without leaving a mark in the archaeological record. Ötzi’s injury was real, but its setting is missing. That absence prevents the evidence from deciding whether the event should be called a feud, a raid, warfare, punishment, or an isolated attack.

A written law supplies some of the missing setting, though it supplies it in a form shaped by the needs of rule. About seventeen centuries after Ötzi’s death, the Babylonian king Hammurabi placed a long collection of legal decisions and royal claims on a stone monument. The text, conventionally called the Code of Hammurabi and translated by L. W. King, opens with Hammurabi’s relationship to the gods and his duty to establish justice. Its provisions move through property, family, labour, trade, injury, and punishment. Harm appears not as one human impulse but as a set of acts classified by the actor, the victim, intention, consequence, and social position.

The laws concerning bodily injury make the hierarchy explicit. If a man destroyed the eye of another man of the same social rank, the penalty was destruction of his own eye. If he broke another man’s bone, the corresponding injury was imposed upon him. The same physical damage changed legal meaning when the injured person belonged to a different status. Injury to a member of the *awilum* class could call for an equivalent bodily penalty, while injury to a lower-status person could require silver. The body remained the site of harm, but the law assigned different values to bodies within Babylonian society.

Hammurabi’s categories also distinguish intention and consequence. A builder whose poorly made house collapsed and killed its owner could face death. If the collapse killed the owner’s son, the builder’s son could be put to death. The text presents responsibility as a rule that travels through a social relationship, not as spontaneous retaliation. Other provisions impose payments for wounds caused in a fight, for medical treatment, or for harm done to an enslaved person. Injury becomes something that can be measured, priced, and assigned according to rank.

The comparison with Ötzi works because the two records answer different questions. Ötzi’s body shows the force, direction, and lethality of an attack with unusual clarity. Hammurabi’s text shows how a kingdom classified injury after it became a matter for adjudication. The Babylonian document does not give us the sound of a quarrel or the expression on a victim’s face. It gives us a vocabulary in which a wound becomes an offence against a person of a specified status, subject to a specified penalty under the authority of a king.

Law interprets harm and gives some agents the power to decide what harm counts for, while also restraining aggression from outside.

The penalties also show that institutional violence need not resemble the original act. A blow may be answered by a fine, an eye by an eye, or negligent construction by execution. Injury to an enslaved person may produce payment to the owner rather than an equivalent injury to the person harmed. Coercive law is the use of threat, force, economic sanction, or legal pressure to compel conduct or punish its breach. It can reduce private retaliation by transferring judgment to a court, yet it can preserve inequality by making the value of injury depend on status.

The king’s penalty is an instrument of a legal order.

The Babylonian text does not tell us how often its rules were applied or whether ordinary disputes followed them exactly. It shows a ruler presenting bodily harm as an object of classification. The categories determine whether an injury becomes compensation, retaliation, execution, or a recorded offence of lesser weight. Once such distinctions enter law, the institution has done more than observe aggression: it has assigned the act a social and legal meaning.

Thucydides places a different record before us. In Book V of his *History of the Peloponnesian War*, the Athenians arrive at Melos during the war between Athens and Sparta. Melos was an island whose inhabitants were connected to Sparta but had attempted to remain neutral. Before beginning the siege, the Athenians sent envoys to negotiate with the Melians. Thucydides presents the exchange as a dialogue, and his construction separates deliberation, military force, and moral justification.

The Athenian envoys refuse to frame the meeting as an ordinary debate between equal parties. They say that arguments about justice have force when the parties possess comparable power; where one side is stronger, the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must. The Melians answer that they have grounds for hope, appealing to justice, to the gods, and to the possibility that Sparta will assist them. They also argue that surrender would damage Athens by showing other subject peoples that Athenian rule rested on naked force. The Athenians reply that fear, advantage, and calculation govern empires more reliably than moral appeal.

The exchange is a political decision before it becomes a battlefield. The Athenians identify the result they want: Melian submission and the removal of a potential enemy from the Aegean. They consider the island’s relation to Sparta and the example its resistance might set for other communities. Their violence is debated by representatives, justified through arguments about security, and connected to an empire’s need to maintain obedience, rather than presented as an eruption of personal rage.

Discussion does not make the policy peaceful; it organises the path by which force will be used.

Melos differs from Ötzi in scale and documentary form. The arrow wound records a person’s action without preserving the speech that preceded it. Thucydides records speech that leads toward collective action, though he does not give us a neutral transcript of every exchange. The Athenian-Melian encounter shows how institutions create a bridge between individual decisions and public violence. A delegation, an army, and an empire can transform an intention to harm into a policy. The violence then receives names such as security, necessity, punishment, or order. Those names do not erase the harm; they determine how the agents understand and record it.

Aggression is harmful behaviour directed toward another person or group. It may be physical, verbal, or psychological, and it need not arise from anger. Interpersonal violence concerns harm inflicted between individuals, including assaults within families, partnerships, friendships, workplaces, or encounters between strangers. The arrow in Ötzi’s shoulder is evidence of interpersonal violence in this broad sense, even though the identities and relationship of the people involved remain unknown. This definition describes the relation of the actors, not the size of the society around them.

Raiding is more specific. It is an organised armed incursion intended to achieve a limited purpose without permanently holding the place attacked. Raiders may seize goods, destroy an installation, take captives, kill or capture a particular person, or withdraw after gathering intelligence. Its defining feature is the limited operational form: enter, strike, and leave rather than occupy and administer. A raid can be brief and still produce death, enslavement, displacement, and long memory. The evidence from Ötzi does not establish a raid, because the arrow wound reveals neither a group nor a purpose of that kind.

Warfare is organised armed conflict between political communities or other organised parties, usually sustained through command, recruitment, weapons, logistics, and strategic aims. It includes battles but is not limited to them. Siege, patrol, supply, forced movement, and the threat of attack belong to warfare’s wider field. The Athenians at Melos were conducting warfare before the siege began, because the diplomatic meeting occurred within a conflict between political communities and served the objectives of an imperial campaign.

Warfare is violence connected to collective purposes and organised means, carried out by many people.

Coercive law belongs to a different category, although it also relies on the possibility of harm. Hammurabi’s penalties threaten bodily injury, death, or financial loss in order to regulate conduct. A court may impose force after an offence, while a law may deter conduct through the known prospect of punishment. The act of legal coercion is not identical to the act that prompted it. A person who breaks another’s bone commits interpersonal violence; a court that orders compensation or bodily retaliation performs coercive law. The two may be connected in time and still require separate descriptions.

Atrocity names a further condition: severe, large-scale, or systematic violence directed against civilian populations or other protected groups, often through organised policy. Genocide requires intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. Crimes against humanity concern widespread or systematic attacks on civilians. War crimes are grave violations committed in armed conflict. Ethnic cleansing describes the forcible removal of a population from a territory, though its legal status depends on the acts used and the circumstances. These terms describe patterns of harm, the status of victims, and the organisation behind the attack, without being interchangeable with aggression.

The categories overlap without collapsing. A raid may become warfare when repeated incursions serve a sustained political conflict. A military campaign may include atrocities. A law may punish interpersonal violence while itself using corporal violence. The same killing can be described as aggression at the level of conduct, as an act of warfare at the level of organised conflict, and as an atrocity if it forms part of a systematic attack on civilians. Each description answers a different question.

The three cases make this visible through their records. Ötzi offers the body of a victim and the material trace of a weapon. Hammurabi offers rules that sort injury by status and responsibility. Thucydides offers a constructed account of political speech preceding military action. Each source preserves a different stage at which harm becomes knowable: the wound, the classification, or the decision. None supplies a complete account of motive.

The arrowhead near Ötzi’s shoulder is the smallest record and, in one respect, the most direct. Its shape, location, and relation to the damaged artery allow forensic investigators to infer how the wound was produced. The evidence is physical and resistant to the wish of later communities to justify or condemn the attack. Yet the arrow has no language. It cannot say whether the shooter believed Ötzi was an enemy, a thief, a rival, or a danger. It cannot tell us whether anyone ordered the shooting, whether others watched, or whether the dead man’s community sought revenge.

The wound is direct evidence of injury and indirect evidence of society.

Hammurabi’s monument moves from action to language. Its repeated structure—if a person does this, then this penalty follows—turns social conflict into a series of recognisable legal forms. Injury to an equal does not occupy the same place in the code as injury to a dependent. Harm caused by negligence receives a different treatment again. The text’s vocabulary exposes an order in which status was part of the legal fact. The body of a victim entered judgment already marked by rank. That feature prevents the Code from serving as a simple record of humanitarian restraint. Its rules may have sought to limit cycles of retaliation by specifying penalties, but they also authorised unequal valuations. The institution could convert a wound into a payment and thereby reduce the need for a private feud. It could also make the injury of a poor person count for less than the injury of a socially superior man. Law organised violence by replacing some forms of personal action with authorised judgment while retaining hierarchy in the judgment itself.

The Melian Dialogue adds the language of political necessity. The Athenians do not deny that the Melians have a case in moral terms; they set moral argument aside by insisting that power determines what can be enforced. Their decision is an imperial calculation articulated by officials before military force is applied, carried out by soldiers. The Melians deliberate as well. They weigh submission against independence, justice against survival, and expected Spartan help against the danger of standing alone.

Institutional violence includes argument among those who may suffer it and those who will carry it out.

Thucydides then records the consequence in compressed form: the Melians refuse to submit, the Athenians besiege the island, and after its capture the adult men are killed while women and children are enslaved. The decision made in speech becomes a policy enforced by armed power and completed through the treatment of a population according to Athenian choice. The language of necessity has helped determine the target, sequence, and authorised outcome. The institutional decision defines who may be killed, who may be enslaved, and what political explanation will surround the acts.

The contrast with Ötzi is not between a primitive individual and an advanced state. Ötzi lived in a society capable of specialised tools, long-distance movement, and organised production, as his copper axe and carefully made equipment indicate. The case shows how difficult the social setting becomes to recover when the surviving record is a body in ice, rather than an isolated human being outside society. Melos, by contrast, preserves a historian’s account of representatives speaking within an imperial war, so the surrounding decisions are visible in a way they are not for the Alpine killing.

Hammurabi stands between them. The law does not preserve a particular victim’s story, but it makes social structure explicit. A person’s rank changes the penalty; the nature of the injury changes the response; intention and professional responsibility alter the judgment. The text allows us to see the institution at work even when the individual event has disappeared. The body tells us what happened to someone. The law tells us what a kingdom was prepared to recognise and impose. The dialogue tells us how a power defended a decision before acting. This distinction guards against a common error in writing the history of violence: treating every wound as evidence for a general theory of human nature. Ötzi was killed. Hammurabi’s society contained rules for injury. Athens used force against Melos. These facts do not establish that humans are uniformly aggressive, nor that organised societies merely magnify an unchanging instinct. Cooperation, exchange, protection, ritual, and law also shaped the worlds in which these events occurred. The surviving records preserve harm, classification, and coercion, not every peaceful relation that left no comparable trace.

Institutional organisation does not automatically make violence more severe. A legal penalty may replace a feud, and a negotiated surrender may prevent a siege. An army may enforce peace in one place and destroy a population in another. The relevant question is how a particular institution identifies a threat, assigns responsibility, authorises force, and records the result. Hammurabi’s code and the Athenian policy at Melos both show organised power, but they organise harm through different procedures and toward different ends.

For the pages that follow, the working unit will be neither a timeless human instinct nor a body count detached from context. An act of aggression is harmful behaviour. Interpersonal violence concerns harm between people. Raiding is a limited armed incursion. Warfare is sustained organised conflict among political communities or comparable parties. Coercive law is authorised pressure or punishment used to compel conduct or enforce a rule. Atrocity is severe, large-scale, or systematic violence directed against civilians or protected groups, identified through the pattern, purpose, and organisation of the acts. These definitions will sometimes meet in a single event. Ötzi’s shoulder wound begins with an act. Hammurabi’s penalties show how a society can classify such acts and answer them through law. Thucydides shows a political community discussing force, deploying it, and giving it a public rationale. Aggression belongs to the behaviour; institutions decide how behaviour is named, permitted, punished, expanded, and remembered.

## Chapter 2: Before States: Small Communities and the Archaeological Record

At Nataruk, on the western shore of Lake Turkana, the dead lay in ground that had once been close to water and later became part of a dry, open landscape. Marta Mirazón Lahr and her colleagues dated the remains broadly to the early Holocene, between about 9500 and 7500 BCE. The site was not a cemetery arranged in orderly rows. Bodies lay dispersed within a shallow area, some on their sides and some on their backs, while several apparently received none of the treatment that would have gathered them into a protected place. One adult male had been placed face down, his hands held together in front of the pelvis. The bones had not been disturbed as they would have been in a carefully reopened grave. They remained where the bodies were deposited until sediment covered them.

The injuries give the site its force. Stone projectile points were embedded in or lay close to the bones of several individuals, including fragments associated with the skull, thorax, and other parts of the skeleton. One point was lodged near an adult man's left knee. Other remains showed blunt-force trauma, fractures, and damage consistent with blows delivered around the time of death. The pattern does not resemble a single fall or the accumulation of bodies through ordinary burial. It includes men, women, and children, as well as a woman who was pregnant or carrying a fetus when she died, according to the archaeological and osteological study. In several cases, the position of a projectile connected a durable stone point directly with an injury rather than merely placing a tool beside human remains.

What happened at Nataruk?

The careful answer begins with an event rather than a social theory. A group attacked another group, or attacked people who could not escape, and at least twelve individuals died violently. The attackers used weapons capable of striking from a distance and at close range. Some victims may have been bound or otherwise unable to protect themselves, although the position of the hands in one burial does not establish restraint. The bodies were left in or near the place where the violence occurred instead of being gathered into a formal cemetery. The evidence supports inter-group aggression on a substantial scale. It does not establish armies, permanent war leaders, or a settled system of warfare at Nataruk.

The surrounding landscape helps explain how an encounter might have occurred without settling why it occurred. Nataruk was not a village surrounded by a wall. The people who died belonged to a mobile or seasonally mobile society living among lakes, rivers, marshes, and dry ground whose resources changed as climate and water levels shifted. The site lay close to what had probably been a lagoon or shallow lake margin during the period of occupation. Fishing, hunting, and collecting could bring separate groups into the same productive places, particularly when water and food were unevenly distributed. A fight over access to water, an encounter between groups already hostile, the seizure of people or supplies, or a retaliatory raid would all fit this setting more readily than a claim that aggression was a permanent way of life.

The weapons sharpen the reconstruction while leaving its purpose open. The projectile points were made from stone, and the study connected several with wounds. Their presence establishes that arrows or darts were used against human beings at the site. It does not establish how many attackers there were, whether they had planned the assault for days, or whether the same people had fought before. A weapon reveals the manner of an injury more securely than the social organisation behind it. Equipment used routinely for hunting could also serve in a sudden attack; the points do not distinguish those occasions.

The bodily evidence creates a second problem of scale. On an individual skeleton, an embedded point or a fracture can show that a violent act occurred. Across a cluster of skeletons, repeated wounds show that violence affected several people at the same place and time. The leap from that cluster to a general account of life in early Holocene West Turkana requires evidence that Nataruk does not preserve. The site holds the victims of one disastrous episode, not every person who lived beside the lake, every meeting between neighbouring groups, or every dispute that ended without injury. Nor does it tell us how often such attacks took place beyond the excavated area.

The remains are exceptional precisely as remains. Soft tissue disappeared. Wooden shafts, bindings, clothing, and carried provisions were not preserved in a form that allows a complete scene to be reconstructed. The points that survived were the durable parts of a weapon system, and the bones that survived were the durable parts of the human body. Even within that narrow archive, the preservation of trauma depended on the force and location of an injury, the condition of the bone, the movement of sediment, and the work of later excavation. A cut to the abdomen or a blow that left no fracture could have been fatal yet archaeologically silent.

The Nataruk study supports several statements with different degrees of reach. People were killed violently at the site. The attack involved projectiles and close violence and affected more than one category of person. The distribution of bodies and wounds makes an episode of inter-group conflict more plausible than a private quarrel between individuals. Beyond those claims, the ground becomes less firm. The event may indicate competition among mobile communities, but it cannot measure the frequency of such competition. It may resemble a massacre in the ordinary sense, yet the archaeological evidence cannot supply a political organisation equivalent to a state or a war-making institution.

The bones leave one question open: how much can one mass death tell us about a society whose ordinary life has almost entirely vanished? Nataruk demonstrates that small communities could inflict coordinated and lethal harm before cities, kings, and written laws. The episode remains unmistakably violent while providing a poor measure of how often violence occurred.

The excavation of Jebel Sahaba began with a different archaeological problem. Fred Wendorf described Site 117 in his account of Nubian Final Paleolithic archaeology after the cemetery had been exposed in the Nile Valley of what is now northern Sudan. The graves belonged to people who lived roughly 13,400 to 18,600 years ago, depending on the chronological framework used for the site. The cemetery contained dozens of individuals, traditionally counted as sixty-one, and the bones carried an unusually high number of injuries associated with stone projectiles. For decades, the site was presented as evidence of a single prehistoric battle or massacre. A later re-examination made that history less simple.

The cemetery lay near the former course and floodplain of the Nile, where the river's movements and the advance of arid conditions shaped the possibilities of settlement. Wendorf's report described graves cut into sediment and bodies placed in burial positions, with stone artefacts among the remains. Some burials contained more than one person. Unlike the dispersed bodies proposed for Nataruk, these dead had been placed in a cemetery. The surviving evidence therefore includes both the violence that killed or injured people and the decisions made by survivors about where to put them.

The bones were not all alike. Some contained injuries that had healed, showing that their owners survived an earlier wound. Others displayed lesions that had not healed and were compatible with injury close to death. Points and fragments lay in association with bodies. Men, women, and children were affected. A later analysis reported that about two-thirds of the individuals had weapon-related injuries, while healed and unhealed wounds together suggested repeated episodes rather than one encounter involving everyone at once. That pattern changes the reconstruction of the cemetery. It need not be the burial ground of one army defeated on one day. People may have returned to it after separate violent incidents, placing their dead beside earlier burials. Some individuals who were wounded in one encounter recovered and later died in another event or of another cause. The cemetery accumulated a history of danger. Its concentration of wounded people may reflect violence unfolding over years or generations rather than one battle.

The re-analysis published in 2021 linked this recurrence to climatic instability and ecological stress in the Nile Valley. As water and habitable land became less predictable, groups using the same corridor may have encountered one another more often or competed over increasingly restricted places. Such conditions can help explain why violence recurred, but they do not prove that scarcity caused each wound. Climate is a setting in which choices occur, not a motive preserved inside a rib or a projectile point. The bodies show what violence did; they do not identify the decision that sent one group against another.

Jebel Sahaba complicates the category of warfare as well. The cemetery contains evidence of repeated attacks with ranged weapons, and the injuries affected a population rather than one isolated adult. Those facts suggest recurrent conflict among groups. The site does not reveal a command structure, a territorial state, or a campaign season. The people buried there may have belonged to mobile communities that fought intermittently over movement routes, fishing grounds, or access to the river. Their conflict could have been organised without resembling a later army. The absence of ranks and fortifications does not establish peace, while many projectile wounds do not prove formal war.

Burial supplies another layer of evidence. Someone survived each episode long enough to recover the injured where recovery was possible, transport or carry bodies to the cemetery, and place the dead in graves. That work indicates social continuity amid danger. The same community that experienced repeated violence maintained a practice of burial. Jebel Sahaba therefore holds two histories at once: attacks that injured and killed people, and collective actions that treated the dead as members of a continuing group. The cemetery records recurrence alongside destruction.

Nataruk and Jebel Sahaba both contain projectile injuries, but they pose different problems for the historian. At Nataruk, the central task is to reconstruct one violent episode from bodies left in a place that may have been close to the attack. At Jebel Sahaba, the task is to separate many episodes deposited in one cemetery. One site concentrates an event in space; the other may concentrate a history in space. Similar weapons do not make the two social histories identical.

Nataruk offers the clearer picture of bodies abandoned after an attack. The adult male with his hands placed together in front of the pelvis, the pregnant woman, the children, and the stone points associated with wounds make the violence difficult to reduce to accident. The positions of the bodies and the lack of a regular cemetery arrangement support the idea that the dead were not buried through a sustained communal rite immediately after the attack. The site preserves the suddenness of a disaster more readily than its aftermath. It may represent a raid, an ambush, or a massacre of people encountered in a vulnerable setting.

Jebel Sahaba offers a clearer picture of repeated exposure. Healed injuries carry a time dimension that Nataruk largely lacks. A healed lesion shows that a person was wounded, survived, and remained alive long enough for bone to repair. An unhealed lesion places another injury close to death or burial. When these marks occur among many members of one cemetery, they create a pattern of recurrence. The pattern does not count every violent encounter, since only certain injuries preserve and only some injured people were buried at the site, but it moves beyond one isolated death.

The burial arrangements alter the inference. At Jebel Sahaba, the cemetery indicates that the dead were gathered and cared for. At Nataruk, dispersed bodies suggest that such care was absent, delayed, or interrupted. The difference may reflect the circumstances of the attack rather than different attitudes toward the dead. A group surprised at a lakeshore could leave bodies behind; a returning community could reclaim its dead. Burial is evidence of social action after violence, and that action may reveal the survivors' circumstances more than their general level of aggression.

The projectiles raise a related problem. At both sites, points associated with human remains are stronger evidence than loose points found in a settlement. Yet a point identifies a weapon and a wound more securely than an attacker, target, or objective. The same technology that served hunting could be turned against people. At Nataruk, projectile wounds across women, children, and men support an attack involving several victims. At Jebel Sahaba, the concentration of wounds across a cemetery supports repeated conflict. Neither site tells us whether attackers were specialists, whether every adult carried a weapon, or how violence was authorised within the group.

Patterned mass death and individual wounds are not interchangeable forms of proof. An isolated skull fracture may establish interpersonal violence if its shape, timing, and context rule out accident, but it says little about the number of participants or the duration of hostility. A mass grave with consistent trauma can establish that many people died violently in one episode or a short sequence, but it may still not reveal whether the attackers formed a permanent fighting group. Jebel Sahaba adds healed injuries, allowing recurrence to enter the argument, though not as a numerical rate for the entire population.

Nataruk is strongest for the fact of a lethal attack involving multiple victims. Jebel Sahaba is strongest for a history of repeated violent encounters affecting a community over time. Nataruk's weakness is duration; Jebel Sahaba's is the uncertain number and spacing of episodes represented by one cemetery. Neither site converts exceptional death into a measure of ordinary conduct. A society may experience a rare catastrophe, or it may endure frequent attacks, and the same grave can preserve the dead in both circumstances. Nor does the difference permit a simple progress story. Jebel Sahaba is older than Nataruk, but its age does not make it a primitive version of later warfare. Nataruk is later, yet its weapons and social setting place it outside any progression toward states. The archaeological record preserves conditions and consequences of violence across the varied development of societies from individual aggression to organised war. Organisation can exist in small groups, and large institutions can restrain violence as well as amplify it.

The Talheim grave invites a different argument because its bodies belong to a later Neolithic farming world. Around 5000 BCE, near Talheim in what is now southwestern Germany, at least thirty-four individuals were placed in a mass grave. Christian Meyer and his colleagues examined the skeletons and found widespread perimortem injuries, especially severe blows to the skull. The victims included men, women, and children. The grave contained no ordinary arrangement of individual burials and no indication that the dead had been placed there after a long period of normal cemetery use. The bones indicate a concentrated episode in which a community or group suffered collective death.

A large grave beside a farming settlement seems to invite a ready conclusion: a village was attacked by migrants, neighbours fought over land and food, or a demographic rupture destroyed one population and scattered its survivors. Each possibility has a plausible historical shape. Early farming communities occupied fields, stored crops, inherited houses, and depended on places that could not be moved as easily as a fishing camp or hunting party. Population growth could bring neighbouring settlements closer together. If newcomers entered a region, conflict over land might follow; if a poor harvest reduced supplies, a full store could become a reason for attack. Talheim can fit all these stories.

The evidence does not choose among them so quickly. Meyer and his colleagues reconstructed the injuries as a massacre in which victims were struck repeatedly, often on the head, with blunt weapons. The blows were delivered around the time of death, and the absence of healed trauma in the grave population did not suggest a group that had repeatedly survived attacks before this event. Stone axes or adze-like tools may have caused some injuries, but precise weapons cannot be assigned to every wound. The dead were deposited together, and the grave appears to have been closed after the killing.

Migration enters the discussion through more than the movement of people imagined by the modern reader. Neolithic Europe was connected by pottery styles, stone materials, exchange networks, and the spread of farming practices, but shared objects do not identify the people who carried them. Biological ancestry, strontium isotopes, and settlement distributions can help test whether individuals came from elsewhere, whether communities were mobile, and whether a regional population had recently changed. At Talheim, this evidence has been used in debates about whether the victims represented a local population caught in a wider movement of Linearbandkeramik peoples. The grave itself cannot reveal the direction of migration or the identity of the attackers.

A demographic explanation gains substance when attached to settlement evidence. Early Linearbandkeramik settlements were often small clusters of longhouses distributed along fertile loess soils and waterways. Houses could be occupied, rebuilt, and abandoned over periods that are difficult to synchronise precisely. A regional gap may indicate movement, later destruction, or incomplete archaeological discovery. If Talheim stood within a densely occupied corridor, competition among nearby communities becomes more plausible. If it lay at the edge of a newly expanding settlement zone, another kind of encounter becomes possible. The grave cannot supply the map.

Alexander Bentley and his colleagues emphasised mobility, population movement, and the danger of treating Neolithic violence as a straightforward struggle between fixed villages. Their commentary resisted the assumption that the Linearbandkeramik spread represented one uniform population moving across Europe in a single process. Isotopic and genetic evidence can identify differences among individuals and communities, but those differences must be connected to dates and places before they become an account of Talheim. A person with a nonlocal isotopic signature may have moved during life; a local signature does not establish political separation from neighbouring groups.

Resource conflict remains possible, but it is not a conclusion extracted from a broken skull. Farming communities required land, water, labour, and stored food. Loess soils could produce wealth in some years and vulnerability in others. A raid might seek food, remove competitors, avenge an earlier killing, seize captives, or eliminate a settlement whose continued presence threatened a neighbouring group. These motives can overlap. Material pressure may make conflict more likely without dictating who attacked, why the target was chosen, or why the dead were buried in one place.

The victims complicate a simple raid for portable goods. Men, women, and children were killed. That pattern could mean that attackers encountered a settlement population rather than a fighting party, or that the purpose of the attack extended beyond taking animals or grain. It may indicate the destruction of a household group or the removal of a community from contested ground. It does not establish ethnic hostility, extermination as a policy, or a desire to seize land. Later state categories are too large for a grave whose social boundaries remain uncertain.

Talheim is not an isolated human deposit floating outside a landscape. Houses, pits, ceramics, animal bones, stone tools, cultivation traces, and pathways can reveal how people lived around the grave. Contemporary settlements can show whether the area was occupied continuously, whether sites were abandoned, and whether the attack occurred within a changing frontier. A mass burial gains historical meaning when placed among those traces. Without them, it can prove killing but not the demographic rupture that killing may have caused.

One account imagines migrants entering a region and attacking residents who resisted. Another imagines local farmers fighting over increasingly scarce land or food. A third sees the episode as part of a demographic collapse in which settlements were abandoned and survivors relocated. Each explanation can incorporate Talheim's victims. The first requires evidence of population movement and cultural transition; the second requires signs of competition and contemporaneous settlement density; the third requires regional abandonment rather than one grave alone. The grave provides the event. Other evidence must carry the cause.

The injuries answer some parts of the dispute. They show that the victims were killed by repeated blows, probably with hard tools, and that the violence was directed at people who were not simply injured in an accident or buried after an ordinary battle. The concentration of dead in one grave indicates a collective disaster. The wounds do not identify the attackers' place of origin, show whether the attack was planned by a village council, kin group, or temporary coalition, or establish that food shortage preceded it. Those questions remain historical rather than anatomical.

Talheim differs from Jebel Sahaba in the social world surrounding the dead. At Jebel Sahaba, the main issue is recurrence in a cemetery used by a group exposed to repeated projectile violence. At Talheim, it is the cause and demographic meaning of one concentrated massacre among farming communities whose houses and fields could anchor people to particular places. Agriculture makes resource conflict imaginable but not inevitable. Sedentary settlement can intensify competition and create exchange, marriage ties, and negotiated boundaries. The same fields that produce rivalry can support cooperation.

Talheim records a massacre affecting a substantial group in a Neolithic farming landscape. Its victims were killed in a concentrated episode, and the manner of injury suggests deliberate collective violence. Settlement, isotopic, genetic, and regional demographic evidence may help determine whether migration, competition, or social rupture best explains the setting. None of those explanations is established merely because the grave is dramatic. One violent event can destroy a community without representing the ordinary conduct of the communities around it.

A historian looking only at the dead would miss the larger landscape. The missing settlement is one of the main limits on interpretation. At Nataruk, no complete camp survives to show how the victims lived together, what they carried, or whether the lakeshore was a temporary meeting place. At Jebel Sahaba, the cemetery preserves burial practices more clearly than the domestic sites of the people who used it. At Talheim, the grave survives with greater force than the network of houses, fields, paths, and neighbouring groups that might explain why an attack occurred there.

Archaeological absence has several causes. Houses built from wood, reeds, and packed earth can collapse into traces too thin to detect. Floods can move or bury deposits. Later farmers can plough away shallow features. Rivers change course, shorelines retreat, and deserts cover former camps. A site may remain undiscovered because survey has not reached it or because its material looks ordinary beside a grave that commands attention. The absence of settlement evidence therefore cannot be read as the absence of settlement.

Uninjured bodies are missing in a different way. The people who survived Nataruk did not leave a count of themselves beside the dead. The Jebel Sahaba cemetery may contain many of those who died or were buried there, but it does not contain everyone who lived with them. At Talheim, the grave does not tell us how many members of the affected community escaped, were absent, or were killed elsewhere. A population in which one hundred people lived peacefully and thirty-four were massacred would leave a different social history from a population of thirty-four people destroyed in its entirety, although the grave could look similar in the crucial respects.

The uninjured are archaeologically uneven as well. A blow may kill without breaking bone; an arrow may strike soft tissue; a wounded person may recover without a mark that survives. Those who die later from infection may be buried in an ordinary grave and never connected with the original attack. People who flee may carry the event into another settlement whose remains have yet to be found, so the skeletons establish selected consequences of violence while the event's full human reach extends beyond the surviving archaeological record. This is why an injury rate within a cemetery cannot become a violence rate for a society. At Jebel Sahaba, about two-thirds of the examined individuals showed weapon-related trauma, a striking concentration that supports recurrent danger at or around the community represented there. It does not mean that two-thirds of all people in the Nile Valley were wounded. At Nataruk, women and children among the dead establish the breadth of one attack, not the proportion exposed to violence in every season. At Talheim, the grave's size establishes the scale of one catastrophe, not the frequency of massacres among Linearbandkeramik communities.

The record can still answer important questions. It can distinguish a projectile wound from a burial without visible trauma when context and bone damage support the distinction. It can show whether injuries healed. It can reveal whether bodies were gathered, arranged, or left where they fell. It can identify weapons when points remain embedded or closely associated with bone. It can place the dead beside houses, fields, river channels, or other traces when those traces survive. Each answer has a proper reach.

Beyond that reach lies explanation. Migration may be tested through isotopes, genetics, settlement distributions, and material change. Resource conflict may be considered alongside climate, subsistence, storage, land use, and population density. Organisation may be inferred from the number of attackers, the coordination implied by simultaneous wounds, or the construction of defences, but none of these alone supplies a chain of command. Frequency requires repeated sites, dated sequences, and ordinary settlements as well as spectacular graves.

Nataruk records abandoned bodies after a lethal attack. Jebel Sahaba records a cemetery marked by repeated injuries and repeated burial. Talheim records a massacre whose demographic setting remains contested. Their differences prevent the archaeological record from becoming a simple graph of human aggressiveness. The dead establish that prehistoric people could attack one another with lethal coordination, sometimes repeatedly and sometimes against whole groups. They leave the larger question—how often ordinary communities chose violence, and how often they avoided it—to evidence that is quieter, more scattered, and much harder to find.

## Chapter 3: Cities, Kings, and the Invention of Organized War

Cities did not turn aggression into warfare merely by placing more people behind walls. A wall may protect a settlement, mark a boundary, or control water; it does not show who commanded the men above it or supplied the labour that repaired it. The decisive change lay in the ability of a palace or temple to gather grain, count workers, assign offices, preserve decisions, and make an order effective after the speaker had gone. At Ur, Lagash, and Akkad, violence acquired a duration that a household quarrel or a small raiding party could not possess. A campaign could be prepared through stores and personnel, then remembered in an inscription that turned force into a royal achievement.

Agricultural surplus made this possible, but surplus by itself explains too little. Barley in a storehouse is food; barley measured, allotted, and redirected to boatmen, metalworkers, scribes, or soldiers becomes a political resource. The change rested in offices and procedures. A city that could receive produce, record quantities, feed dependants, and demand labour could maintain people who did not cultivate fields during the season when an army marched. A ruler who appointed an overseer and punished an evader could make military service recurrent rather than exceptional. Local initiative remained, but it now moved within a superior command that supplied a schedule and a destination.

Charles Tilly’s formula—war made the state and the state made war—describes later European states more closely than the first cities of southern Mesopotamia. There kingship, temple administration, irrigation, and warfare developed together rather than in a single sequence. Yet Tilly identifies a real connection. The ruler needed grain and labour to campaign; a campaign brought captives, animals, land, and prestige; those gains enlarged the stores and dependants through which another campaign could be mounted. Drought, rebellion, defeat, or a succession crisis could break the cycle, but the offices that collected and distributed resources allowed it to resume.

Earlier chapters showed why archaeological remains cannot measure everyday aggression directly. Ötzi’s arrow wound establishes an attack without revealing an army. The mass graves of Nataruk, Jebel Sahaba, and Talheim establish lethal encounters without naming an office that ordered them. A Sumerian administrative text can name a functionary, a levy, a ration, or an obligation. Its categories do not recover every violent act, but they reveal the channels through which a ruler could make violence repeatable. When the Standard of Ur places a king above ordered ranks of fighters and captives, it shows a society in which bodies, goods, and commands have been arranged into a sequence the city can recognise.

The object commonly called the Standard of Ur is itself uncertain in purpose. Leonard Woolley excavated it in the Royal Cemetery at Ur in the 1920s. Its wooden core had decayed, and the surviving mosaic of shell, red limestone, and lapis lazuli required reconstruction. The British Museum record dates it to about 2600 BCE. Its two broad faces are traditionally called “War” and “Peace”, though it may have been a standard carried in procession, a sound box, or another ceremonial object. Its original use remains unsettled. Its images are clear enough, however, to show how command, organised force, and abundance were joined.

On the war face, the lowest register contains the defeated. Men lie beneath moving chariots; others appear bound or led away. Above them, soldiers advance in line. Their shields overlap, their spears point in a common direction, and their bodies form a ranked formation rather than a confused crowd. In the upper register the king, larger than the soldiers around him, stands at the centre. Attendants and armed men stand behind him; prisoners approach from before. The ruler’s scale makes rank visible, ensuring that the king cannot be confused with the men whose labour produces victory.

Each register moves from force towards possession. Chariots pass over the fallen; infantry control the field; prisoners are brought towards the ruler. The order is chronological enough to suggest action, but it is also administrative. Someone has distinguished the dead from the living, the armed from the captured, and the royal figure from the rank and file. The artists have not represented battle as an unbounded eruption. They have represented its aftermath as a controlled passage of bodies through categories.

The opposite face is usually called Peace, though the image does not prove that it records a formal peace. At the bottom, men carry baskets, animals, and provisions. In the middle register, figures bring goods in procession. At the top, the king sits at a banquet with high-ranking companions while a musician plays nearby. Food and drink form the counterpart of the battle scene. Goods move towards the centre just as captives do, and the king presides over both movements. War produces the conditions for feasting; administration makes the products legible and distributable.

The pairing is more exact than an opposition between violence and peace. The two faces show two outcomes of royal command. In one, organised men defeat and deliver captives. In the other, producers and carriers deliver goods for consumption in a ceremony of rank. The same hierarchy appears in both scenes. At the banquet, ruler and elite occupy the elevated field while bearers remain below. The object offers no picture of an equal community celebrating together. It shows access to the king arranged by rank.

Weapons matter because they are held by men placed in formation. Chariots matter because animals, drivers, craftsmen, and supplies have made them usable. Captives matter because they appear as a managed result rather than merely as victims of an encounter. The visual grammar joins equipment to personnel and personnel to command. A bronze blade can injure one person; a ranked unit can take territory, guard a convoy, and escort prisoners. The Standard preserves the second fact more strongly than the first. Its silence sets the limits of its evidence. The object does not name the enemy, specify the disputed field, record the number of dead, or explain why the campaign began. It shows what royal victory was made to mean: ordered troops, captured enemies, transported goods, and a ruler at the point where these flows converge. The banquet implies routes, handlers, stores, and accounting even though it displays no ledger. The seated guests and nearby musician imply a court sustained by the labour represented below them. The royal meal is an image of extraction.

When Ur’s artists placed battle beside abundance, they gave organised war a social foundation. The city could represent violence as one operation within a wider order: fighters advance, captives are delivered, goods are collected, and elites consume under royal supervision. Although the images cannot prove that every campaign followed this sequence, they show that a ruling house wanted military success understood in these terms. Earlier graves establish the possibility of lethal attack; the Standard supplies a vocabulary in which attack has become a public function of the city. That function depended on offices. The palace needed people who could measure grain, assign work, supervise transport, maintain animals, make weapons, and communicate demands. The Reform Texts attributed to Urukagina of Lagash, written around 2350 BCE, bring these less glorious activities into view. Their purpose was to praise a restoration of proper order, not to offer an impartial account of Lagash. Their complaints nevertheless expose the places where power was exercised.

Urukagina presents himself as a ruler correcting conditions created by earlier officials. The texts describe men connected with the palace and temples taking access to fields, boats, animals, and other resources. They mention payments and exactions attached to funerals, marriages, fishing, and the use of goods. The surviving versions vary, and the tablets are damaged, but their recurring image is clear: offices meant to serve the city had become channels through which officials claimed produce and labour for themselves.

A complaint about a tax is also a statement about administrative reach. A levy can be collected only if someone knows who owes it, what counts as payment, where it must be delivered, and what follows refusal. The official at a gate or beside a canal links royal declaration to household compliance. When a fisherman pays for access to water, or a field yields a measured portion, the city has entered activities that might once have remained within family or neighbourhood control.

The texts describe this entry through correction. Urukagina claims that he restored the widow and orphan, limited the grasp of officials, and returned resources to their proper owners. The inscription alone cannot establish how broad those reforms were. Kings commonly represented accession as rescue from disorder. Yet boats, fields, herds, funerary practices, and labour appear because these were places where authority could be collected and contested.

Consider the labour obligation. A palace that demands work needs a schedule and an overseer. Men may be assigned to dig or repair canals, move materials, construct buildings, or serve in a campaign. Their absence from their fields creates a cost that must be managed through rations, exemptions, replacement labour, or pressure. The office recording the assignment therefore reaches into production twice: it takes time from the household and attaches that time to a public project. Military service is one possible destination. The same apparatus can sustain walls, roads, temples, and storehouses. Coercion becomes durable because the city can move labour between purposes.

Taxes perform a similar conversion. Grain taken from farmers can feed palace dependants; animals can supply transport; metal and textiles can equip soldiers and officials. Nothing in the Reform Texts permits a precise calculation of Lagash’s army, and the document does not provide a complete budget. It does show claims on production distributed through named social roles. Palace, temple, and official did not draw from surplus identically, but together they made surplus available for purposes that households could not undertake independently.

Urukagina’s attack on officials also reveals a problem within state formation. Once an office can collect a payment, its holder may treat the collection as a private entitlement. A superintendent controlling boats, herds, or workers may obstruct reform, conceal quantities, or turn a public demand into personal profit. The ruler must discipline the officials who make his power possible. Command over subordinate agents has to be maintained before coercion can remain continuous. This explains why royal inscriptions so often describe restoration rather than invention. Urukagina claimed to have returned taxation, labour, and office-holding to a just order. His language converts administrative conflict into royal legitimacy: the king protects the weak by restraining the collector and presents extraction as lawful when properly supervised. The system that presses households can also be portrayed as the remedy for arbitrary pressure.

Military power depended on this distinction between authorised and unauthorised taking. A soldier seizing grain for a campaign may be fulfilling a royal demand; an official diverting the same grain to his household is accused of abuse. The physical act looks similar from below. Its political meaning changes according to who ordered it, who recorded it, and where the goods went. Institutions decide who may be harmed, and whose taking counts as service rather than theft.

Lagash adds an interior view to the Standard of Ur’s public image. Ur’s object shows the completed hierarchy: king, soldiers, captives, bearers, banquet. Urukagina’s text reveals the friction needed to maintain it. Officials had to be watched, taxes defined, labour assigned, and exemptions claimed. The army rested on the same offices that measured fields, controlled boats, and demanded work.

Naram-Sin’s Victory Stele, made around 2250 BCE, presents the royal claim in another form. The monument, now in the Louvre, commemorates an Akkadian victory over the Lullubi of the Zagros. Its carved surface rises diagonally towards a mountain peak. Naram-Sin climbs with armed followers while defeated enemies fall or plead below. Astral symbols appear above him, and he wears a horned crown normally associated with divinity. The stele places Naram-Sin’s victory within a cosmic order and makes the king the visible hinge between army and gods.

The sloping composition directs the eye upwards. At the lower left, soldiers advance behind their ruler. Their bodies are smaller and more regular than his, and their movement follows the incline. Naram-Sin stands apart, equipped with bow, axe, and spear. He leads the army into the mountain landscape rather than facing it in a static command scene. The defeated occupy the lower right: some are pierced or falling, while another raises his hands. Naram-Sin is the figure through whom the campaign becomes a world-defining event.

The horned crown changes the statement. Earlier royal images could enlarge a ruler without making him divine. Here the horns attach Naram-Sin’s body to a sign of godhood. His military action is represented as more than successful violence by an exceptional commander. It carries divine sanction, perhaps even divine power. The inscription associated with the stele calls him the mighty, god of Akkad, and records his victory over the Lullubi. Royal title and visual form reinforce one another.

Divine kingship did not feed an army. The soldiers behind Naram-Sin required recruitment, weapons, food, animals, and routes through difficult terrain. The stele is not a logistical report; it turns logistics into an attribute of the ruler. Where the Standard of Ur showed the transfer of goods and captives, the Victory Stele concentrates the process in a single body moving upwards with an army.

The conquest formula extends beyond the battlefield. A defeated mountain people become a named object of royal action, and the landscape separating Akkad from them becomes part of the king’s domain of representation. The stele does not record the length of the campaign or the size of the force. It records a ruler whose reach crosses a difficult frontier. Tribute and submission are implied by the posture of the defeated and the monument’s claim to victory, though actual quantities are absent.

Akkadian expansion required more than one battle. Sargon, Naram-Sin’s grandfather, had already built a territorial kingship claiming authority over numerous cities and regions. Under Naram-Sin, the title “king of the four quarters” expressed universal ambition, while rebellions tested the ability to hold distant places. Divine language answered that problem symbolically. If conquest was sanctioned by the gods, resistance could be represented not as a conflict between equal rulers but as opposition to the order embodied by Naram-Sin.

The empire nevertheless depended on governors, garrisons, routes, supplies, and communication across a wide territory. Governors could collect tribute only if local producers and elites recognised force behind the demand. Garrisons needed food; roads and passes had to remain usable; rebellion could interrupt the movement of grain or metal before an army arrived. The divine formula concealed these difficulties while addressing them symbolically. The king was made present where he could not stand through titles, images, officials, and the expectation of punishment.

The Victory Stele makes three connected claims. Naram-Sin possesses extraordinary personal power; the army moves as an extension of that power; and conquest is validated by the gods. The army’s material functions remain beneath the surface, but the monument requires us to imagine them. Soldiers march, weapons are supplied, captives controlled, and tribute or labour made available. Officials carry the king’s victory into administrative practice.

The treatment of the enemy is especially revealing. Some figures are dead, one is falling, and one turns upwards towards the ruler. Their individuality is limited, but they remain identifiable as defeated opponents whose bodies prove success. Captives in the Standard of Ur move towards the ruler; the Lullubi remain fixed in collapse. Both representations make human bodies evidence for royal capacity. Naram-Sin’s monument places that evidence in a cosmic landscape rather than a civic procession.

The stele adds a sacred explanation to the practical reasons soldiers obey: rations, discipline, reward, and punishment. To follow Naram-Sin is represented as moving with the chosen ruler of Akkad. As a royal representation made for an audience, it presents the campaign through the concerns of the ruling house. Its force lies in the public connection between military success and legitimate kingship. A campaign that brought captives and tribute could be remembered as proof of divine status; divine status could authorise the next demand for soldiers and resources.

Organised war should not be reduced to the presence of a standing army. The Akkadian ruler’s power rested on a chain including conquest, appointment, collection, transport, and display. The army might be assembled from different populations and supported by local obligations. A conquered city could provide grain, labour, or recruits without being emptied or destroyed. Tribute made submission useful to the victor and burdensome to the subject. The monument records the triumphal end of this process while administration kept it alive.

Naram-Sin’s kingship was personal and bureaucratic at once. The king appears alone at the centre, but the empire could not operate through his body alone. His name had to travel; his title had to be recognised; officials had to collect; soldiers had to enforce. Divine conquest supplied a language for these dispersed acts. It transformed demands made by governors and soldiers into the will of a sacred king, and successful extraction into evidence that the gods favoured the system.

The image does not establish how many people died in the Lullubi campaign or show the ordinary consequences for villages in the Zagros. Its evidence lies in the political work performed by royal art. Conquest becomes durable when it can be repeated in stone, attached to a title, connected to a god, and used to explain why bodies and goods must continue moving towards the centre. The stele is part of the machinery it represents.

Ur, Lagash, and Akkad were not three versions of one state. Their similarities arose from the pressures of urban rule; their differences show that surplus and hierarchy could be joined to coercion in more than one way. Ur’s Standard of Ur presents a city-centred kingship in which battle and feasting form paired scenes. The ruler stands at the head of a local hierarchy, and the goods entering the banquet suggest a palace able to organise production and distribution. Its world is compact: army, captives, procession, and court belong to a civic centre whose authority is made visible through proximity.

Lagash’s Reform Texts reveal a more troubled civic order. Urukagina’s concern is not the distant frontier but the official at the point of collection. Taxes, labour, boats, fields, herds, and funerary payments bring palace and temple into household life. The ruler’s power depends on these exactions, yet he must describe them as abuses when they escape his control. Lagash shows administration as conflict within the state. The official is both instrument of royal power and possible predator upon the people that power claims to protect.

Akkad extended the scale. Naram-Sin’s Victory Stele places the king and his army against the Lullubi of the Zagros and gives conquest a divine form. The problem is no longer only how a city feeds a court or restrains an officeholder. It is how a ruler makes claims across distant regions and turns military success into universal kingship. The army carries Akkadian authority outward; the monument and royal title carry its meaning back through the empire.

The three cases also differ in how they make violence recordable. The Standard sorts combatants, captives, goods, and diners into visual ranks. The Lagash text records grievances through offices and payments. The Victory Stele condenses conquest into the king’s sacred body and the enemy’s fall. None is a neutral report, but each preserves a different administrative problem. Ur displays circulation between battle and court; Lagash justifies regulation of collectors; Akkad legitimises rule over conquered distance.

Did state formation increase violence, redirect it, or make it more visible? The evidence supports all three, though not equally everywhere. Urban administration increased the potential scale and duration of coercion because it could sustain specialists, gather provisions, and replace losses. It redirected violence towards politically defined targets: rival cities, tributary populations, rebellious officials, and communities placed outside the ruler’s order. It also made violence more recordable by assigning it to offices, ranks, titles, and monuments. The record grew not because every violent act was written down, but because rulers needed selected acts to support claims about authority.

Administration could restrain as well as intensify. Urukagina’s complaints show a ruler limiting unauthorised extraction, at least in the language of reform. A city government could regulate payments and prevent some private retaliation. Restraint did not end coercion; it redirected coercion through approved channels. A household spared an official’s arbitrary demand might still owe labour to the palace. A captive spared immediate death might become a worker or tribute-bearing subject. The same apparatus could reduce one form of harm while extending another.

Population alone cannot explain this transformation. A large settlement does not automatically possess a durable army, and weapons do not create command. Surplus becomes military capacity only when someone can claim it, store it, distribute it, and defend the procedures by which it is claimed. Hierarchy becomes coercive capacity when ranks attach to offices, obligations, and punishments. The Standard’s bearers, Urukagina’s officials, and Naram-Sin’s soldiers belonged to different political settings, yet each case joined bodies to an ordered flow of goods and commands.

Earlier evidence showed that people could attack one another lethally before cities and kings. These Mesopotamian records show something more specific: a city could make attack part of a continuing public system. The palace supplied soldiers; the army secured or enlarged the palace’s claims; officials converted fields and labour into provisions; royal images converted victory into legitimacy. War was woven through the same administrative threads that fed courts, built temples, managed canals, and disciplined collectors.

The city did not merely house violence. At Ur, it joined captives and goods to a royal ceremony; at Lagash, it turned taxes and labour into obligations that officials could enforce; in Akkad, it attached conquest to Naram-Sin’s divine body. Administration made coercion continuous by giving it stores, offices, schedules, and a memory that could outlast the individuals who first carried it out.

## Chapter 4: Empires and the Management of Difference

In the ninth year of his reign, Mesha, king of Moab, set up a stone at Dhiban and had words cut into its surface. He began with Chemosh, the god of Moab, and with his own position as Chemosh’s chosen servant. Omri, king of Israel, had oppressed Moab for many days because Chemosh was angry with his land, Mesha says, and Omri’s son continued the same rule. Israel’s possession of towns east of the Dead Sea is thus placed inside a divine history: its king held them because Israel’s god had, for a time, prevailed over Chemosh.

Mesha’s revolt began with Madaba. The king of Israel had occupied the town, Mesha says, although the people of Gad had lived there from ancient days and Israel’s king had built the settlement. Mesha fought against Madaba, captured it, and killed its population, dedicating the place to Chemosh and to the people of Moab. The account passes rapidly from conquest to dedication, but the order is exact. The town is recovered, its inhabitants removed, and its political meaning transferred to the god and people of Moab.

At Nebo, Mesha says, Chemosh told him to take the town. He went against it at night, fought from dawn until noon, captured it, and killed all its inhabitants. Seven thousand people were put to death because Mesha had devoted them to Ashtar-Chemosh. Objects connected with Israel’s worship were carried before Chemosh, while the women and girls of Nebo were assigned to the sanctuary or to the service of the god. Military victory and religious purification are one act in the inscription. Population, property, and cult change hands together.

Jahaz presented a different task. Israel had fought against Moab there, and Mesha built a road before attacking the settlement. He names reservoirs, walls, towers, houses, and sheep pens among the works he completed. Israelite prisoners were captured, while men from Sharon and Maharath were settled in the land. The road carried soldiers toward the contested place, but it also connected the newly claimed territory to the wider work of rule. Mesha’s revolt became an occupation, and occupation required people who could hold and cultivate what the army had taken.

The stele gives the rebel’s answer to the language of empire. Moab was confronting a neighbouring kingdom rather than Assyria or Persia, but Mesha describes the same political problem: a ruler must explain why violence against an established order is rightful. Israelite rule followed Chemosh’s anger; Moabite rule returned when Chemosh favoured Mesha. Killings, dedications, and resettlement are presented as repair.

The stone records Mesha’s voice, yet its public location gave that voice a task beyond recounting campaigns. It told Moabites who had defeated them, who had restored their towns, and why the restored kingdom deserved obedience. Mesha’s first-person verbs—building, fighting, capturing, killing, and settling—join his person to the territory he claims. The end of Israelite tribute and the beginning of Moabite administration appear as one movement. Once Israel’s grip is broken, roads open, walls rise, and people are assigned places within Chemosh’s land. That account also exposes what royal records conceal. Mesha can call killing restoration only by defining the defeated population as part of the disorder being repaired. The inhabitants of Madaba and Nebo disappear into formulas of dedication; the people brought into Moab’s rebuilt settlements become evidence of successful rule. Moving people so that a territory can be governed is liberation in the victor’s account and dispossession in the account that the stele excludes. Mesha writes as the power that has broken one arrangement and is establishing another.

Imperial violence stabilizes rule only when it changes the daily terms of obedience. A defeated king sends tribute, a governor keeps office, a garrison controls a road, and farmers continue to cultivate fields under a new claimant. The battlefield decides who can impose terms. Administration decides whether those terms will endure. Grain, labour, prisoners, messages, and judgments must move through a territory whose inhabitants have reasons to resist.

Mesha’s stele speaks from the side of a smaller power recovering territory. Assyrian annals speak from the centre of a far larger kingdom. Persian royal inscriptions place rebellion inside an even wider geography, where peoples and languages are arranged beneath one king. Their political grammar remains recognisable: the rightful ruler faces a false claimant or disobedient province; punishment removes the disorder; victory restores the world to its proper condition.

Mesha could present the recovery of Moabite towns as a return to Chemosh’s land. Darius had to explain why peoples from Elam to Media, Babylonia, Armenia, and Scythia belonged within one king’s peace. Ashurbanipal had to make the ruin of Elam serve a similar purpose across generations of rivalry. Their violence was directed not only at bodies and settlements but at the memory of political alternatives.

A royal inscription calls rebellion a disease, a lie, or treachery because such terms make punishment appear curative. Darius says that Gaumata deprived the people of their king and that his removal restored legitimate rule. Ashurbanipal describes Elamite hostility as repeated violation of oaths and treaties, allowing the destruction of Susa to appear as the conclusion of a long case. Mesha describes Israelite occupation as the result of Chemosh’s anger and his campaigns as its correction.

If violence is aggression, the conquered may answer it with resistance. If violence is restoration, resistance becomes support for disorder. The vocabulary does not settle the result by itself. Assyrian kings repeatedly announced the pacification of places that revolted again. Darius’s Behistun narrative lists successive risings after the death of Cambyses, showing an empire restored in one passage and contested in the next episode. Mesha’s account makes his success substantial, but it does not reveal how long every recovered settlement remained secure or how displaced populations experienced the change.

Accommodation belonged to restoration as well. Persian kings could preserve local cults, languages, and officials while demanding taxes and loyalty. Assyria could relocate communities, use conquered elites, and leave agricultural routines in place. An empire that destroyed every intermediary would also destroy the people who collected grain, interpreted custom, and transmitted commands. Difference had to remain visible enough to administer, yet subordinate enough not to become an alternative centre of power.

Exemplary terror could warn governors and towns that rebellion carried a cost. It could also teach them that imperial rule offered no dependable place for negotiation. Tribute combined with local autonomy might make submission cheaper than revolt. The destruction of a city could instead make submission a pause while enemies gathered strength. Public punishment stabilised rule when it made obedience more credible; it renewed resistance when it made accommodation look useless.

Darius’s inscription at Behistun gives this problem a sequence. The text was carved high on a cliff in western Iran, above the road linking Mesopotamia with the Iranian plateau. Darius appears in relief with a bow, a line of defeated figures before him, and Ahuramazda above. The image places the king between the god and the bound rebels. The accompanying text explains how that arrangement came to exist.

Darius begins by naming himself, his lineage, and the extent of the kingdom granted to him. He is Darius, son of Hystaspes, an Achaemenid; by the favour of Ahuramazda, he rules many lands. Persia, Elam, Babylonia, Assyria, Arabia, Egypt, the peoples of the sea, and other regions appear in a royal geography that turns diversity into evidence of reach. The empire is first made legible as a list of peoples.

Then the succession breaks. Cambyses had gone to Egypt when Gaumata seized power in Persia. Gaumata concealed his action, claimed to be Bardiya, and deprived the people of their king. Darius converts a succession crisis into deception: the man in the palace was not entitled to occupy it, and those who obeyed him were living under false rule.

Darius and a small group of Persian nobles killed Gaumata at Sikayauvati. The act restored the king’s authority, but restoration did not end with the palace coup. Rebellions arose across the empire. Babylon rebelled under Nidintu-Bel; Elam recognised Martiya; Media followed Phraortes; Armenia, Parthia, Margiana, and other regions entered the account. The empire appears not as a territory quietly awaiting its rightful ruler but as a field of competing claims.

Darius records the order in which he defeated them. He marched against Nidintu-Bel and defeated him near the Tigris, then fought again near the Euphrates before taking Babylon. In Media he defeated Phraortes at Kunduru; the rebel fled east before being captured. The text repeatedly returns to the king’s movement, the rebel’s flight, and the battle that closes each challenge. Routes become evidence of royal power reaching the places where the kingdom has broken apart.

Punishment follows capture. Phraortes was mutilated and displayed at Ecbatana, Darius says, together with other leading rebels. The defeated body becomes a warning. The text does not dwell on pain or describe the act as cruelty. It presents the rebel’s fate as the visible completion of justice, as though the king’s order had been waiting for the proper body to occupy its place.

“Restored” belongs to the structure of the account even where a translation does not repeat the word. Darius restores the kingdom and the temples that had been destroyed or neglected; he restores the conditions under which truth can prevail over the Lie. Ahuramazda stands behind this division. Rebellion is not simply a political challenge but falsehood, while Darius’s military actions become the means through which truth returns to the empire.

The sequence persuades by joining event to explanation. Gaumata is false and Darius legitimate. The rebels are disobedient and Darius restorative. Defeat proves divine favour, while divine favour confirms the rightness of defeat. The monument presents a completed order. But the list of campaigns records the strain hidden inside that completion. Rebellion appears in detail because it had to be suppressed in several places. Darius’s victory could be genuine while the empire remained unstable. He had to travel, send armies, rely on commanders, and turn local defeats into a sequence of eventual successes. The monumental surface offers one order; the chronology beneath it records interruption. Its placement on the cliff extends the claim beyond the palace. The inscription stood above a route used by travellers and officials, although its height and languages limited who could read every line. Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian versions carried the king’s message across audiences within the empire. The monument announced that the roads below were secure because Darius had defeated false rulers. It also depended on those roads to make the announcement visible.

Behistun records rebellion, punishment, and restoration, but it compresses the arrangements that allowed a defeated province to produce revenue again. It does not explain which local families kept office, what tribute resumed, or how many soldiers remained in each district. A kingdom was restored when fields were assessed, roads watched, messengers travelled, and local people calculated that obedience would last.

Tribute began that calculation. Grain, silver, livestock, timber, horses, and labour entered imperial systems, but tribute was more than a shipment of goods. It was a scheduled declaration that a local ruler recognised a superior king. A province’s assessed obligation could differ from the goods supplied by a frontier people, yet both forms made submission measurable. The governor who sent the expected amount announced that his province remained inside the king’s order.

Collection required people who knew the fields and households from which payment could be drawn. Persian satraps, Babylonian temple authorities, Aramaic-speaking scribes, and local landholders served this purpose in different places. Their cooperation reduced the officials the crown had to send, while giving local elites power between the king and the population. They could explain an imperial demand, conceal resources, or turn collection to private advantage. Collaboration was one of the ways coercion reached ordinary households.

Deportation addressed a different difficulty. Assyrian kings moved conquered populations because a defeated town retained leaders, memories, and local networks that could support revolt. Families from one region were transferred to another, where they cultivated abandoned land, worked on royal projects, or supplied skilled labour. The movement broke old concentrations of power while preserving productive bodies for the state. Reliefs from the reigns of Sargon II and Sennacherib show deportees travelling with carts, animals, and possessions; displacement appears orderly because the state needed it to be useful.

Orderly representation does not make movement voluntary. A family did not become free labour because a relief showed it walking rather than lying in chains. Deportation altered kinship networks, property claims, and relations with the communities that received the newcomers. Some deportees gained a place in an administrative or military economy. Others carried the loss of home into the new settlements. Imperial security depended on making removal productive.

Roads joined tribute and deportation to force. The road Mesha built toward Jahaz served a local campaign, while larger empires required longer routes for armies, couriers, and supplies. The Achaemenid Royal Road connected Sardis with Susa over roughly 2,500 kilometres, with stations and relays that shortened the time required for royal messages. Such a road gave the king a route to a challenged district and allowed information to arrive before a local crisis became regional.

Garrisons made the route credible. A fort, troop settlement, or military colony protected a crossing and watched nearby communities. Soldiers consumed grain and required payment, so the garrison entered the tribute system itself. Its presence might deter revolt, but requisitioning and interference with local authority could also provoke it. Armed supervision made accommodation conditional.

Local intermediaries completed the chain. A conquered king might retain his throne as a client ruler; a priesthood might continue its rituals; a village headman might collect taxes. These figures translated imperial demands into local categories and made compliance compatible with older identities. Persian rule often allowed subject peoples to preserve laws and cults while insisting on loyalty to the Great King. The arrangement held when local rulers could protect their standing by serving the empire. It weakened when imperial demands made collaboration more dangerous than revolt.

Assyria used a harsher repertoire, but it too depended on routines. Deportees had to be registered and assigned. Tribute had to be counted, roads guarded, and cities supplied with officials after their ruling houses had been removed. Terror could open the way for these procedures, but procedures had to continue after the army departed. The power that destroyed a city still had to decide who would plough its fields.

Ashurbanipal inherited this problem as king of Assyria. His Elam campaign of 653–646 BCE grew from a long contest involving Babylon, Elam, and Assyria, not from one sudden quarrel. Teumman, king of Elam, had supported Assyria’s enemies and entered the politics of Mesopotamia. Ashurbanipal’s annals cast the conflict as the enforcement of broken agreements, not as expansion.

The campaign moved towards the Tigris and reached a confrontation at the Ulai River in 653 BCE. Ashurbanipal’s forces defeated Teumman. Assyrian representations show the Elamite king captured and killed, while one image places his severed head in a garden at Nineveh. Ashurbanipal sits at a banquet; attendants and musicians occupy the cultivated space; the defeated ruler’s head hangs from a tree. Teumman’s body has been brought from the battlefield into the Assyrian king’s ordered world.

The arrangement turns violence into governance. Ashurbanipal sits in a garden, where service, music, and royal leisure continue around the sign of Elamite defeat. The head occupies a defined place within the scene. Elam’s king is not merely dead; his death has been fitted into the palace’s representation of security.

Public terror worked through this conversion. Bodies displayed on walls, processions of captives, and images of defeated kings allowed distant viewers to encounter punishment without seeing the campaign itself. The spectacle travelled farther than the army. It warned officials within Nineveh as well as enemies beyond it, since subjects could see that rebellion ended in a form of display controlled by the palace.

Ashurbanipal’s annals join oath language to bodily destruction. Elamite rulers are accused of breaking agreements, supporting Babylon, or refusing to return fugitives. Once defeated, they lose cities, populations, royal symbols, or all three. The king’s actions appear as the answer to treachery. Assyria does not present itself as choosing terror for its own sake; it presents itself as the court enforcing a contract violated by others.

Elam’s political background makes that claim less complete. The kingdom had its own succession, factions, and interests in Mesopotamia. Babylonian resistance drew upon local traditions that Assyria could not erase. Teumman’s opposition was part of a regional struggle over Babylon and the routes east of the Tigris, not an irrational attack on a neutral order. The annals compress these competing interests into the shape most useful to Ashurbanipal: loyal king against oath-breaker.

After Teumman’s death, Ashurbanipal supported Ummanigash as ruler of Elam. This was the practical side of exemplary punishment. The Assyrian king could kill an enemy and still require an Elamite intermediary to govern the land. Ummanigash’s position depended partly on Assyrian support, giving Ashurbanipal influence without direct occupation of every settlement. Elam was not treated as a population that could simply vanish. It remained a political space whose leadership could be replaced. That arrangement failed. Ummanigash later broke with Assyria, and another Elamite ruler, Tammaritu, entered the continuing conflict. The annals record shifts of allegiance, fugitives, and royal changes. A client ruler could use authority granted by Assyria to pursue local interests, while an empire could not ensure that dependence would survive pressure. The intermediary who made rule cheaper also carried the possibility of betrayal.

Ashurbanipal’s forces eventually entered Susa, the ancient Elamite royal city. His account describes the destruction of walls, temples, and palaces, the removal of gods and royal treasures, and the scattering of the city’s remains. The destruction appears as the fulfilment of judgment. Elam had interfered in Babylon and violated the relationship imposed by Assyria; Susa became the place where accumulated offences were answered.

The account insists on physical transformation. Ashurbanipal describes opening graves, disturbing the dead, carrying away royal images, and making the land experience the consequences of Elamite hostility. Susa was not simply conquered but stripped of the signs by which its kings claimed continuity. Destroying palace, temple, and tomb attacked the political memory through which a dynasty could present itself as enduring.

Terror required memory as much as fear. A punishment seen once might deter a nearby governor; a punishment carved in stone could instruct viewers after the army returned. Written accounts named places and kings. Reliefs showed bodies, captives, deportation, and triumph. The palace multiplied violence through different media and gave it a durable audience. That audience could read the display in two ways. The destruction of Susa demonstrated Assyrian strength, but it also showed that no treaty guaranteed protection once the palace judged it broken. Terror stabilised rule when obedience seemed safer than resistance. It generated resistance when subjects concluded that submission would not shield them from punishment, or when the destruction of local authorities removed the people able to negotiate with the empire.

Ashurbanipal’s campaign produced both results. Teumman’s defeat removed an active enemy and restored an Assyrian-backed arrangement in Elam for a time. Later rebellions and succession struggles show that the political problem remained. The invasion of Susa eliminated a major centre of Elamite power, but it also made the Assyrian claim more absolute and harder to accommodate. A province might pay tribute while its elites waited for an opportunity to reverse the hierarchy.

Deportation deepened the tension. Moving populations weakened the social base of revolt and placed workers where the crown needed them. It also created communities carrying memories of removal and rulers with reasons to regard Assyria as an enemy. Deportees were not necessarily organised into one opposition, and many became part of Assyrian society, but the policy turned imperial order into a personal history of displacement. A stable register of labour and tribute could rest on unstable memories.

The annals speak as though victory ends the matter, yet their repeated campaigns reveal otherwise. A client could become a traitor. Tribute could be withheld. Garrisons secured roads while consuming resources. Deportation weakened one rebellion while producing new social mixtures that officials had to supervise. Violence solved immediate problems and created administrative ones.

Mesha offers a smaller parallel. He kills at Madaba and Nebo, settles people in recovered land, and builds roads and fortifications. His violence is the foundation of Moabite administration, not its substitute. Mesha must make the territory productive and defensible after the campaign, just as Darius must restore tax collection after suppressing rebels and Ashurbanipal must place clients in Elam after killing its king. Divine restoration legitimises the work that follows.

The Persian model shows another route. A king who permits local law and worship can reduce the need for constant punishment when tribute arrives and officials remain invested in the crown. This is a way to govern difference by assigning unequal political positions to peoples who retain distinct identities. The empire promises that each group may remain recognisable under the Great King’s supremacy. That promise works when its boundaries are predictable. A temple may keep its rites, a governor may retain office, and a village may continue customary law if taxes are paid and rebellion avoided. Local leaders then have something to defend through collaboration. A succession crisis or military emergency can change the bargain quickly. Accommodation may leave a people with loyalty to an empire that has not removed the possibility of coercion.

The final boundary remains uncertain. Behistun converts repeated rebellions into completed restoration. The Annals of Ashurbanipal convert terror into royal order while Elam resists through successive rulers. The Mesha Stele converts killing and displacement into recovery of land. Each inscription gives punishment the appearance of ending disorder, but none tells us how many subjects accepted the restored order, how many complied while waiting, or when fear became a reason to revolt again.

A conquered population may be quiet because violence has made resistance impossible, because tribute is tolerable, because local leaders profit from cooperation, or because a later rebellion has not yet found its moment. The stone at Dhiban, the cliff at Behistun, and the palace walls of Nineveh show rulers claiming that punishment restored order. They leave open whether the people living under that order regarded it as settlement or as the interval before resistance returned.

## Chapter 5: Warriors, Cities, and Moral Order in the Americas

The Codex Mendoza begins with provinces rather than a battlefield. Its first painted pages recount Mexica rulers and conquests; the later tribute pages turn expansion into columns of obligation. Each province appears through the goods it owed, the quantities demanded, and the intervals at which those goods were to arrive in Tenochtitlan. Made in the 1540s, after the Spanish conquest, the document probably drew on information preserved by Nahua scribes and administrators. It belongs to two moments: the imperial order represented in its pages and the colonial setting in which that order was copied, translated, and made legible to Europeans.

A province is not represented as a shaded area on a map. It is represented by what can be sent. One place supplies cotton mantles; another sends jaguar skins, greenstone, feathers, or gold ornaments; another provides food or weapons. The tribute pages name Tochtepec, Tlachco, and Xoconochco, and distinguish fabrics by size, pattern, and quality. Forty bundles are not interchangeable with four hundred cloaks. Each item belonged to a different chain of production, transport, storage, and redistribution. The painted signs give the reader a political geography made from goods.

The quantities are often striking. A province might owe several hundred loads of cloaks, hundreds of shields, or large numbers of feather adornments. From one conquered district came cotton cloth in specified measures; from another came cacao, warriors’ equipment, or precious materials. Staples also appear, goods that could sustain households and officials. These entries do not describe plunder seized at the moment of victory. They describe repeated deliveries, scheduled and measured, which required local authorities to gather goods from fields, workshops, markets, and dependent communities. That repetition changes the meaning of conquest. A defeated ruler could remain in place, and a city could preserve its temples and local offices, while the province became answerable for a regular flow of material toward the basin of Mexico. The Triple Alliance, formed in 1428 by Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan after the defeat of Azcapotzalco, did not govern every conquered territory through a single bureaucracy. Local rulers often continued to govern. Their position depended on acknowledging the alliance’s supremacy and meeting its demands, while military assistance could be required when a neighbouring campaign or rebellion called for it.

The Huey Tlatoani of Tenochtitlan stood at the summit of a political order that joined conquest, negotiation, tribute, and delegated rule. The Codex Mendoza shows that order in administrative form. The imperial centre expected goods from named places, but those goods passed through provincial officials and local communities. Cotton had to be grown, spun, woven, counted, packed, and carried. A shield required a craftsman, materials, and a chain of command that made production an obligation rather than a private commission.

The list of provinces preserves an uneven geography of power. The central basin lay close to Tenochtitlan and was connected by roads and water routes. More distant regions presented other problems. Xoconochco, on the Pacific coast near the Guatemalan frontier, was far from the imperial capital and valuable for cacao and other products. Its appearance in a tribute register signals reach, but reach was not easy control. Goods from distant provinces required couriers, porters, storage points, and officials able to enforce a demand across languages and local rivalries. A painted entry compresses those difficulties into a sign and a number.

The numbers should not be turned into a simple account of total annual income. The Codex Mendoza is not a complete financial ledger, and the Spanish glosses do not resolve every question about its pictorial conventions. Some pages may preserve information from different administrative moments; some amounts may have been assessed for particular periods rather than for the whole imperial system. The categories are nevertheless regular enough to show that obligations were itemised and understood through material classes. A grand total may be elusive, but the administrative habit is plain.

Cotton connects ecology, labour, and status. It did not grow equally everywhere in the highlands around Tenochtitlan. Regions suited to its cultivation became important sources of cloth, while skilled weavers transformed fibre into garments and ceremonial textiles. A demand for cloth thus extracted more than a natural product. It drew on agricultural land, household labour, spinning, weaving, dyeing, and inspection. The finished mantle was portable, countable, and suitable for storage or redistribution, which made it an effective form of imperial wealth.

Feathers carried a different political meaning. Bright feathers from tropical birds could be assembled into shields, headdresses, and objects that marked rank and military distinction. The tribute system brought distant environments into the ceremonial centre. A bird or plant associated with a lowland region became part of the visual language of authority in Tenochtitlan. The imperial city did not merely consume these goods; it displayed them in a courtly and religious world where material splendour made hierarchy visible.

Weapons appear in the register as well. Shields, darts, and other military equipment could be demanded from provinces, so tribute supported future campaigns even when a province’s defeat had occurred years earlier. A successful campaign might produce captives for ritual or status, but it also produced a province that could supply cloth, food, arms, or labour. The relation between captive-taking and extraction was therefore not a choice between two separate systems. Conquest could yield both a person for ceremonial use and a community placed under recurring material demands.

A shield shown in a tribute page does not identify the campaign for which it was made or the fighters who carried it. It does show that military objects belonged within the same imperial economy as textiles and food. The state’s demands did not divide neatly into economic and military categories. A province supplied what sustained the court, marked rank, outfitted armies, and enacted the relation between centre and subordinate community.

Some obligations were tied to particular provinces because of local resources. Others reflected the labour available through established crafts. A community was not asked to provide an abstract tax calculated in identical units everywhere. Its obligation emerged from what its land, workshops, and trade connections could make available. Local specialisation survived, but it served an imperial demand.

Tribute was not voluntary gift exchange between equal rulers. Goods were owed because the alliance had conquered, threatened, or otherwise subordinated the province. Political language could present the arrangement as a proper relationship between powerful and lesser altepetl, but the practical consequence was a recurring transfer backed by the possibility of military action. When a province resisted, the alliance could campaign against it; when a province complied, its local ruler could retain office and avoid the costs of direct occupation. That bargain helps explain how imperial territory could expand without being governed like the central city. A local ruler had reason to collect tribute if cooperation preserved his position. Communities beneath him faced the labour of production and delivery, but extraction often ran through familiar authorities rather than replacing every local institution. Tenochtitlan demanded, provincial elites assessed, households produced, and carriers moved the goods. The imperial centre depended on each step.

The catalogue can thus be read as a map of relationships. Provinces are linked to the capital by roads, obligations, and official expectations. Goods reveal environments: cotton zones, cacao regions, feather-producing lowlands, and places where precious materials could be gathered or acquired. Quantities reveal administrative judgement. Objects reveal the courtly and military uses toward which provincial production was directed. A province becomes visible because something is expected from it.

The Aztec Tribute List, preserved in a separate document, organises conquered places through goods and quantities in a similar way. Its entries have been used to estimate the wealth extracted by the empire, though the manuscripts differ in arrangement and the figures cannot simply be treated as one complete account. Both records make the same political fact visible: imperial power was enacted through recurring demands that could be written, pictured, inspected, and compared.

At the end of these routes stood a city that was more than a military headquarters. Tenochtitlan’s markets, palaces, temples, storehouses, and workshops gave the goods somewhere to go. Tribute could support the ruler’s household, feed officials and dependants, reward allies, furnish rituals, and provision armies. Imported materials announced that distant provinces had been brought into the ruler’s world. Wealth was political because its movement made rank and command tangible. This arrangement changes how the word warrior should be read. A noble who took captives could gain honour and advancement, and military orders formed an important part of elite life. The political system required people who represented assessments, officials who collected them, labourers who produced them, and routes that brought them to the capital. The celebrated fighter stood at the visible end of a larger process.

The obligations could be heavy without being identical to permanent occupation. A city might send tribute twice a year, supply warriors when summoned, and continue its local ritual life. This flexibility helped the Triple Alliance govern a wide region through force and accommodation. It also created vulnerability. If a local ruler judged imperial demands intolerable, or if a rival offered better terms, the province could become a point of resistance. Conquest created a relationship that had to be renewed through collection.

The pages do not show the experience of every household that met these demands. Their form privileges the centre’s categories: province, object, amount, obligation. That silence leaves the pressures of production and delivery in place and exposes the institutions that made conquest durable. Imperial violence appears here after the battle as a schedule.

A painted bundle of cloth begins in a field where cotton is cultivated, continues in work by hands the register does not name, passes through a local authority, travels along a route, and enters a capital where it may clothe an official or supply a ritual. A captive taken in war could become a ritual victim or a mark of military achievement; tribute goods became the more predictable means by which the conquered remained subordinate. The register does not reduce conflict to economics. It shows why a portrait of Mexica society as a culture of warriors is inadequate.

The Codex Mendoza was assembled after Tenochtitlan fell, and its images were accompanied by explanations intended for a European audience. Antonio de Mendoza, the first viceroy of New Spain, has traditionally been associated with the manuscript’s commission, though the circumstances of its production remain debated. Nahua painters and informants preserved much of the information, while Spanish glosses translated its signs into alphabetic descriptions. The document is neither an untouched imperial ledger nor a Spanish invention. It is a colonial-era rendering of Nahua administrative knowledge. That layered origin becomes decisive when the subject changes from tribute to war itself. A register can show what a province owed. It cannot by itself tell us how Mexica participants described the moral purpose of fighting, or how Spanish observers selected the words sacrifice, idolatry, and conquest to describe what they saw. Book 12 of the Florentine Codex offers a more difficult record.

Compiled in the 1570s under Bernardino de Sahagún, with Nahua collaborators and informants, Book 12 concerns the conquest of Mexico. Its pages preserve accounts of omens, embassies, battles, the arrival of strangers, the fall of Tenochtitlan, and the suffering that followed. The work was written in Nahuatl and Spanish, and the linguistic layers do not simply mirror one another. A Nahua account passes through questions posed by Sahagún, the memories of informants, the work of scribes, and the expectations of a colonial reader.

The Nahuatl text gives the conquest a texture of uncertainty and shock. It describes signs preceding the Spaniards’ arrival, the movement of messengers, the exchange of gifts, and changing interpretations of unfamiliar weapons and animals. When the narrative reaches the fighting, it attends to streets, causeways, temples, canoes, and the movement of people through a city under attack. The war unfolds through decisions by rulers, actions by allied and hostile communities, and the material geography of Tenochtitlan and the lake.

The Spanish version, shaped by translation and editorial framing, places those events within a Christian history of conquest. Spanish forces appear as instruments of victory, while Mexica religious practices are described through categories that identify idolatry and sacrifice as signs of error. Those categories helped justify the destruction of temples, the seizure of political power, and the conversion of survivors. A practice could be observed accurately in part and still be assigned a meaning that served the conqueror. This is clearest in accounts of captive sacrifice. Nahua ritual language connected offerings to obligations between humans and divine powers. Spanish observers described the same practices as evidence of barbarity and demonic worship, often emphasising the heart, the temple stairway, and the victim’s body. Such descriptions were not necessarily fabricated. Excavations at the Templo Mayor have confirmed sacrificial practices and displays of human remains, including the skull rack known as the Huey Tzompantli.

Confirmation of an act does not validate the category through which a conqueror explained an entire society.

Book 12 also makes military practice visible. Mexica and allied forces defended causeways, used canoes, and attempted to control access through the lake. The Spanish relied on Indigenous allies, including forces from Tlaxcala and other communities hostile to Mexica rule. Their victory cannot be understood as the triumph of a few Europeans over a unified Indigenous population. Rivalries created openings, and the siege depended on local knowledge, manpower, and supplies. The conquest frame can conceal this coalition unless the reader follows the named peoples and movements within it.

The Nahua account does not stand outside politics. Its speakers lived after the fall of Tenochtitlan, and their testimony was gathered under Spanish rule. Memory had become part of survival. Yet the persistence of local names, military actions, translated speeches, and descriptions of neighbourhoods allows the narrative to be read against its colonial frame. It shows people interpreting invasion, making alliances, and fighting for a city, not merely providing examples for a Spanish moral narrative.

The difference between the tribute register and Book 12 is instructive. The Codex Mendoza renders imperial power through obligations. Book 12 renders conflict through movement, memory, and catastrophe. Neither opens directly onto the past. Together they prevent the reader from treating a representation of ritual as a complete account of warfare or a Spanish description of sacrifice as an explanation for the organisation of an empire.

The Andean case shifts the problem from tribute pages to a succession war. Pedro de Cieza de León wrote The Second Part of the Chronicle of Peru in the 1550s, drawing on testimony gathered after the Spanish invasion and on his own observations in the Andes. His account of Atahualpa and Huáscar describes a conflict that had already weakened the Inca state before Francisco Pizarro’s forces captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532.

Huáscar, based at Cusco, and Atahualpa, whose power was rooted in the northern Andes after campaigns against the Quito region, were not merely two individuals competing for a crown. Their factions drew on different military resources, regional loyalties, commanders, and routes of movement. Cieza describes armies, battles, punishments, and shifting fortunes. The war followed the death of the Inca ruler Huayna Capac and the succession crisis that accompanied it, though the precise sequence and causes are filtered through later accounts.

Atahualpa’s faction included veterans hardened by campaigns in the north and commanders who had gained authority during those struggles. Huáscar’s position rested on Cusco, the dynastic centre, and on the legitimacy associated with the imperial heartland. The conflict joined succession to a geographic division within the expanding empire. Each side could claim more than personal right. Each could draw upon institutions and relationships built through earlier conquest.

Cieza gives particular weight to violence within the conflict. Captured nobles were killed, communities were punished, and the struggle damaged the political order that Spanish forces encountered. Such scenes can be read as evidence of brutal warfare, but they also carry the chronicle’s concern with disorder and rightful rule. Cieza admired aspects of Inca administration and described roads, storehouses, officials, and organised labour while presenting the succession war as a calamity that opened the way to foreign seizure.

The Spanish exploited that opening with speed. When Pizarro met Atahualpa at Cajamarca in November 1532, the Inca faction had recently defeated Huáscar’s forces. Atahualpa was powerful but politically exposed. His army was present in the region, yet the ruler and his attendants entered a situation in which Spanish cavalry, steel weapons, firearms, and surprise could be concentrated against them. The capture was not the conquest of an intact and unified empire by force from outside. It was an intervention in a conflict whose winner had not secured the whole political order.

Atahualpa’s ransom shows the overlap between political authority and extraction. The Spanish demanded gold and silver for his release, and objects were gathered from temples and storehouses across the realm. The ransom was material, but the prisoner was political. Holding the Inca gave Pizarro leverage over officials and communities that might otherwise have resisted. The Spanish used the symbols and channels of Inca authority while dismantling the ruler who embodied them.

The contrast with Mexica tribute is sharp. The Codex Mendoza displays a long-running system in which provinces were assigned goods through an imperial framework that could preserve local rulers. Cieza follows a succession crisis in which the channels of authority had become contested. In Mexico, Spanish forces entered a world of rival city-states and used the hostility of Tlaxcala and others against Tenochtitlan. In the Andes, they seized a ruler whose faction had just won a civil war and turned unsettled succession into a means of command. Neither case supports one model of precolonial American warfare. The Mexica alliance built domination through repeated tribute, military threat, and local collaboration. The Inca state incorporated territories through conquest, negotiation, administrative resettlement, labour obligations, and the incorporation of defeated peoples into imperial armies. Its reach depended on roads, storehouses, provincial officials, and the movement of armies across difficult terrain. Those systems could expand rapidly, but succession exposed the personal and dynastic concentration of power.

Cieza’s chronicle reveals Spanish opportunism through the language of judgement. Atahualpa’s conduct can be presented as tyrannical, Huáscar’s supporters as wronged, and Spanish intervention as the arrival of order. The sequence points elsewhere. Spanish leaders sought wealth, jurisdiction, titles, labour, and Indigenous allegiance. They did not merely enter an existing dispute to settle it. They converted the dispute into conquest. The same political violence could be described in different registers. A faction’s killing of enemies might appear in a chronicle as cruelty, in a dynastic account as punishment, or in a military context as the securing of succession. Spanish writers often treated Indigenous conflict as proof that conquest was necessary while their own forces manipulated divisions and carried out executions. The source must be read for its sequence of alliances and decisions, not only for the judgements attached to it.

A ritual explanation has force when applied to some American evidence. Mexica ceremonies included human sacrifice, and captive-taking could bring military prestige, social advancement, and ritual opportunity. Spanish witnesses repeatedly described sacrificial killing, and the Templo Mayor excavations support important parts of those accounts. It would be mistaken to dissolve ritual into a later European fantasy because the sources are polemical.

The conclusion that ritual alone explains American warfare asks one practice to carry more weight than the records allow. Tribute pages name provinces and quantities. Book 12 describes alliances, lake warfare, and Indigenous forces. Cieza describes a succession conflict fought by factions with regional resources. These details are the political and logistical conditions through which campaigns were organised.

A captive could have several values at once. He might be a ritual victim, a sign of a warrior’s achievement, a prisoner whose rank made him useful in negotiation, or a person whose capture weakened an enemy’s political community. Those possibilities could overlap without becoming identical. A high-ranking captive might be treated differently from an ordinary fighter; a campaign against a rebellious province might produce captives without having been launched primarily for sacrifice. The act of taking a person from battle did not carry one fixed meaning.

The idea that Mexica armies fought chiefly to supply temples also fits poorly with the geography of tribute. A province that sent cotton, cacao, cloth, or shields was not simply a reservoir of future victims. It was a source of predictable income and equipment. The empire needed roads, collectors, storage, and provincial compliance. Campaigns could secure these advantages, protect routes, punish refusal, or bring a rival city into the alliance’s orbit. Ritual could authorise war and interpret its results, while extraction gave conflict a durable political purpose.

Spanish conquest accounts magnified ritual because the category served their needs. Describing Indigenous religion as murderous helped present invasion as rescue and conversion. The language of sacrifice turned political opponents into objects of moral condemnation. When Spanish authors placed their own violence within the vocabulary of justice, warfare, or Christian salvation, they did not treat killing as proof of barbarism. The asymmetry belonged to the conquest argument.

The Florentine Codex was produced under colonial conditions, and Nahua informants could shape memory according to later interests. Cieza wrote within the Spanish imperial world and relied on testimony gathered after the Inca civil war. The Codex Mendoza was assembled for a Spanish viceroy. Each source requires attention to who gathered it, when, for whom, and in what form. That qualification changes how the evidence is used; it does not erase the evidence.

Material remains can help separate the layers, while interpretation remains necessary. The skull rack at Tenochtitlan confirms public displays of human remains. It does not establish the total number of victims described by a conquest writer or explain the place of sacrifice within finance, diplomacy, or military strategy. Fortifications, roads, storehouses, weapons, and settlement patterns provide different kinds of evidence. A wall may indicate defence, formal separation, or both; a weapon may show manufacture and use without revealing the political decision that sent an army to war.

Representation had its own historical force. A ruler’s display of captives could intimidate subjects, warn rivals, satisfy divine obligations, and proclaim legitimacy. A tribute register could make subordination appear orderly by turning conquest into scheduled delivery. A chronicle could make Spanish intervention appear necessary by placing Indigenous violence at the centre of the story. These representations did not merely describe power. They helped exercise it.

The Inca case makes the problem especially clear. Cieza’s account contains information about Atahualpa, Huáscar, Cusco, Quito, armies, and the political consequences of civil war. It also reaches the reader through a conquest-era effort to explain why Spanish rule followed. If the chronicle’s moral categories are accepted without examining its sequence, the Spanish appear as outside judges of an Indigenous crisis. If the account is discarded entirely, the factions, resources, and decisions that made the crisis intelligible disappear with it.

A careful reading keeps actions and categories apart long enough to compare them. Atahualpa and Huáscar commanded competing factions. Their supporters fought, punished, and redistributed authority. Spanish leaders entered after the conflict had weakened the empire and seized Atahualpa for ransom and leverage. Those events can be established through a source whose interpretation of justice must still be questioned. The document remains useful when its evidence is not confused with its verdict. The same discipline applies to Nahua descriptions. A phrase about a god, a captive, or a ceremonial obligation belongs to a religious world that should not be translated into the language of mere economic interest. The presence of ritual language does not erase the provincial obligations recorded elsewhere. The people who understood war as part of a sacred order also organised armies, collected tribute, negotiated alliances, defended roads, and fought for political supremacy.

The categories of conquerors become most misleading when they turn difference into essence. “Indian sacrifice” can be made to name an entire civilisation, while “Spanish conquest” is made to name a legal or religious undertaking rather than a process of alliance, seizure, ransom, and occupation. The evidence is more particular. A province owed cloth. A city supplied an ally. A faction controlled a road. A ruler was captured. A ritual was performed. Each fact belongs to a different register, and the history emerges from their relation.

Ceremonial representation should not be treated as decoration around real politics. The temple, the skull rack, the feathered shield, and the captive before a ruler were political instruments because they made supremacy visible. Ritual could reward warriors, bind rulers to divine obligations, terrify enemies, and integrate conquered materials into the centre’s public world. It was part of how authority was understood and displayed. Its presence does not make battlefield practice unreal; it explains why battlefield actions acquired particular meanings.

The political extraction shown by the Codex Mendoza is more than background finance. Tribute determined who worked, who collected, who benefited, and which provinces remained subordinate. It could supply campaigns that made further extraction possible. The list’s calm sequence of places and goods rests upon the threat of force, but it also reveals a system in which force had been converted into routine. Administrative regularity was one of conquest’s achievements.

Battlefield practice must be tested by different evidence. Book 12’s accounts of causeways, canoes, attacks, and Indigenous allies concern movement and combat. Cieza’s account concerns factions, commanders, succession, and the intervention of Pizarro’s men. Tribute registers concern assessment and delivery. Archaeology concerns structures, remains, objects, and traces of use. No single source should be asked to answer all these questions. That division protects historical judgement from two opposite errors. One accepts conquest categories and mistakes condemnation for explanation. The other treats every record of ritual as ideological invention and misses the religious obligations that participants considered real. The evidence supports a harder account: American societies represented violence through sacred and political forms, while institutions of tribute, alliance, succession, and military organisation determined how violence could be carried out.

A captive taken on a battlefield was not only a body for ceremony, just as a bundle of cloth was not only a commodity. Each could enter a larger order of rank and obligation. The captive might confirm a warrior’s status or serve a ruler’s display. The cloth might feed a court, equip soldiers, dress an official, or announce the submission of a province. Their meanings were made by institutions that connected war to the city, the palace, the temple, and the road.

The conquest sources remain compromised and still offer evidence. The Spanish description of sacrifice must be compared with Nahua wording, archaeological remains, and the political circumstances of conquest. The account of the Inca civil war must be read for factional resources and Spanish timing as well as for Cieza’s judgements. The Codex Mendoza must be read as a post-Conquest manuscript that nevertheless preserves an unusually concrete account of provincial obligation.

Tenochtitlan’s alliance made provinces answerable through goods and military service. Nahua accounts placed war within a sacred and civic world that Spanish writers recast for conquest. The Inca succession conflict turned dynastic legitimacy, regional armies, and imperial infrastructure into competing resources, then gave Spanish opportunists the opening they needed. Ritual mattered, but it operated alongside extraction and alliance rather than replacing them.

A city could worship through violence, tax through conquest, and defend itself through organised armies without any one of those activities explaining the others. The records become clearest when their categories are kept distinct long enough to show how they joined. Precolonial American societies represented violence through moral and ceremonial forms, while institutions gave that violence reach, duration, and political consequence.

## Chapter 6: Frontiers, Faiths, and Political Fragmentation

William of Poitiers began his account of the Norman conquest with a claim about right. William, duke of Normandy, had been deprived of the kingdom that Edward the Confessor had promised him, while Harold Godwinson, who had sworn to support the Norman succession, had taken the crown. In the *Gesta Guillelmi*, written by a Norman cleric who knew the conqueror’s circle, the invasion of 1066 was not an unprovoked crossing of the Channel but the recovery of a lawful inheritance. English resistance was placed inside a moral frame before the army reached Sussex: a disputed succession became an offence, and the duke’s armed response became correction.

The ships gathered on the Norman coast, but contrary winds delayed the crossing. William of Poitiers gives that delay a providential meaning; the practical circumstances were plainer. Horses, men, weapons, and provisions had to be assembled and transported together. When the fleet crossed, it landed at Pevensey in September 1066. The army moved towards Hastings, built a timber fortification, and waited for Harold’s forces. The English king had already marched north against Harald Hardrada of Norway, defeating him at Stamford Bridge on 25 September. He then turned south. The two armies met on 14 October.

William of Poitiers describes the battle as a contest between disciplined Norman force and English obstinacy. His account attends especially to the English shield wall, Norman cavalry, and the danger created when some of William’s men retreated and English soldiers followed them down the slope. Whether the feigned flight had been planned from the beginning or was exploited when it appeared, it opened gaps in the English line. Harold was killed, and the army gathered around him lost the person through whom resistance could be coordinated.

The account is shaped by victory, but it preserves the material sequence that made victory possible: a fortified landing place, a rapid march, infantry holding a defensive position, and cavalry seeking a breach. The death of the king altered the field.

Hastings did not make England Norman. London remained outside William’s immediate control, and English nobles could still choose another king. After Harold’s death, Edgar Ætheling was elected, while resistance gathered in different places rather than disappearing with the southern army. William moved through Kent towards the Thames, taking towns and receiving submissions. At Berkhamsted, in December 1066, leading Englishmen accepted him as king. William of Poitiers presents these submissions as the restoration of order, but their timing shows the pressure behind them. The duke’s army had demonstrated what it could do, and those who negotiated knew that further resistance might bring devastation to their lands.

Coronation at Westminster on Christmas Day gave the conquest a legal and liturgical form. William was acclaimed king, and the ceremony connected him to the English monarchy rather than presenting him merely as a foreign warlord. It did not settle the country. Rebellion followed in the north, the southwest, and along the Welsh border. William’s forces returned repeatedly to regions that had accepted him or had been defeated. English resistance was not one continuous national movement with a single command. Local nobles, towns, religious houses, and rural communities changed their decisions as Norman garrisons appeared and the costs of resistance became clear.

The land redistribution made conquest durable. William rewarded the men who had crossed with him, while the estates of English landholders who had resisted were confiscated. Some Englishmen retained property through submission, marriage, or service, but the broad direction was unmistakable. The names in the *Domesday Book*, completed in 1086, record a landholding class transformed within a generation. Norman and French-speaking tenants-in-chief occupied the upper ranks; English subtenants survived beneath new lords or disappeared from the record as independent holders. The change went beyond a transfer of ownership after a battle: it changed who could raise men, collect rents, build castles, and speak for a district.

William of Poitiers treats reward as justice because his narrative depends upon the idea that William’s followers had earned what they received. The conqueror’s companions had supplied ships and service; their grants could therefore be described as payment for loyalty and participation in a rightful enterprise. For those who lost land, the same process meant that a manor changed lord and rents were redirected. The armed presence behind the new arrangement was often a castle or a group of mounted retainers. The source gives the conquerors a voice, but its administrative consequences expose the interests of peasants, tenants, and local clergy who had to live under the new distribution.

Resistance was answered by force beyond the battlefield. The revolt in Northumbria in 1069, supported by Danish intervention, led to the campaign later known as the Harrying of the North. William of Poitiers’s account is less full on this episode than later chroniclers, and the evidence for its demographic consequences is debated. The policy is nevertheless clear in outline: settlements, crops, and resources were attacked to make rebellion difficult to sustain. Norman authority could not rely on a single victory at Hastings because the kingdom contained many places where the king’s commands were weak. Punitive destruction compensated for the absence of immediate administrative reach.

William’s castles served a related purpose. They were garrisons and visible claims to possession. At Pevensey, Hastings, Dover, York, and elsewhere, fortifications allowed a small conquering elite to hold ground among a much larger population. Their construction consumed labour and material drawn from the surrounding countryside. A castle could protect a Norman lord from local hostility, but it could also provoke hostility by making extraction permanent. The new order depended upon both accommodation and coercion: oaths, coronation, and ecclesiastical recognition, together with confiscation, garrisons, and punitive campaigns.

The *Gesta Guillelmi* turns this combination into a story of providential kingship. Its author was not an observer outside the conquest. William of Poitiers had been a chaplain and had served in a military world whose values he understood. He celebrates courage, lineage, and rightful lordship, attending more closely to the king and his nobles than to the people whose fields became the ground of war. That position shows how conquest had to be explained to those who benefited from it. Land seizure became legitimate when tied to a broken oath, a papal banner, a coronation, and the restoration of peace.

The Norman conquest reveals political fragmentation that enabled violence and made it contestable. England had a king, but royal authority depended on earls, bishops, sheriffs, local courts, and armed men who could carry orders into the countryside. When the succession broke, these parts did not automatically obey one centre. Some resisted, some negotiated, and some changed sides. William won not because England lacked political structures, but because he captured, replaced, or recruited the people who operated them. The conquest made a new hierarchy by occupying the old one.

A century later and far to the south, Ibn Khaldun described a different relation between mobile power and a settled city. His account of Mahdia’s fall in 1148 concerns the Almohad advance in North Africa and the defeat of the Zirid ruler al-Hasan ibn Ali. The city on the Tunisian coast was wealthy, fortified, and connected to maritime commerce. Its walls and stores gave settled society resources that a mobile army did not possess; they also made Mahdia an object of desire. Whoever held it controlled people, goods, harbour facilities, and the prestige of a capital.

Ibn Khaldun’s explanation rests on *asabiyyah*, the solidarity that allows a group to act together. Mobile groups, in his account, possess the cohesion and martial discipline required to challenge cities whose inhabitants have become accustomed to settled protections. The contrast concerns different resources available to nomads and townspeople. A mobile coalition can move quickly and preserve loyalty through kinship or shared allegiance. A city can store grain, maintain walls, and draw upon merchants and craftsmen. Neither form of power is complete by itself.

The Almohad movement had more than mobility. Its religious programme and dynastic leadership gave separate groups a language of common purpose. Its advance placed pressure on local rulers whose authority was already divided. Mahdia’s ruler could command the city, but his position depended upon troops, urban notables, and subjects calculating whether resistance was safer than submission. A merchant might value the existing regime’s protection while fearing that continued resistance would destroy trade. A soldier might remain loyal, join the besiegers, or wait to see which side could pay.

The siege exposed the difference between possession and control. A city behind walls could deny immediate access to its stores, but it also concentrated people whose food and safety depended on decisions made by a small ruling group. Mobile forces needed to make the city’s rulers doubt that they could maintain supplies, allies, or obedience, without storming every house. When Mahdia fell, urban resources passed into the hands of a wider power that had previously operated through movement and affiliation. Conquest joined the two systems rather than simply replacing one with the other.

The people of a conquered city could be killed, enslaved, displaced, or incorporated. Merchants and artisans might be retained because their skills and revenues were useful. The conqueror’s interest in Mahdia was therefore not exhausted by its defeat. A ruined port would yield less than a functioning one. Violence could open a city to seizure, while rule required selective restraint. Property, trade, and skilled labour had to survive if conquest was to become government.

Compared with William’s England, Mahdia shows what changes when the main pressure comes from a mobile coalition against an urban centre rather than from a duke converting invasion into a landed aristocracy. William redistributed estates to create a territorial elite whose power rested on castles and rents. The Almohad conquest drew a wealthy city into a larger movement whose initial strength lay in solidarity beyond the walls. In both cases, fragmented authority created openings. English nobles could resist or submit one by one; North African rulers could not assume that urban wealth guaranteed political loyalty. War became possible where no single command could bind every local interest, and contestable because each group still possessed something the conqueror needed.

The bishops who met at Charroux in 989 addressed the problem in another register. Their canons identified acts that local warriors and powerful laymen were forbidden to commit, then attached spiritual penalties to those acts, without describing a campaign or naming an enemy army. The Council of Charroux is associated with the Peace of God, an effort that spread through Aquitaine and other parts of western Europe as bishops, clergy, nobles, and communities tried to restrain violence where kings could not reliably police every armed conflict.

The first canon protected churches and the goods held within them. Those who violated churches or removed their contents were threatened with excommunication. Another canon extended protection to the poor, peasants, and others who lacked the means to defend themselves. The canons also addressed clerics and church property, including animals and provisions associated with religious houses. Their wording converts a broad moral concern into named categories of vulnerable people and usable things. A church was a store of grain, a refuge, a landholder, and a community whose protection could be claimed publicly, as well as a building.

The sanctions were severe in spiritual terms. Excommunication separated an offender from the Christian community and from the sacraments that marked belonging. Yet a bishop could pronounce the penalty more easily than enforce it. A lord with armed followers might ignore a sentence, protect his men, or compel a local priest to continue religious services. The canon’s force depended on recognition by people who believed clerical judgement mattered, on pressure from other clergy and lay communities, and on nobles willing to support the church against a neighbour.

Moral restraint required a social coalition.

The protected categories are more revealing than the language of peace alone. The bishops were drawing boundaries around violence that could be tolerated. A knight might fight another lord, but seizure of church goods threatened the church as a corporate power. A campaign might continue, but attacks on peasants endangered cultivation, rents, and the population on which lords and monasteries depended. By naming the poor and the unarmed, the canons turned their suffering into a matter that could implicate an aggressor before a wider religious audience.

Charroux produced a claim that violence could be judged by authorities other than the warrior who exercised it, without producing a central police force. Bishops, abbots, local clergy, and assemblies could identify forbidden conduct, publicise sanctions, and negotiate with armed men. Success varied by place and time, but the canons show that fragmented rule did not mean the absence of order. Order had to be assembled through overlapping jurisdictions whose powers were moral, economic, social, and sometimes military.

The question concerns the institutions already at work; comparison with the modern state hides them. William of Poitiers describes a conquest in which a king gained control by taking over the distribution of land and the command of fortresses. Ibn Khaldun describes a city whose wealth could be captured only through the collision of mobile solidarity, dynastic weakness, and urban resources. The bishops at Charroux attempted to make churches, peasants, and ecclesiastical goods unavailable as ordinary spoils. Each case presents authority as divided, but none presents society as ungoverned.

In England, royal power expanded through men who held local authority in the king’s name. The Norman tenant-in-chief depended on sheriffs, castellans, reeves, priests, and cultivators. A royal order could travel through these people only if they had reasons to carry it out. Land grants supplied those reasons to the conquerors; oaths and offices connected local enforcement to the crown. The chain could break when an earl rebelled, a town admitted an enemy, or a rural population refused rents and supplies. Fragmentation made rebellion possible because command was distributed; conquest made rebellion harder by redistributing command.

The land settlement also made aggression ordinary rather than exceptional. A new lord’s claim had to be performed through rent collection, court sessions, labour demands, and protection of the estate. The violence of 1066–1071 continued in quieter forms after the battles ended. Peasants lost access to woodland or pasture, tenants supplied services, and local disputes were heard by officials linked to the new lord. These practices cannot be reduced to a single Norman plan. They show how armed conquest became a daily relation between people who owned land, people who worked it, and people who could compel payment.

Mahdia presents a different settlement of the same problem. A mobile or tribal coalition could defeat a ruler, but it could not live indefinitely without the revenues of settled places. Urban merchants, craftsmen, sailors, tax collectors, and religious leaders became participants in the new order because their work produced the wealth that conquest sought. Their position was dangerous. A city’s inhabitants could bargain collectively, open its gates, or endure a siege, but the terms were set by the distribution of food, weapons, and reliable allies. The conqueror’s need to preserve revenue could protect some civilians while leaving others exposed to punishment or enslavement.

Ibn Khaldun’s language of solidarity clarifies why military strength does not reside in weapons alone. A ruler may possess walls and wealth yet lack the loyalty needed to mobilise them. A mobile group may lack stores and buildings yet possess the cohesion required to act at a distance. Once the mobile group conquers, its problem changes. It must turn personal allegiance into taxation, appointments, garrisons, and law. Urban life must serve a new dynasty without losing the trade that supports it. Mahdia was therefore not simply a triumph of nomads over townspeople. It was a transfer in which each side’s resources were reassembled under altered leadership.

The Peace of God addressed the same reassembly from below and beside the ruler. A bishop could not command every armed retainer, but he could declare that violating a church placed a man outside Christian communion. A monastery could not defeat a warband in the open, but it could seek protection from a count, invoke a saint, and bring a local offender before an assembly. Peasants could not write the canons, yet their labour and vulnerability gave the peace movement its practical subject. If fields were burned and animals taken, the consequences reached monasteries, lords, markets, and households.

Clergy judged violence while owning land, collecting dues, sheltering fugitives, negotiating ransoms, and sometimes accompanying armies. Their participation complicates any picture of religion as standing outside politics. At Charroux, ecclesiastical authority sought to protect ecclesiastical goods and persons, but it also offered a language through which lay communities could challenge predatory power. The protected peasant was both a Christian soul and a producer whose survival mattered to every local estate. Religious and economic concerns met in the same canon.

Merchants occupied another unstable position. In Mahdia they were tied to the port and to the ruler who promised security, but their cargoes could also finance the conqueror who replaced him. In England, traders depended on roads, markets, and courts that conquest could disrupt or regularise. A raid might enrich a warrior while damaging the market through which his household obtained grain. Merchants therefore had reasons to resist arbitrary violence even when they accepted hierarchy. They could lend money, move information, support a city faction, or withdraw goods when the road became unsafe.

Captives made the boundary between war and social order especially visible. A captive could become ransom, labour, status, or a bargaining instrument. Norman campaigns in England produced displaced people and prisoners whose treatment depended on rank and circumstance. The fall of a North African city exposed inhabitants to sale, flight, or incorporation. Religious peace legislation tried to place some persons beyond seizure, but its need to specify protected people shows how normal capture and plunder remained.

Protection was negotiated, not assumed. These examples qualify the picture of medieval frontiers as empty margins between states. The frontier was inhabited by peasants, herders, monks, traders, soldiers, and local lords who carried obligations across political boundaries. Raiding could be an economic practice or a military tactic. It could also be condemned when it threatened the property of a church or the revenues of a lord who had not authorised it. The same act changed meaning according to who ordered it, who suffered from it, and which authority claimed the right to judge it.

Violence did not become organised only when a strong state suppressed private force. William’s conquest shows a king using aristocratic relationships and local offices to extend violence and stabilise its gains. Mahdia shows a mobile coalition converting solidarity into control of a settled centre. Charroux shows bishops trying to regulate armed men without possessing a monopoly of force. Organisation existed in all three settings, distributed among rulers, nobles, towns, monasteries, assemblies, and communities. Nor did religious authority merely decorate secular power. William’s invasion gained legitimacy through a Christian vocabulary of oath, justice, and rightful rule. The Peace of God imposed costs on those who attacked protected people and goods. Almohad expansion drew strength from a religiously articulated solidarity that gathered mobile followers against established dynasties. These religious forms could authorise aggression as readily as restrain it. Their political importance lay in defining what violence meant, which victims deserved protection, and which acts converted a warrior into an enemy of the community.

Local authority remained decisive because broad principles needed particular enforcers. A bishop had to identify an offender. A lord had to decide whether to shelter or surrender him. A town had to determine whether to open its gates. A peasant household had to calculate whether flight, submission, concealment, or resistance offered the best chance of survival. The absence of a central state multiplied these decisions rather than eliminating them. It also made outcomes unpredictable. A church could be protected in one valley and plundered in the next; a conquered city could preserve its merchants under one ruler and lose them under another.

Political fragmentation thus created opportunities for raiding, invasion, and seizure because no single authority controlled every road, fortification, estate, or armed group. It also created several places from which violence could be challenged. A king might be resisted by an earl, a lord by a bishop, a conqueror by a city, or a warband by the community whose crops it needed. None of these checks guaranteed safety. They made violence a matter of bargaining and jurisdiction as well as force.

The records are thickest where power speaks. William of Poitiers names William the Conqueror and the nobles who served him. Ibn Khaldun analyses dynasties, cities, and the solidarity of ruling groups. The canons of Charroux preserve the language of bishops. Their pages leave the people they sought to govern in partial outline. We know that peasants were protected because the bishops named them, that merchants mattered because cities and ports were worth preserving, and that captives entered the economy of conquest because war repeatedly produced them. But what did a peasant decide when a Norman garrison demanded supplies, or when a Charroux prohibition conflicted with a local lord’s order?

The question becomes sharper in Mahdia. Ibn Khaldun can explain the city’s resources and the Almohads’ cohesion, yet the individual choices of a merchant, sailor, artisan, or household head are difficult to recover. Did some inhabitants open negotiations before the surrender? Did others hide goods, flee, or assist the besiegers? The fall of a city is recorded as a political event, while its people appear as revenue, population, or spoil. Their actions helped determine the outcome, but the narrative usually assigns agency to rulers and armies.

England presents a similar difficulty beneath the names of kings and tenants-in-chief. The *Domesday Book* records holdings, obligations, and owners after the conquest, but rarely preserves how a particular cultivator experienced the transfer. Resistance, cooperation, migration, concealment, and adaptation may leave traces in later disputes without producing a peasant account. The silence shows that the surviving documents were made for those who collected, judged, administered, and defended property, while leaving ordinary people’s activity largely unrecorded.

The Peace of God offers a small opening because it makes vulnerable people visible as a problem for public authority. Visibility does not confer voice: the peasant in a canon is a protected category, not a named speaker. The merchant whose goods survive a change of rulers may appear only through a tax or market privilege. The captive may be listed in a ransom agreement while the circumstances of capture, fear, bargaining, and return remain unrecorded. How far can historians reconstruct agency from rules written by bishops, surveys made by conquerors, and analyses composed by men concerned with dynasties?

The answer must remain provisional, but it is not empty. Choices made by ordinary people shaped which armies could eat, which cities could hold out, which lords could collect rent, and which religious prohibitions acquired force. Their actions are often visible indirectly, in interrupted settlements, negotiated submissions, preserved markets, abandoned estates, and the repeated need to forbid the same violence. The surviving record cannot restore every voice. It can still show that medieval war was never made by rulers alone, and that the contest over violence extended into the hands of those whom the documents most often reduce to property, protection, or spoil.

## Chapter 7: Steppe Powers and Connected Worlds

Temüjin first appears in *The Secret History of the Mongols* as a child marked by loss. His father, Yesügei, is poisoned while returning from arranging Temüjin’s marriage, and the Tayiči’ut abandon the household that had depended on him. Börte, Temüjin’s mother, keeps the family alive by gathering roots and catching fish, leading the children through a life stripped of the protection kinship was supposed to provide. The detail is political before it is sentimental. A household without allies could be left outside the distribution of meat, pasture, and safety. The boy who would become Chinggis Khan learned power first as the difference between being claimed by a group and being exposed to one.

The anonymous court historian who composed *The Secret History* in the thirteenth century does not present kinship as a stable inheritance. It is made and remade through obligation. Temüjin’s early companion, Jamuqa, becomes his anda, or sworn friend, and the two separate and reunite as their followers shift between them. Camps divide, men leave, and a group follows one leader rather than another. When Temüjin defeats rival groupings, he does not simply destroy every defeated family. He absorbs people, redistributes them, and places them within a new order whose central bond is loyalty to the khan rather than exclusive attachment to an old lineage. His marriage to Börte links him to the Onggirat through her father, Dei Sečen, but the alliance does not protect the household from violence. The Merkit seize Börte, and Temüjin turns to Toghril, khan of the Kereyit, and to Jamuqa for assistance. The rescue is a coalition assembled from remembered obligations: Temüjin’s father had once exchanged gifts with Toghril, and the old relationship could be converted into military support. The campaign against the Merkit shows kinship as a resource that could summon armed men. Börte’s return shows how household injury could become the occasion for a larger political alliance.

The group gathered around Temüjin was not held together by descent alone. *The Secret History* repeatedly records men being elevated because of what they did. Bo’orču joins Temüjin after helping him recover stolen horses; Jelme serves him when food and water are scarce; Muqali and Boroqul become trusted commanders through service rather than a simple ranking of noble birth. The text later preserves Chinggis Khan’s speeches to companions who remained with him during hunger, flight, and defeat. Service under pressure became a title to office. This rearrangement of status is clearest in the treatment of defeated enemies. After the battle with the Tayiči’ut, Chinggis Khan orders the people of the defeated group divided into units and incorporated into his own commands. The *Secret History* describes a similar logic when he reorganizes the army into tens, hundreds, thousands, and units of ten thousand. The decimal structure placed men from different lineages under appointed commanders, disrupting older loyalties as it organized soldiers. A household could remain a household, but military obedience now passed through a chain that led upward to the khan.

Kinship did not disappear. Chinggis Khan’s sons received territories and responsibilities; brothers and close companions remained central to the distribution of power; marriage continued to join ruling families. Yet the story insists that inherited connection alone was dangerous. Chinggis Khan quarrels with his brothers over authority, and the conflict with his half-brother Belgütei exposes the volatile boundary between family honour and political discipline. When a dispute becomes an injury within the ruling household, the khan must decide whether the old relationship or the new command structure will prevail.

The decision repeatedly favours obedience. After the defeat of Jamuqa and the consolidation of power, Chinggis Khan punishes those who abandon a leader in battle or seize goods without permission. A warrior may not take booty before distribution; a commander may not hold back a unit. Rules that seem harsh in isolation turn mobile followers into a force whose members can expect others to perform their assigned duties. The *Secret History*’s portrait is not of an isolated conqueror whose personal ferocity explains expansion. It is of a man who converts remembered dependence into organized loyalty, recruits from households, promotes men who have proved themselves, absorbs defeated people, and disciplines companions who treat old privilege as a licence to disobey. That transformation depended on the ecology of the steppe, but ecology did not perform it by itself. Pasture allowed households to move with herds and made rapid concentration possible; it also scattered people across a wide landscape and made allegiance negotiable. A leader who could not protect followers or distribute animals lost them. Chinggis Khan’s achievement lay in making movement serve a hierarchy that could survive the departure of one camp and the incorporation of another. The household remained the smallest political world, yet it no longer set the limit of political belonging.

A mounted army began with animals, while the Mongol advantage lay in converting pasture, breeding, riding skill, information, and command into a sequence repeatable across campaigns. A warrior who arrived on one horse was not the whole unit of movement. The army’s speed depended on remounts, and remounts depended on herds carried or encountered along routes whose water, grass, and seasonal conditions had been assessed in advance.

Mongol riders commonly maintained several horses and changed mounts during a journey, allowing one animal to recover while another carried the rider. The practice reduced exhaustion over long distances and made a column appear to move faster than an army relying on a single mount per soldier. Horses also carried bows, spare equipment, food, and sometimes the means of crossing rivers. The rider’s weapon was supported by a mobile household of animals. Without that reserve, the celebrated bow and mounted manoeuvre would have lost much of their range.

Movement required knowledge before it required speed. Scouts searched for water, pasture, roads, river crossings, enemy camps, and the condition of local settlements. Captives, merchants, allied rulers, and displaced people could provide information about distances and political divisions. Mongol commanders entered landscapes informed by routes gathered through human testimony, using local knowledge to decide where a force could separate, reunite, or sustain itself.

Recruitment drew on the same social world. Chinggis Khan’s army included his own followers, troops supplied by subordinate rulers, men incorporated after conquest, and specialists whose value lay in craft or knowledge. The decimal organization placed these men into units that could be counted and commanded. A captured population could supply immediate booty as well as soldiers, artisans, interpreters, scribes, engineers, or guides. The army’s composition changed as it expanded, and expansion provided skills that widened its reach.

Commanders received responsibility through service and trust, not only through descent. The *Secret History*’s accounts of Bo’orču and Jelme connect promotion to particular acts performed when Temüjin’s position was weak. Later, commanders such as Muqali were entrusted with enormous authority because they had demonstrated loyalty and administrative ability. Aristocratic rank remained useful, but the army made room for men whose political standing grew from usefulness to the khan.

Signals, messengers, agreed formations, and appointed commanders allowed a force to divide without dissolving. A wing could move around an enemy position while another held the centre; a retreat could draw pursuers into a prepared attack; detachments could converge after travelling by separate routes. Such tactics required soldiers to understand when not to pursue and officers to communicate a plan whose immediate purpose some units could not see. Command transformed scattered movement into coordinated force.

The remount system also affected the rhythm of violence. A fast-moving army could approach before a town had gathered supplies or before a neighbouring ruler had decided whether to resist. If resistance prolonged a siege, Mongol forces could draw on surrounding pasture, seize animals, and use engineers and specialists taken from earlier conquests. Movement produced surprise; capture supplied the means for the next advance. Yet a mobile military advantage did not equal an empire. Horses could carry a command across the steppe, but they could not assess tribute, secure roads, translate an ultimatum, register households, or maintain obedience after the army had departed. The system became imperial when the mounted force was joined to messengers, censuses, officials, hostages, tax collectors, and appointed governors.

Giovanni da Pian del Carpine entered that world as a Franciscan envoy in 1245, when Pope Innocent IV sent him eastward after Mongol armies had devastated much of eastern Europe. His account, *The Story of the Mongols Whom We Call Tartars*, was written after the journey and combines observation with information gathered from interpreters and people who had encountered the conquerors. Carpine travelled through Bohemia, Poland, and Rus before reaching the Mongol court, crossing a political landscape in which the memory of invasion was still immediate.

At Kiev, he found a city taken and damaged during the Mongol advance. His description of its reduced population gives conquest a material scale: streets and settlements were not only scenes of battle but points in a network whose inhabitants had been killed, carried away, or displaced. He continued through lands under Mongol power, where local rulers and communities lived within obligations imposed by the conquerors. The road itself marked a new political geography.

Carpine’s journey was diplomatic, but it was also an administrative event. He needed permission to pass, guides to interpret the route, animals for travel, and access to officials who could forward him toward the khan. At the Mongol court, envoys were received, questioned, and sent onward according to procedures controlled by rulers. Western Europeans experienced the empire as a managed passage through permissions, inspections, relays, and demands. His report describes the Mongols as disciplined and attentive to command. He emphasizes their skill as riders, the number of horses available to them, and their ability to travel over difficult distances. He also records the harsh consequences imposed on peoples who resisted. Cities could be destroyed, inhabitants killed or enslaved, and lands divided after conquest. Carpine’s account does not soften this violence, but its very form places destruction beside taxation, submission, and communication.

The Mongols demanded tribute from subject peoples and expected rulers to recognize their supremacy. Submission could require gifts, hostages, military support, or attendance at the khan’s court. A conquered ruler might retain local authority, but that authority operated under Mongol confirmation. The empire could govern through existing elites while reserving the right to remove them. The terms of submission were established through the threat or use of force and maintained through recurring obligations.

Diplomatic travel followed routes made possible by conquest. Carpine’s movement from eastern Europe toward Mongolia passed through territories linked by Mongol authority, though the journey remained difficult and dangerous. The imperial system used relay stations and fresh animals to move messages and officials. Later descriptions of the yam show a network of stations supported by requisitioned resources and protected by command. A messenger’s speed was an administrative product: horses, station keepers, roads, orders, and penalties had to work together.

The Mongols also treated envoys as bearers of political consequence. Carpine’s mission carried a papal letter, and the response from the Mongol court demanded submission rather than an equal exchange between distant sovereigns. Diplomacy gave coercion a formal channel. A letter could announce the expected place of a ruler within the imperial order; a refusal could be interpreted as defiance; an envoy could return with terms that allowed a state to calculate whether resistance was possible.

Carpine encountered people from different regions, languages, and religious backgrounds. The court depended on interpreters and officials drawn from conquered populations, because a realm stretching from China to the western steppe could not be governed by one language or one local tradition. His report is often hostile in its descriptions, yet it records a world in which Mongol power relied on movement across cultural boundaries.

Rus cities and populations bore the costs of invasion, and tribute extracted from them supported a ruling system whose centre lay far away. Once conquest had occurred, however, the Mongols had to count, tax, communicate with, and restrain the people they had subordinated. The route Carpine travelled was made by violence, but it remained open through administration. The same empire that destroyed towns organized the movement of envoys through their ruins and successor settlements.

A durable imperial order needs rules, and the Mongol Empire is often said to have possessed one especially important body of rules: the Yassa. In popular accounts, the Yassa appears as a written code issued by Chinggis Khan, comprehensive enough to regulate military discipline, taxation, religion, family life, and criminal punishment. This image offers a neat explanation for Mongol expansion: mobility supplied the force, while a universal legal code supplied the state.

David Morgan has warned that this picture rests on evidence more fragile than its confidence suggests. Later Persian and Arabic authors refer to the Yassa, and Mongol rulers invoke the customs or commands of Chinggis Khan, but no complete text survives. The word may refer to orders issued by the khan, to established practice, or to remembered regulations whose contents changed as they were transmitted. Treating every reference as a fragment of one written code creates a document the sources do not provide.

Thomas Allsen’s work points toward a more historically grounded understanding of Mongol law and government. Imperial commands, court practice, customary norms, decrees, and religious regulations could operate together without being assembled into one code. A ruler could issue a specific order concerning military discipline or taxation, and later officials could invoke Chinggis Khan’s authority when applying it. The authority of a command did not depend on its belonging to a fixed legal volume.

The evidence does support strong and repeated rules. The Mongol army punished unauthorized plunder, required obedience to commanders, regulated the distribution of booty, and protected the movement of messengers. The imperial system registered populations, assessed obligations, and imposed taxes. These practices could remain consistent across distant territories because officials, commanders, and rulers referred to the precedent of the founding khan. Consistency, however, is not codification, and memory is not a surviving statute book.

A mobile empire needed rules that could travel: orders intelligible to commanders, penalties enforceable across camps, and procedures that allowed tribute and messages to pass between regions. The evidence supports a disciplined political order whose rules were carried through command, custom, and administrative practice, without requiring a single comprehensive code. It does not establish that every area of Mongol life was governed by a formally compiled Yassa.

Mongol government rested on practices too durable and extensive to constitute improvisation. When Persian historians, Armenian chroniclers, Chinese officials, or later Mongol rulers refer to the Yassa, they preserve evidence of the authority attached to Chinggis Khan’s commands and precedent. They do not automatically preserve the contents of a unified code. The imperial order was real, but its reality lay in enacted decisions and institutions rather than in a text that can be read whole.

The difference becomes visible when Mongol domains are compared by what they did after conquest. The campaigns of Chinggis Khan and his successors could destroy cities and kill inhabitants on a massive scale. Nishapur, Merv, and other centres became examples in later histories of the price of resistance. The terror was deliberate: a city that resisted might be made an example so that another city would submit before battle. Violence was communication as well as destruction. Yet the same rulers who used terror sought to preserve the movement of merchants and officials. The empire linked China, Central Asia, Iran, the steppe, and eastern Europe through routes that had existed before the Mongols but now fell, for a time, under more extensive political protection. Merchants received privileges, travelled with official passes, and could appeal to imperial authorities when local officials interfered with trade. The state profited from exchange through taxes and fees. A merchant who crossed several jurisdictions carried revenue toward the rulers who maintained the route.

The contrast between Merv and a protected caravan is not a contradiction. Submission and destruction were paired instruments. A city that resisted could be annihilated; a city that submitted could retain officials, markets, and religious institutions under new obligations. The difference depended on military circumstances, local negotiation, the value of the place, and the willingness of its rulers to accept Mongol supremacy. The empire did not apply one uniform treatment to every population because its goal was obedience and revenue, though it repeatedly used destruction to obtain them.

Census-taking made that aim measurable. In northern China, Iran, and the western territories, Mongol rulers sought information about households, craftsmen, animals, land, and taxable resources. Although the precise forms varied by region and the surviving figures do not provide a single empire-wide account, they show officials turning conquered populations into categories that could be assigned obligations. A household counted in a register could be taxed or recruited; an artisan could be transferred to a workshop serving the court.

When an army left, a town remained within imperial knowledge: officials could assess its population, assign taxes to its merchants, and attach its artisans to a ruler or military unit. Registers converted people into administrative objects, but they also allowed distant rulers to govern without permanently occupying every settlement. The imperial centre could act through numbers carried by scribes and enforced by local intermediaries.

Religious policy followed a similar practical pattern. Mongol rulers patronized different faiths and granted exemptions or privileges to clerics. At Karakorum, as William of Rubruck later observed, churches, mosques, and temples stood within a cosmopolitan capital that housed merchants, envoys, artisans, and officials from across Eurasia. Founded by Ögedei in the 1230s, Karakorum was an administrative centre with walls, markets, workshops, and quarters for people of different origins. It was also a fixed city serving a ruling elite whose political culture remained connected to mobile camps. This tolerance had limits. It was not a modern doctrine of equal citizenship and could be withdrawn when political circumstances changed. Religious communities competed for patronage, and rulers favoured clerics who prayed for the khan, advised the court, or administered protected property. Still, the absence of a single required imperial religion allowed people and institutions to move across conquered territories. Muslim administrators could serve Mongol rulers in Iran; Chinese officials could work in northern China; Christian envoys could reach the court. Cultural difference became a resource of government.

The empire’s officials were often themselves products of conquest. Chinese and Central Asian administrators, Persian scribes, Turkic commanders, Armenian and Georgian intermediaries, and European or Muslim merchants entered a political world whose ruling dynasty was Mongol but whose working languages and customs were multiple. The Mongols did not govern by replacing every existing elite with men from Mongolia. They selected, subordinated, relocated, and promoted people who could make the empire function. Such recruitment sometimes preserved local practices and sometimes uprooted communities, especially when artisans or specialists were moved to distant centres. This mixture altered the conquerors as well. The Uyghur script became central to Mongol writing; Persian bureaucratic traditions shaped government in Iran; Chinese institutions structured Yuan rule. Mongol rulers adopted scripts, religions, titles, administrative forms, and court practices from the peoples they governed. Conquest transformed the ruling group as the conquerors carried their political habits into settled societies and absorbed the techniques needed to govern them.

The western khanates reveal another difference. In the Jochid lands, Mongol rulers relied on steppe mobility, tribute from Rus princes, control of river routes, and commerce connecting the Black Sea to Central Asia. Rus princes travelled to the khan’s court to receive confirmation, and local administration continued through them. The khan claimed supremacy, demanded payment, and reserved the right to punish disobedience while using existing political structures to collect what was owed.

In Iran, the Ilkhanate confronted older urban and agrarian systems whose tax records, irrigation works, religious foundations, and provincial offices could not simply be discarded. Mongol rulers employed Persian viziers and scribes, adopted forms of kingship legible to Iranian subjects, and relied on cities whose wealth came from cultivation and trade. Their armies remained essential, but governing meant repairing or exploiting fiscal structures that had survived the first conquest. The later reforms associated with Ghazan Khan attempted to regulate taxation, coinage, and official abuse because arbitrary extraction threatened the revenue on which the state depended.

China presented a different administrative scale. Yuan rulers inherited and adapted bureaucratic practices designed for a densely populated agrarian empire. Census registers, taxation, grain transport, official appointments, and urban administration became central to their rule. The conquest brought warfare, population loss, discrimination, and new hierarchies, but it also connected the Chinese domains to a wider imperial circuit in which Central Asian Muslims, Tibetan Buddhists, Chinese officials, and merchants from distant regions could serve the court. The conquerors governed China through institutions whose complexity exceeded the arrangements of a mobile camp.

Trade depended on this combination. Pastoralists supplied horses, livestock, and hides; settled regions supplied grain, textiles, metalwork, and manufactured objects. Merchants moved along routes where Mongol protection could reduce some risks while imperial taxes and official demands created others. The great expansion did not invent exchange across Eurasia, but it brought many routes under rulers who understood that merchants were useful when alive, mobile, and solvent. A caravan could be taxed repeatedly; a destroyed caravan produced only a short-term gain. The same calculations shaped policies toward craftsmen. Conquered artisans were sometimes relocated to serve the court, the army, or the construction of cities. Their movement spread techniques across regions, and the empire’s cosmopolitan character included deportation alongside other forms of migration. A metalworker from a captured city, a scribe taken into service, or a musician brought to a court could become part of cultural mixing through a process that began in defeat.

The problem of distance joined the destructive and integrative sides of Mongol rule. A ruler in Mongolia could not personally supervise tribute from Rus, a caravan crossing Central Asia, a census in northern China, and a tax dispute in Iran. Orders had to be written, translated, carried, and enforced by officials whose interests might diverge from the centre. The yam, the census, the appointment of local rulers, and the protection of merchants extended imperial reach beyond the army’s presence.

When the empire fragmented among the Chaghatayid, Jochid, Ilkhanid, and Yuan realms, these practices did not vanish. Political unity weakened, and warfare among Mongol successor states disrupted trade and redirected loyalties. Yet the successor states retained taxation, diplomatic travel, religious patronage, census-taking, and cultural exchange developed during the period of expansion. The connected world outlasted the single command that had briefly held it together.

Steppe mobility made rapid conquest possible because pasture, horses, remounts, knowledge of routes, and flexible recruitment allowed armies to appear across distances that strained the resources of settled opponents. The empire endured where that advantage was joined to promotions that rewarded service, commands that reorganized kinship, registers that counted households, officials who collected tribute, relays that carried orders, and policies that kept merchants and specialists moving. Violence could open a corridor, but it could not by itself maintain the road.

Mounted power gave the conquerors an unusual ability to concentrate force and punish resistance. Imperial power required them to make conquered populations legible, useful, and governable across regions with different languages, economies, religions, and political traditions. Their expansion was aggressive, often catastrophic, and repeatedly dependent on terror. It was also sustained by the institutional labour that followed the charge: counting, appointing, transporting, taxing, translating, protecting, and incorporating.

## Chapter 8: States, Slavery, and the Atlantic World

In 1685 Louis XIV issued the Code Noir for the French colonies. The ordinance addressed governors, intendants, and other officials in a world where French plantations already depended on African labour, but it did more than describe an existing custom. It gave colonial violence a vocabulary, assigned duties to owners and enslaved people, and placed the king’s officers around the plantation as guardians of an order whose profits came from compulsion. Its thirty-eight articles moved from religion to status, marriage, punishment, food, clothing, and manumission. Read in sequence, they show slavery being made durable through several kinds of rule at once.

The first article required the expulsion of Jews from the islands. The second ordered that all enslaved people be instructed and baptised as Roman Catholics. Masters had to provide that instruction, and government supervision extended to religious affiliation. A person held in bondage was to be baptised, married, and buried within the Catholic church even as the same law defined that person as movable property. Christianity imposed obligations upon the master and gave the enslaved a recognised place within a sacramental community; property law preserved the master’s power over labour, movement, and sale. The contradiction was not resolved. It was administered.

Descent made that administration hereditary. Children born to an enslaved woman and a free man followed the condition of the mother if the father was not legally married to her, while the children of enslaved women were themselves enslaved. The rule answered a planter’s need to make labour inheritable and ownership transferable. A child born on a plantation entered the owner’s account as an addition to wealth. A sexual relationship, whether coerced, tolerated, or formalised, could produce another person whose labour was claimed by law.

Marriage was permitted among enslaved people with the owner’s consent. An enslaved man could not marry a woman held by another master without permission from both owners, and children remained with their mother’s owner. Family life was recognised, but property claims governed its limits. A marriage could exist inside slavery without giving the couple control over residence, work, or the future of their children. The family clauses did not merely leave kinship unprotected from sale; they organised kinship around the plantation’s title to persons.

The official who approved a marriage, the priest who recorded a baptism, and the owner who consented to a transfer each placed enslaved people within categories that courts and administrators could read. The family could become the channel through which status passed from one generation to the next. Yet marriage and baptism also created relationships that masters could not wholly reduce to inventory. A church register named a person who was also owned.

The ordinance required owners to provide specified quantities of food and cloth. It forbade them from forcing enslaved people to work on Sundays and Catholic feast days except in urgent circumstances, and it required masters to maintain those who were old, sick, or disabled. These duties did impose limits, but they also served the plantation. Labour had to be sustained, and an owner could not simply shift the cost of dependence onto the wider community. Protection here was a condition of preserving property, not an alternative to it.

Punishment made the state’s interest plainer. Enslaved people could not carry weapons, gather in groups, or leave the plantation without permission. A first attempt to escape could bring branding and the cutting of an ear; a second could bring another mark and the cutting of a hamstring; a third could bring death. Governors and councils retained power to decide particular cases, but the sequence defined unauthorised movement as an attack on the owner’s command rather than a dispute over labour.

The ordinance prescribed corporal punishment for theft and other disobedience. Owners could not kill enslaved people at will, and the text threatened penalties against masters who mutilated or killed those they owned. Violence was therefore placed under official control without ownership being challenged. A governor could intervene when punishment was judged excessive, while enforcing the rules that made flight, assembly, and weapons dangerous to plantation order. The state claimed the right to discipline the disciplinarian because it claimed a larger interest in preserving the labour system.

Manumission reveals the same structure from another direction. An owner could free an enslaved person before a notary or through a declaration before a parish priest, subject to prescribed formalities. A freed person acquired the status of a free subject, and the law treated the change as meaningful. Yet freedom required an owner’s decision, a document, and official recognition. It could not be asserted against the owner’s title. Manumission softened the system at its edges while confirming the owner’s power at its centre.

The king’s ordinance named people as Catholics, spouses, parents, workers, dependants, offenders, and possible free subjects. It named them as property too. Religious instruction could coexist with sale, family recognition with hereditary bondage, limits on punishment with forced labour, and manumission with the presumption of ownership. Although the Code Noir did not create every act of cruelty in Martinique or Saint-Domingue, it created a field in which the master’s economic command, the official’s jurisdiction, and the church’s records reinforced one another.

By the eighteenth century, the plantation was not simply a place where one person hurt another. Law arranged the conditions under which such acts could be repeated, recorded, inherited, and defended. Its clauses made repetition administratively possible while leaving enslaved people with relationships, religious identities, knowledge of procedure, and opportunities to press against the boundaries drawn around them. A baptism did not end ownership, but it produced a record. A marriage did not prevent sale, but it created a claim of kinship. Manumission did not undo the system, but it showed that status could be altered through an act the law recognised.

In 1781 the Zong left the African coast under the command of Luke Collingwood, carrying enslaved people toward Jamaica. William Gregson and his partners had purchased the vessel, and their commercial world included ships, cargoes, credit, and marine insurance. A policy treated the enslaved people aboard as property. Their lives entered the voyage as both human bodies and financial units.

After leaving Africa, the Zong became lost and the voyage lasted longer than its owners expected. Water ran short. In late November, Collingwood and the crew killed more than one hundred enslaved people by throwing them overboard. Additional people were thrown into the sea on a later day. Although the surviving records do not preserve each decision or the identity of everyone killed, they preserve the central fact that the captain and crew destroyed part of the cargo while the ship was at sea.

The owners claimed that the killings were a jettison of goods made necessary by emergency. Had the people died from disease or lack of water, the loss might have fallen upon the owners. If they were thrown overboard to save the ship and its remaining cargo, the owners sought recovery from insurers under the law of general average. The economic language was exact: people became cargo whose destruction could be presented as a sacrifice for the safety of the whole venture.

The first case was heard at Guildhall in London in 1783. The insurers resisted payment, and the dispute reached the Court of King’s Bench, where William Murray, Lord Mansfield, presided. The hearing concerned the insurance claim and whether the circumstances satisfied the policy, rather than serving as a prosecution for murder. Mansfield ordered a new trial after defects emerged in the evidence, including uncertainty about the water supply and events aboard the ship. The legal process narrowed the killings into questions that could be handled within commercial doctrine.

Granville Sharp seized upon the case for another purpose. He had already challenged the legal treatment of enslaved people in England, and the Zong killings showed him how property language could absorb deliberate violence. He sought to bring the matter before the authorities as a criminal offence, but no prosecution followed. While Mansfield’s proceedings did not establish that killing enslaved people was lawful, they showed that a court could encounter the deaths primarily through a dispute over insurance.

The Zong was profitable before anyone reached a courtroom because its owners could calculate a loss, a premium, and a possible recovery. Jamaica’s plantation economy gave each surviving person a prospective value as labour, while the ship’s policy converted that value into a financial claim. Scarcity shaped the decision to throw people overboard, but scarcity operated inside a commercial system that had already treated human beings as insurable property. The violence at sea was an extreme act made intelligible by ordinary accounting.

The case ended without a judicial declaration that the killings were acceptable. Mansfield’s ruling and Sharp’s agitation instead exposed the limits of a legal order that recognised property in people while refusing to place the full violence of that relation at the centre of the trial. The Zong’s dead appeared as the substance of a claim, the objects of an argument about necessity, and the reason Sharp demanded a criminal investigation.

Olaudah Equiano entered that Atlantic world as a child, but the path he later described did not move in a straight line from victimhood to freedom. In The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano, published in London in 1789, he presented himself as someone carried through several systems of command and as an individual who learned how those systems worked. His account begins in the region he called Essaka, in what is now southeastern Nigeria, where he described the household, agriculture, religion, and local practices he remembered before his capture. Africa was not an ornamental beginning to an abolitionist book. It established what captivity had interrupted.

Equiano wrote that he and his sister were kidnapped while their parents were away. He was about eleven years old. The Narrative gives the event a domestic scale before placing it within the Atlantic trade: two children removed from their home, separated from one another, and passed among people who treated them as saleable dependants. As he moved towards the coast, he learned more about the trade from experience and from explanations given to him. The account joins private loss to a system that converted capture into transport and sale. He was carried to the coast and shipped across the Atlantic. In his account, the Middle Passage is crowded with sickness, fear, confinement, and the sight of people who died during the crossing. He described the hold as a place where the air became difficult to breathe and where proximity to death altered the meaning of the voyage. He also described his astonishment at the ship, its machinery, and the sea. The child trying to understand the vessel was being made into cargo by the people who controlled it.

After being taken ashore in Barbados, where he remained briefly, he was transported to Virginia and purchased by Michael Pascal, a Royal Navy officer, who renamed him Gustavus Vassa. Equiano learned that an owner could impose a name and that movement between owners did not end dependence. With Pascal he entered naval life, crossed the Atlantic repeatedly, and acquired experience of ships, warfare, and maritime work. He served during the Seven Years’ War in a world of naval provisioning, discipline, and prize-taking.

The sea exposed him to violence, but it also gave him skills that could later be exchanged. He learned navigation, sailing, and the handling of equipment, and became familiar with routes between Britain, the Caribbean, and North America. A person whom law treated as property could still become useful because he could read instruments, perform skilled labour, and understand commercial movement. Those abilities increased his value to an owner; they also gave him knowledge with which to seek a different position.

Pascal sent Equiano to England for schooling. In London he learned to read and write, encountering Christian instruction alongside the practical demands of service. The Narrative presents education as work rather than miraculous transformation. He read the Bible, learned arithmetic, and studied the language of the people who governed and purchased him. Literacy allowed him to interpret religious claims, record transactions, write letters, and address a public that included people who had never seen the slave ship or plantation.

In 1763 Pascal sold Equiano to Robert King, a Quaker merchant of Philadelphia. King placed him on ships trading among Caribbean ports and the mainland. Equiano’s work involved loading, buying, selling, and navigating. King permitted him to earn money in his own time, and Equiano saved towards the price of his freedom. The arrangement remained coercive: King owned him, and his ability to accumulate money depended on work his owner allowed. Commercial skill nevertheless created a route through the legal system that had confined him.

Equiano described saving the forty pounds required for his purchase. The sum was substantial for a seaman, and he had to protect it from people who might take advantage of his status. In 1766 King accepted the money and freed him.

Freedom did not remove the Atlantic from Equiano’s life. He continued to work at sea and later joined expeditions connected with the search for a Northwest Passage. He participated in commercial ventures and managed money, clothing, books, and travel with an independence unavailable when Pascal and King owned him. His choices remained constrained by racial discrimination and by the danger that free Black people could be kidnapped or falsely claimed, but he no longer had to treat every voyage as another owner’s command.

The Narrative follows a conversion as well as a commercial education. Equiano became a Christian and treated belief as a source of moral judgement, but his Christianity did not make him passive. He used Christian language to condemn the treatment of Africans and to challenge Christians who profited from slavery. The Code Noir had placed baptism inside a system of ownership; Equiano used baptism, scripture, and conscience to expose that contradiction. Religion could serve plantation order, but it could also furnish an enslaved or formerly enslaved writer with a public vocabulary against it. His account of the Middle Passage performs similar work. Equiano connected the voyage to the trade that supplied colonial labour and European wealth, presenting the ship as more than evidence of personal suffering. He addressed readers in Britain who purchased sugar or accepted the prestige of empire. By placing those readers near the hold, he made consumption and distant violence part of the same narrative. The ship was a mechanism of profit, but it was also a place from which testimony could travel.

The authorship of the Narrative has been debated because some details of Equiano’s early life may resemble information available from other sources and because the precise location of his birth has been questioned. That debate affects the use of particular passages. It does not erase the documented adult life of Gustavus Vassa, the manumission certificate and other records associated with him, or the political work of the book. Equiano wrote as a witness to a system and as a participant in the commercial and religious networks that made abolitionist publication possible. He selected incidents that could persuade. The book contains fear, but also calculation; it records degradation, but also the acquisition of skills. Equiano did not present agency as proof that slavery was mild. His capacity to save money depended on a narrow opening within an institution that could have closed it. His ability to write depended on education acquired under unequal conditions. His later activism depended on literacy, mobility, and contacts that slavery had partly forced upon him and that he redirected against the trade.

In London he became involved with abolitionist circles, including the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade, founded in 1787. His Narrative appeared as parliamentary debate, petitions, religious dissent, and commercial interests brought the trade into public controversy. Equiano promoted the book, subscribed readers, and used its sales to support himself and his cause. Authorship became political labour: he made his life evidence while also making it an argument about the structure that had carried him from Africa to Britain. His language repeatedly links capture to plantation production. The enslaved person is seized, sold, transported, and put to work; sugar, tobacco, and other goods move towards European markets. The stages are distinct, but none is self-sufficient. A merchant needs ships and credit. A plantation needs labour and legal protection. A purchaser needs commodities at a price that conceals coercion. Equiano’s movements allowed him to see those connections as captive, sailor, servant, wage earner, free man, and author.

The choices of enslaved people within this system were often small in scale and large in consequence. A person might preserve a family tie, learn a trade, bargain for better treatment, hide money, escape, refuse work, seek a court, or use religious instruction for a purpose the master did not intend. Equiano’s savings were not a revolt in the usual sense, but they altered the relation between his labour and his legal status. His writing carried an enslaved person’s analysis into the public sphere and forced readers to confront agency that ownership tried to deny.

The plantation owner, the captain of the Zong, the insurer, the colonial governor, and the enslaved worker did not possess equal power or pursue identical ends. The owner sought labour and returns; the insurer sought to distribute commercial risk; the governor sought order; the captain sought to complete a voyage; the enslaved person sought survival, connection, freedom, or some combination of these. Law placed their unequal decisions within a common structure without making their motives identical.

Profit supplied pressure to obtain labour, maintain production, and minimise losses. Law supplied categories of ownership, inheritance, punishment, marriage, baptism, and insurance. Resistance forced owners and officials to spend resources on patrols, courts, punishment, negotiation, and surveillance, while opening routes through which enslaved people could act. These processes cannot be reduced to one another. Planters wanted returns before a statute authorised their methods; officials authorised violence in forms that exceeded any single planter’s calculation; enslaved people altered the system through choices owners had not designed.

The Code Noir shows authorisation at the level of clauses. The Zong shows what happened when a commercial decision entered court. Equiano shows how a person moved through the same world without becoming identical to its categories. The plantation depended on a chain in which ownership, shipping, credit, colonial government, parish records, courts, and markets made one another useful, with cruelty everywhere.

French Caribbean law and the Zong litigation occupied different legal settings. The Code Noir was a royal ordinance for colonies; the Zong case was heard in London as an insurance dispute. One openly regulated slavery’s domestic relations and punishments. The other did not begin as a case about slavery’s legitimacy. Yet both made the enslaved person available to a legal vocabulary of property. Under the Code Noir, status could pass through the mother and children could be inherited. In the Zong litigation, people could be treated as cargo whose destruction might generate compensation. Their differences reveal the breadth of the institution. French officials claimed to discipline masters and protect Catholic order, while English judges addressed the terms of marine insurance. Neither setting needed a single command directing every act of violence. Each supplied rules that allowed violence to be organised around a plantation or voyage. The Code Noir worked through status and colonial administration. The Zong dispute worked through risk, evidence, and commercial loss.

Equiano’s trajectory fits neither document. He was not a clause and not an insurance claim. He was moved through the world those instruments helped make, learned its languages, earned money within its markets, purchased his freedom, and wrote against the trade. His life shows that institutional power could be comprehensive without being complete. The system determined the conditions of his capture and sale, but not every use of his labour, every relationship he formed, or every meaning he gave to his experience.

Enslaved agency did not cause the law to recognise property in people, and planter profit did not eliminate the need for law. A runaway, a saved coin, a baptism, a petition, or a published Narrative could disturb slavery without standing outside it. The system endured by absorbing some actions, punishing others, and converting still others into evidence of its supposed benevolence.

By 1789 Equiano could address British readers as a free man, but the Zong dead had no comparable opportunity to speak in the litigation that followed their killing. The Code Noir preserved legal categories more readily than the voices of the people placed within them. Equiano’s book therefore does more than add personal feeling to administrative records. It changes the scale of the evidence. A law can show how a state classified a person; an insurance case can show how commerce valued a person; a life narrative can show how that person learned, calculated, endured, and answered.

Atlantic slavery was sustained when these scales met. A king’s ordinance, a planter’s account, a ship’s manifest, an insurer’s policy, a governor’s order, and a sailor’s labour could converge on the same human body. Violence became repeatable because it could be inherited, financed, administered, and defended. It remained contested because the people subjected to it made families, acquired skills, saved money, escaped, negotiated, worshipped, testified, and wrote. The law protected slavery, but it never fully possessed the lives it classified.

## Chapter 9: Gunpowder, Bureaucracy, and Mass Mobilization

Wars do not become larger merely because weapons become more powerful. They become larger when rulers can keep men supplied, replace losses, hold fortified places, and continue fighting after a single campaign has failed. Michael Roberts began the military revolution debate by placing that change in the century from about 1560 to 1660. Firearms and cannon mattered, but they mattered within a widening political scale. A ruler who could maintain an army through winter, replenish its powder and food, and coordinate operations in several theatres possessed a different kind of power from a prince who summoned a feudal host for a short campaign. Roberts connected military change to centralized government, religious conflict, and permanent competition among European states. His dates were chosen with care. Firearms had already appeared at Pavia in 1525, and artillery had helped take Constantinople in 1453. Neither event marked the sudden birth of modern war. The change gathered during the following century, when the Dutch revolt, the French Wars of Religion, the Thirty Years’ War, and the rivalry among Spain, France, Sweden, and the Dutch Republic made military preparation a standing concern. Maurice of Nassau’s reforms at the end of the sixteenth century used drill, ordered ranks, standardized commands, and printed military instruction to make infantry more predictable. The army was becoming a trained body rather than a crowd temporarily assembled around noble retainers.

Roberts gave technology a place without giving it first place. Drill answered the slow loading of matchlock firearms and the need to coordinate volleys through smoke and noise. Yet drill required wages, officers, inspection, written orders, and time. A formation could be copied on a parade ground only when someone supplied muskets, powder, clothing, food, and reliable pay. The tactical instrument and the administrative conditions arrived together, though they were not the same thing.

Geoffrey Parker accepted much of Roberts’s chronology but shifted attention toward fortification and siege warfare. His account of the “trace italienne” emphasized the low, thick, angled bastions that spread from Italian practice across Europe after artillery made tall medieval walls vulnerable. Palmanova, founded by Venice in 1593, was a fortified system whose geometry allowed defenders to cover the approaches with intersecting fire, while broad earthworks absorbed cannon shot better than masonry alone. An attacking army had to dig trenches, bring up guns, protect engineers, construct roads, and feed thousands of men during a siege that might last months.

Parker’s claim enlarged the meaning of military scale. If fortified towns multiplied, conquest required more than battlefield courage. Armies had to occupy territory and reduce fortified points one after another. The Dutch Republic’s war against Spain displayed the pattern: towns, canals, inundations, and garrisons made the map itself part of the conflict. France under Louis XIV later invested heavily in fortresses and siege trains, while Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban developed offensive and defensive methods that treated fortification as a technical discipline. War became expensive because the objects being fought over had become expensive to overcome.

The evidence does not permit a choice between the gun and the state. Cannon made certain fortifications necessary; fortifications made larger armies necessary; larger armies pressed governments to tax, borrow, register, and supervise more extensively. Parker’s fortress could slow an advance without deciding who had the resources to maintain the siege. Roberts’s drilled infantry could win a field without possessing the magazines required to remain there. A weapon’s tactical effect opened a problem. Fiscal and bureaucratic arrangements determined whether a government could solve it.

Army numbers reveal the pressure. European armies, excluding Russia and the Ottoman Empire, grew from roughly 300,000 men in the middle of the sixteenth century to more than a million by about 1780. The figures combine different definitions of an army and do not represent a precise head count of equally trained soldiers, but the direction is unmistakable. England, which could field perhaps 25,000 men in 1475, had around 199,000 under arms in 1760 during a period of repeated war. France’s forces expanded dramatically under Louis XIV, reaching hundreds of thousands when wartime establishments, militia, garrisons, and contractors are counted. Such numbers could not be sustained by a lord bringing his followers to the king’s banner.

Taxation supplied the arithmetic beneath the rhetoric of glory. After 1500, domain income alone rarely met the cost of artillery, fortifications, naval stores, mercenaries, uniforms, transport, and interest on loans. Governments extended excise duties, customs systems, direct taxes, tax farms, and public borrowing. Britain’s fiscal machinery, strengthened after the Glorious Revolution of 1688, enabled the government to borrow on terms that France often found more difficult, even though France had a larger population. The difference lay in the relationship among parliament, creditors, customs officials, and the treasury, with the musket itself offering no superior explanation.

A government able to borrow against future revenue could lose a battle and continue the war. One dependent on a ruler’s estate income or irregular levies might win a battle and still fail to occupy a province. The Dutch Republic’s taxation and credit helped it resist Spain; England’s later fiscal strength helped it maintain campaigns far from home. The arsenal at Venice showed the same principle in material form. Ships and guns could be produced efficiently only where officials coordinated timber, iron, rope, powder, skilled workers, and payment.

Parker corrected any account that treated firearms as an independent cause, but fortifications were not sufficient either. The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453 involved enormous cannon, yet the fall of the city also depended on blockade, manpower, naval action, political isolation, and Mehmed II’s ability to feed and command a large army. The Ming state produced firearms and artillery while relying on administrative practices unlike those of France or the Dutch Republic. Technology travelled across borders more easily than the institutions that sustained it.

The Ottoman case makes that difference visible through people rather than weapons. The devşirme system, developed under Ottoman rulers from the fifteenth century, recruited Christian boys from parts of the Balkans for service to the sultan. Officials recorded recruitment in registers, assessed the boys, transported them, converted them to Islam, and assigned them according to qualities the state sought to measure. Some entered palace schools and could rise into administrative or military office. Others joined the Janissary corps, whose members served as salaried infantry attached directly to the ruler.

The process began in districts that did not choose to offer their sons. Officials selected boys according to age and physical condition, recorded names and households, and gathered them for transport. The registers gave the process an administrative shape: a boy was not merely taken by a soldier but entered a sequence of lists, examinations, transfers, and assignments. Halil İnalcık places the corps within the wider household of the sultan, where service, status, and reward were connected to personal dependence on the ruler rather than to inherited Muslim nobility.

Training followed selection. Boys placed with Turkish-speaking Muslim households learned language, religious practice, and agricultural discipline before later assignment. Those chosen for palace schools received more specialized education. A career could depend on literacy, obedience, military skill, and access to the court. Conversion was part of a political technology of recruitment: it severed an earlier local affiliation while creating a new one, though it did not erase the memories or social knowledge carried from the village.

The Janissaries’ value lay in regular service. They were paid, lodged, trained, and deployed as a corps rather than summoned for a single expedition. Firearms became increasingly important, particularly during the sixteenth century, but the gun did not create the corps. The corps made the gun usable at scale by supplying men who could drill, officers who could direct them, armourers who could maintain weapons, and a treasury that could pay them. Ottoman officials drew on provincial taxation, urban workshops, transport routes, and the imperial household to place Janissaries at key points of the army. Their service extended beyond the battlefield. Janissaries guarded fortresses, participated in sieges, and formed part of the military presence in Istanbul and provincial centres. The same organization that gave the sultan an infantry force created a political body with interests of its own. By the seventeenth century, recruitment increasingly became hereditary and the corps changed in composition. An arrangement built for central control could acquire social weight and resist the purposes for which it had been designed.

A recruitment register cannot tell us what every selected boy felt about the journey from a Balkan village to imperial service. It does show the state translating population into personnel through repeated procedures. The Janissary system was coercive and depended on lists, quotas, inspectors, transport, training establishments, pay chests, and promotion, giving the seizure of manpower an organized structure. Gunpowder gave the infantry new tactical possibilities; the devşirme gave the Ottoman government a durable route into the supply of infantry.

The cost of military expansion appears differently in Hans Jakob Christoffel von Grimmelshausen’s *Simplicius Simplicissimus*, a novel published in 1668 and set against the Thirty Years’ War. Grimmelshausen did not write a treasury account or a campaign diary. He wrote through the voice of Simplicius, a young man whose movement through war exposes what official military histories compress into the word “logistics.” Villages are where soldiers obtain food, horses, clothing, shelter, information, and money.

The novel opens with Simplicius in a rural household before soldiers enter his world. Troops attack and plunder the farm, carrying the boy into confusion. Requisition is not presented as an orderly exchange between government and subjects. Soldiers take what they need, and the household is broken apart by the demands of an army that is present without being fully supplied by the authority that sent it. The violence rests in ordinary things: food, livestock, bedding, clothing, tools, and buildings that can be searched or destroyed.

Grimmelshausen’s satire is sharp because the soldiers’ appetite has a bureaucratic background even when the village experiences it as disorder. Armies in the Thirty Years’ War were often raised and supplied through contractors, officers, contributions, and local exactions. A regiment might be paid irregularly by a prince while being expected to live from the territory through which it marched. A formal contribution could become an informal seizure when no official stood between a hungry company and a household’s stores. Requisition was both a fiscal practice and an immediate act of force.

Simplicius passes through several forms of military life, and his changing status alters what he can see. He becomes attached to soldiers, learns military customs, and moves within the world that has displaced him, inhabiting more than the role of a victim. The novel does not divide the world neatly between innocent civilians and aggressive soldiers, although it never turns that blurred boundary into moral equality. A person may be compelled into an army, benefit from its protection, participate in its predation, and remain vulnerable to a superior commander or a new unit passing through.

Quartering gives the pattern a fixed shape. Troops require houses in which to sleep and places for horses to be kept. Villagers must provide meals, fodder, and fuel. The burden is repeated because armies do not pass once and disappear. A unit may return, another may arrive, or a garrison may remain after a campaign. The same community can be stripped by successive claims, each justified by a different flag but producing the same shortage in the kitchen and stable.

In the novel, the military world is full of movement: marching, retreating, changing allegiance, seeking employment, and pursuing plunder. That movement does not make war fluid for everyone. Soldiers move because armies move; peasants are displaced because armies occupy. Roads become channels through which demands travel. A farm near a route can be more exposed than one farther away, while a walled town may gain protection from one force and become a target for another. Geography enters the fiscal system through the feet of soldiers and the wheels of wagons.

Illness carries the same burden. Disease accompanies crowded quarters, poor food, contaminated water, cold, exhaustion, and movement from one region to another. Grimmelshausen’s world does not separate the battlefield casualty from the fevered soldier or the civilian weakened by hunger. An army may lose men without fighting a major battle, and a village may be damaged by the medical consequences of military presence long after troops have departed. Disease turns the scale of mobilization against the people who host it.

The Thirty Years’ War involved armies whose supply systems were incomplete, uneven, and frequently privatized, a condition embedded in the conduct of the war rather than an accidental side effect of an otherwise efficient state. Commanders negotiated contributions from towns and districts; contractors advanced money or provisions; soldiers sought compensation through local extraction when wages failed. The state appeared through uniforms, orders, taxes, and claims, but its demands were often transmitted by men who had immediate reasons to take more than their instructions allowed.

Simplicius encounters the contradiction in personal form. Military service offers food, employment, status, and access to spoils, yet it also exposes him to hunger, disease, discipline, capture, and sudden changes of fortune. The novel’s picaresque movement is not simply a sequence of adventures. It follows the unstable position of a person trying to survive within a system that converts movement into opportunity for some and ruin for others. The army is both employer and predator.

Requisition changes the meaning of property. A household may own a horse, but an officer can treat it as necessary for military service. A store of grain belongs to a family, but an army can define it as a contribution owed to the war. A bed is a domestic object until a soldier is quartered in the room. The conversion occurs through command and is enforced by armed presence. The distinction between taxation and theft depends on who authorized the claim, who recorded it, and whether compensation was possible.

Grimmelshausen’s comic passages intensify rather than soften this observation. Bureaucratic language and military pretension sit beside hunger and improvisation. Men boast of rank while lacking shoes; officers invoke discipline while tolerating plunder; soldiers seek order because it may secure their pay, then violate it because violation supplies them. An army can be too large for its treasury and still be powerful enough to take from the countryside. Its administrative weakness becomes strength against unprotected households.

Displacement follows from repeated extraction. When food, animals, tools, and money are taken, people leave not because a single battle has commanded them to move but because continued residence becomes impossible. Some flee to towns, forests, relatives, or territories controlled by another ruler. Others remain and adapt to the passage of troops. Grimmelshausen’s narrative makes mobility a condition of survival without romanticizing the wanderer. Movement carries danger, dependence, and exposure to new authorities.

The narrative also records how war remakes knowledge. Simplicius learns to read signs unnecessary in peacetime: the appearance of troops, the meaning of uniforms, the likely consequences of a battle, and the dangers of being identified with a defeated side. Civilians acquire military intelligence because their safety depends on it. This learning is practical knowledge produced by coercion, not the same thing as political loyalty.

When the narrative turns toward the army’s internal hierarchy, violence and administration become inseparable. Orders move downward through officers, but provisions do not always move with equal reliability. A commander may possess authority over a regiment while lacking cash to pay it. A soldier may obey a tactical order because discipline is strong and disobey a supply order because hunger is stronger. The army’s capacity to fight depends on both forms of obedience, and the second cannot be secured through ceremony alone.

The war’s duration magnified every failure. A short campaign might be supported by stores prepared in advance; a conflict lasting from 1618 to 1648 required systems that could renew themselves. Governments borrowed, contracted, taxed, and negotiated, but no single authority controlled every troop in the field. Imperial, Catholic, Protestant, Swedish, French, and local forces operated through shifting combinations of public purpose and private military enterprise. The soldier’s employer could be a prince, a city, a general, or a contractor, while the civilian usually encountered only the immediate demand.

Simplicius’s movement through this world answers the military revolution debate from below. Powder had to be manufactured and transported; weapons had to be stored; soldiers had to practise; units had to be paid; horses had to be fed; roads had to be secured; wounded and sick men had to be managed. At every link, the weapon’s existence depended on offices and supplies. When those failed, the cost moved onto people who had little power to refuse.

Large armies required inhabitants to support soldiers, provide transport, surrender food, repair roads, house troops, pay taxes, or abandon homes, even when those inhabitants never became soldiers. The civilian population was mobilized even when it never received a uniform; the burden could be distributed through law, while weak or predatory law distributed it through fear.

The difference between the Ottoman and European paths becomes clearest when the Janissary register is placed beside Simplicius’s village. The Ottoman state sought to capture manpower at recruitment, process it through training, and retain it within a salaried household corps. Rulers in the Holy Roman Empire and its neighbours more often relied on a patchwork of levies, mercenary contracts, contributions, and territorial taxes. Both systems could produce formidable armies. They did not make soldiers in the same way.

The devşirme was selective and centralized. Officials recorded boys, moved them through supervised stages, and assigned them according to training and aptitude. Its coercion was concentrated at recruitment and embedded in a career structure. A Janissary’s connection to the sultan could bring pay, status, promotion, and access to the imperial household. The state did not simply ask a village to feed an army; it appropriated a portion of the population and invested in converting that portion into a permanent military resource.

European fiscal warfare more often extracted money and supplies from territories while recruiting men through contracts, press systems, local obligations, and mercenary networks. France’s standing army under the Bourbon monarchy moved toward regular wages, uniforms, depots, and centralized inspection, but it developed through older practices rather than replacing them at once. The Dutch Republic combined taxation, credit, fortified towns, and trained forces, while Habsburg armies depended on dynastic lands, provincial estates, contractors, and contributions from occupied regions. There was no single European model.

The Janissary corps rested on taxation from provinces, provisioning networks, arsenals, and the political authority of the sultan, making the contrast one of differing institutional arrangements rather than an efficient Ottoman system opposed to a chaotic European one. Its recruitment system could not by itself supply artillery, cavalry remounts, siege engineers, naval stores, or food for campaigns in Hungary, Anatolia, or the Arab provinces. European governments, meanwhile, learned to regularize recruitment and pay even when soldiers continued to live partly from local resources. The difference lay in the route by which manpower became available, not in the presence or absence of administration.

Military technologies travelled through both systems. Ottoman forces used cannon at Constantinople, field artillery in later campaigns, and firearms among Janissaries. European armies borrowed Ottoman, Italian, Dutch, and German practices while adapting them to local fortifications and fiscal conditions. A matchlock did not announce the political form of its user. Its effect depended on whether an army could train enough men to fire in sequence, replace powder, protect a supply train, and remain in the field.

Fortification exposes the same relationship. European rulers built bastioned towns that demanded specialized engineers and long sieges. The Ottoman Empire maintained major fortresses and used artillery to attack them, while also relying on roads, river transport, provincial forces, and frontier garrisons. Palmanova and the Ottoman fortress at Belgrade belonged to different political settings, yet each made military reach partly a question of masonry, magazines, and labour. A fortress extended violence in time because a government had to keep paying after the first shot.

Taxation linked the two paths in different forms. European states increasingly used excises, customs, direct taxes, public loans, and tax farms. The Ottoman government relied on land revenues, provincial assignments, customs, extraordinary levies, and the imperial household. Neither system made taxation a neutral transfer. Taxes could impoverish cultivators, provoke resistance, and force local officials to negotiate or coerce. The administrative question was whether revenue could be collected repeatedly without destroying the population and converted into soldiers and supplies before an enemy did the same.

The scale of armies made that question unavoidable. European forces grew greatly between 1550 and 1780, while the Ottoman Empire maintained armies that could be comparably large in periods of major war. Numbers conceal differences among garrisons, field troops, militia, auxiliaries, and camp followers. A force of 100,000 men was a moving settlement requiring food, animals, roads, and people to keep it functioning, not a single tactical instrument. Here Grimmelshausen’s civilians enter the comparison again. European armies often converted local territory into a supply system because central treasuries could not deliver every ration, producing the requisition, quartering, and displacement that shape *Simplicius Simplicissimus*. Ottoman armies also requisitioned, quartered, and burdened provinces; campaigns in the Balkans and against Iran depended on supplies gathered along routes and on the cooperation or submission of local authorities. The difference lay in the forms of record, the distribution of responsibility, and the degree to which the imperial centre could coordinate provincial resources.

A weapon changed the terms of a fight. The bastion made assault more difficult, while taxation determined whether a siege train could remain before it and therefore who could afford to fight repeatedly. The musket rewarded drill, but recruitment and wages determined whether drilled infantry existed. Cannon could breach a wall, but transport, powder manufacture, engineering, and food determined whether the breach led to possession. The battlefield effect was immediate; the fiscal effect accumulated over years.

Rulers could seek the same things before and after gunpowder: territory, tribute, dynastic security, religious authority, commercial access, revenge, or prestige. Gunpowder made some forms of pursuit more demanding and, where administration succeeded, more durable; these aims themselves did not become more aggressive. A prince who wanted a fortress before artillery might need a quick assault or a blockade; after artillery, he might require engineers, heavy guns, trenches, and credit. The object of war could remain while its machinery changed around it.

The Ottoman devşirme shows that military reach could be built through controlled recruitment into a household corps. European fiscal-military states show another route: draw revenue from a wider population, borrow against future income, contract soldiers, and construct offices capable of managing them. Grimmelshausen reveals the human cost when those offices cannot supply the army adequately. These cases connect weapon, soldier, treasury, and household without collapsing them into one cause.

Administrative power could produce restraint in one place and intensified coercion in another. Regular pay might reduce the need for plunder, yet a centralized army could enforce extraction more effectively. A register might prevent a state from losing track of recruits while making their seizure more systematic. A fortress might shelter a town from raiders while subjecting surrounding villages to months of forced labour and supply.

Gunpowder altered tactics, siegecraft, the place of infantry, and the vulnerability of older fortifications. It did not by itself create larger armies, permanent competition, or new motives for conquest. Those developments depended on rulers who could collect revenue, recruit people, preserve records, train specialists, maintain arsenals, and impose demands across distance. Where those capacities were weak, firearms remained expensive tools in fragile hands. Where they were strong, the same tools could be deployed repeatedly and against larger populations.

The Janissary register and the village in Grimmelshausen’s novel mark opposite ends of the process. One begins with a person entered into a state’s military household; the other ends with households made to sustain soldiers they did not choose. Between them stands the fiscal question: who supplies the army, through what office, and with what power to compel? That answer determines whether a weapon remains an innovation on a battlefield or becomes part of a durable system of organized violence.

## Chapter 10: Revolution and the Politics of the Crowd

On the morning of 14 July 1789, Paris did not possess one settled account of what was happening at the Bastille. Camille Desmoulins had already helped turn political uncertainty into public alarm. Speaking at the Palais-Royal on 12 July, after the dismissal of Jacques Necker, the finance minister whose removal many interpreted as preparation for a royal assault on the city, Desmoulins gave political language to fears that had previously travelled without a settled conclusion. News moved through speeches and printed sheets, but also through taverns, bells, and men crossing between districts. The Estates-General had become the National Assembly in June. Troops had gathered around Paris and Versailles. The king’s intentions were being inferred from movements that few people could verify. Fear supplied urgency, but it did not supply a single objective.

The crowd first needed weapons. On 13 July, Paris voters and electors helped establish a civic militia, later called the National Guard, while guns and powder were sought wherever arms could be found. On the morning of the fourteenth, people went to the Invalides and removed thousands of muskets, though ammunition remained scarce. The Bastille contained gunpowder as well as prisoners, and its towers still represented royal power even though its military value had diminished. The Archives parlementaires preserve the sequence of deputations and negotiations that followed. Representatives of the electors went to the governor, Bernard-René de Launay, asking that the fortress’s cannon be withdrawn and that the garrison not fire on the inhabitants.

The first visitors arrived without a common command, coming from a city in which district representatives, electors, militia officers, artisans, soldiers, and political speakers occupied overlapping but unequal positions. Some wanted powder. Others wanted the removal of cannon whose muzzles appeared to threaten nearby streets. Some demanded the surrender of the fortress; others had come because people around them had come. As the crowd outside grew, the terms of the encounter shifted. A promise concerning the cannon was reported, disputed, or judged insufficient. Men crossed the ditch and entered the outer works. De Launay had to decide under pressure from the crowd, from his officers, and from the physical vulnerability of a fortress whose defences could still kill but could no longer be reconciled easily with the city’s new claim to political sovereignty.

Negotiation did not prevent violence. It gave the violence a sequence. Deputations approached; demands were carried inward; some defenders lowered or raised bridges; people entered parts of the complex; shots were fired; the crowd’s numbers and access changed. The Archives parlementaires record the later insistence that the fortress had been surrendered, while accounts gathered after the event differ over the exact moment and terms. That disagreement does not make the episode unintelligible. It shows that action depended on contested information. A man outside the Bastille did not need to know the governor’s full plan to act, but he did need some belief about whether the garrison had yielded, whether the bridge was safe, and whether the people inside intended to fire.

The defenders had cannon and fortified walls, yet the crowd had acquired muskets at the Invalides and had brought smaller arms and tools to force an entry. The physical contest altered the political one. The fortress could be defended, but firing on Parisians would identify the king’s garrison with an attack on the city at the moment when royal legitimacy was already weakening. De Launay’s command was not simply a military calculation. He had to consider relief, supplies, the king’s support, and the possibility that surrender might preserve the lives of the defenders. A fortress designed to make royal command visible became a place where command could no longer be exercised without creating a larger rebellion.

By the afternoon, the crowd’s immediate objectives had narrowed around powder, cannon, and control of the Bastille, but the meaning of success had widened. The fortress’s fall demonstrated that an armed population could force a royal garrison to yield, giving the fortress a significance beyond that of a storehouse or a prison. Prisoners were released, though only seven were found inside, a fact that made the event’s symbolic force larger than its custodial purpose. The governor and several defenders were taken into custody. De Launay was killed while being escorted away, and his head was carried through the streets on a pike. Jacques de Flesselles, the prévôt des marchands, was also killed after accusations that he had deceived or obstructed the crowd. These deaths did not follow a general plan issued by one revolutionary authority. Custody, suspicion, and public judgement collapsed into one another as the prisoners passed from official hands into those of the crowd.

Desmoulins had helped make the language of resistance available before the assault, but he did not command every act at the Bastille. People carried information, weapons, and demands from one site to another. Electors negotiated. Militia formations supplied some structure. Unofficial leaders interpreted events. The crowd could pressure officials without possessing a stable chain of command, and its action was neither a random eruption nor a completed revolutionary campaign. Its objectives changed as each decision altered the next available choice.

The fall of the Bastille became evidence that sovereignty had shifted in practice before its legal form was settled. Parisian crowds had entered a contest that had begun among deputies, ministers, and the crown, and their intervention changed what those actors could do. The king could no longer treat the National Assembly’s challenge as a dispute confined to Versailles. Yet the crowd had not simply caused the collapse by expressing anger. It had located powder, armed itself, selected a target, sent deputations, tested the defences, interpreted partial concessions, and continued when negotiation failed to secure what it wanted. Fear opened the encounter. The decisions made around weapons and surrender carried it to its result.

For much of the day, people bargained rather than firing indiscriminately at the garrison. Some defenders negotiated; some representatives tried to secure a surrender; the fortress was not immediately reduced to a scene of unbounded killing. Restraint was fragile and uneven, and it did not protect De Launay or Flesselles once the crowd’s judgement turned against them. Still, the record shows that a crowd could make violence possible without making every form of violence equally likely. Its conduct depended on who could speak, which demands appeared attainable, what weapons were available, and whether an official concession could still be believed.

Two years later, Paris faced a different prison crisis. On 2 September 1792, news of the fall of Verdun and the advance of the Prussian army intensified fear that enemies outside the city would be joined by enemies within it. The September Massacres lasted several days and spread through a number of prisons, including the Abbaye, La Force, the Carmes, Bicêtre, and the Salpêtrière. Prison registers and municipal records place the killings inside an administrative landscape. Prisoners had been arrested, transferred, classified, guarded, and held under revolutionary authority before crowds entered their custody. Political prisoners, refractory priests, common criminals, and people awaiting trial occupied different legal categories, though rumour and emergency increasingly compressed those distinctions.

Richard Cobb and Colin Jones, in *The People’s Armies*, emphasise the relationship between revolutionary mobilisation and the breakdown of confidence in official protection. Paris had alternatives to mass killing. Prisoners could have remained under guard or been transferred away from the threatened frontier. Municipal officials could have reinforced the prisons or publicly guaranteed that detention was not a sentence of death. These options were difficult, since Paris lacked secure communications, the government feared treason, and armed men believed that a hostile army might soon enter the city. Difficulty did not erase choice. The prisons, the guards, the municipal authorities, and the ministries formed an institutional chain through which intervention could have occurred, but no intervention secured the prisoners.

At the Abbaye, improvised tribunals separated some prisoners from others and pronounced judgements before executions occurred. This procedure did not make the killings lawful, but it gave collective violence a thin administrative form. Prisoners were called and questioned; some were released, while others were sent to be killed. The procedure could restrain action at one moment and authorise it at another. It transformed fear into decisions about categories: priest or layman, soldier or conspirator, ordinary criminal or political enemy. Rumour supplied the connecting tissue. Reports of a prison plot, of prisoners ready to attack the city, and of treasonous cooperation with invading forces made people outside the prisons treat custody as evidence of guilt.

Georges Danton, then minister of justice, became associated with the massacre because his office stood near the crisis and because later observers sought a central author for events that had no single command. His phrase about audacity, audacity, and still more audacity belonged to the emergency atmosphere of September, though it did not amount to a documented order for prison murder. Municipal and ministerial authorities possessed means to intervene, and their failure to stop the killings allowed fear to acquire political force. Silence, delay, ambiguous warnings, and the use of armed volunteers did not equal a written plan, yet they narrowed the range of action available to the crowd.

The killings sit between spontaneous violence and organised campaign. The alarm spread rapidly, but the prisoners were held in known places, reached in sequence, and judged through repeated procedures. Some participants acted from fear of invasion; others from hostility towards priests, aristocrats, or criminals; still others from the conviction that the Revolution required enemies to be removed before they could strike. Their motives cannot be reduced to one emotion. The prison system supplied the targets, municipal weakness supplied the opportunity, and revolutionary language made preventive killing appear to some participants as a defence of the political community.

The Bastille crowd had confronted a visible fortress and pursued immediate material and political objectives. In September, crowds entered institutions that the revolutionary government had already marked as dangerous. In the first case, negotiation remained a major means of action. In the second, custody made execution easier to organise. The difference does not separate popular violence from politics. It shows how institutions carried fear into a crowd and how the crowd converted official categories into death sentences.

Jean-Jacques Dessalines wrote after a revolution in which political membership had been inseparable from forced labour, race, and imperial rule. The Haitian Declaration of Independence, issued at Gonaïves on 1 January 1804, did not describe independence as a general philosophical exercise. It addressed a population emerging from slavery and war, and it named France as the power whose return would threaten the freedom just won. Dessalines’s document belongs to the moment after the defeat of the French expedition and the collapse of the colonial order, when military victory had to be turned into a claim about the future.

The declaration joins expulsion to sovereignty. Haiti would no longer accept French rule, and the formerly enslaved population would not wait for another promise of emancipation from the power that had repeatedly withdrawn or violated such promises. The political meaning of violence appears through the memory of French conduct and the insistence that separation is necessary for survival. Dessalines did not present independence as a preference among constitutional forms. He presented it as the condition under which the people could remain free. This logic arose from a sequence in which legal inclusion had repeatedly failed. The French Revolution had announced rights, but those rights were contested in the colonies. Emancipation was granted, withdrawn, and restored amid war. Formerly enslaved soldiers defended the colony while French officials attempted to preserve imperial control. The Haitian Revolution did not simply apply European universal language to a Caribbean setting. It tested whether universal claims could survive the property regime and racial hierarchy that France had maintained. Dessalines’s declaration answered the test by making national independence the safeguard against renewed enslavement. Its most severe passages assign political meaning to the expulsion of the French. The call to unite against the former masters does not treat every French person as an individual combatant in a battlefield encounter. It constructs the French colonial presence as a continuing danger, because soldiers, officials, planters, and imperial policy had been connected in the restoration of domination. That construction carried a terrible consequence: collective categories could make violence appear preventive and necessary. Independence was imagined not merely as the removal of an administration but as the destruction of a social order whose return would place freedom at risk.

The declaration’s address to the Haitian people also seeks to turn an army into a nation. Dessalines had commanded forces, negotiated with former allies, and inherited the problem of governing a devastated territory. A declaration could identify the community whose survival justified the burdens of feeding soldiers and repairing plantations. The people who had been treated as property were now named as the authors and defenders of a state. Their citizenship was not offered by a distant legislature; it was asserted through the achievement of independence.

The document is neither a simple confession of revenge nor a neutral legal instrument. It remembers injury, warns against trust in French promises, and establishes exclusion as a principle of security. Its ideology did not float above material conditions. The plantations, fortifications, ports, weapons, and exhausted armies of Saint-Domingue limited what any government could do. Yet material conditions alone could not decide whether former enslavers would be treated as citizens, enemies, or potential conspirators. The declaration provided the category through which that decision could be made.

Dessalines’s politics reveals the danger of treating revolutionary violence as the uncontrolled expression of popular rage. The document was signed by named leaders and issued through a new state, but it drew force from a population that had fought, suffered, and feared the restoration of slavery. Delegated authority and collective memory met in a political text. The declaration made independence an answer to terror, while its language could authorise new exclusions in the name of preventing terror’s return.

A strong account of the revolutionary crowd begins with spontaneity. Crowds assemble before leaders can direct them. Information often arrives as rumour rather than verified report. People may act because others are acting, with no settled plan beyond reaching a place, securing a weapon, rescuing a prisoner, or preventing an imagined attack. The July crowd in Paris improvised its response to the Bastille. The September killings began amid alarm about the frontier and the prisons, before any public timetable for executions had been established. To deny these features would replace historical participants with an invented command structure.

Spontaneity describes the origin of some action more readily than its continuation. A rumour can start movement, but it does not open a bridge, deliver a demand, classify a prisoner, maintain a guard, or issue a declaration. Those acts require decisions and some division of labour. At the Bastille, electors and deputations negotiated while armed people gathered outside. At the prisons, detainees were brought before improvised judges and separated according to judgements. In Haiti, Dessalines’s declaration turned military victory into a state claim. The crowd’s presence mattered in each case, but presence was not the same as causation.

A crowd causes an outcome when its collective action changes what officials, soldiers, and political leaders can do. The Paris crowd caused the fall of the Bastille in this stronger sense because it acquired arms, maintained pressure, entered the fortress, and made continued royal control politically and physically costly. Camille Desmoulins helped frame the crisis, but his speeches did not substitute for the movement of people through Paris. Nor did the garrison’s weakness by itself produce surrender. The outcome came from the interaction of information, weapon supply, negotiation, and the loss of confidence that the king would protect the fortress without provoking a wider revolt.

The September Massacres require a different test. The crowds participated directly, but their causation cannot be inferred from the fact that executions occurred in their presence. Officials had already concentrated suspected enemies in prisons. The municipality and ministry had authority over guards, transfers, trials, and public communication. The government could have chosen reinforcement or removal, and it did not successfully do so. Danton’s rhetoric belonged to a climate that rewarded boldness, but the evidence does not establish him as the sole director of the killings. The massacre resulted from fear, institutional failure, permissive signals, organised access to prisoners, and repeated acts of judgement.

Some participants were plainly organised. Volunteers and sections of the Paris political world possessed meeting places, officers, weapons, and channels through which decisions could circulate. Yet organisation did not make the movement unified. A revolutionary city could contain several authorities claiming to defend the same people. The National Assembly, the Commune, neighbourhood sections, clubs, militia officers, prison officials, and armed crowds did not always agree. Delegated authority operated inside that confusion. Someone spoke for a section, a deputation for electors, a judge for a makeshift tribunal, but each representative could be challenged by people who claimed a more immediate mandate from the nation.

The distinction between spontaneous action and delegated authority concerns time as well as rank. People might rush towards the Bastille without instructions, then accept or reject terms brought by negotiators. Prison visitors might enter amid panic, then adopt a procedure that required names to be called and decisions to be pronounced. Soldiers in a revolutionary army might fight from conviction, yet obey orders transmitted through officers and supply systems. The initial movement and the later conduct need not share the same degree of organisation. Treating the whole episode as spontaneous obscures the moment when improvisation becomes procedure.

An organised campaign has a further quality. It selects objectives across time, allocates tasks, preserves a command relationship, and continues after the first emotional surge. The Haitian struggle against French rule contained campaigns, armies, supplies, negotiations, and political declarations. Dessalines’s document did not merely record a crowd’s anger; it stated a programme of independence and identified the conditions under which the new polity could survive. The September killings did not possess the same coherent structure. They used prisons and revolutionary categories, but their targets varied, their procedures were improvised, and their connection to a single plan remains uncertain.

The strongest version of the spontaneity argument would say that revolutionary violence is fundamentally uncontrolled: fear spreads, crowds gather, and institutions merely follow events. This interpretation explains why leaders often appear hesitant, why rumour outruns official communication, and why individual acts cannot be predicted from ideological programmes. It also preserves the historical reality of participants who were not members of clubs, armies, or governments. A woman at the Salpêtrière, an artisan near the Bastille, or a provincial militiaman during the Great Fear did not need to read a political treatise before acting. Yet uncertainty is not the absence of structure. Prisoners were not selected at random from the whole population; they were already in custody. The Bastille was not chosen because a crowd happened to find an object nearby. It was connected to powder, cannon, and royal authority. Haiti’s independence was not the accidental result of violence continuing until no French officials remained. The revolutionary government named a people, a territory, an enemy, and a future. Institutions did not merely absorb crowd energy. They sorted it, transmitted it, and sometimes gave it durable form.

Fear itself changed when attached to an institution. A rumour about an aristocratic plot could circulate through villages, but a list of prisoners provided names and locations. A report of royal troops near Paris could alarm many districts, but the Invalides supplied muskets and the Bastille supplied powder. The emotional force remained important: fear determined what evidence appeared credible and what risks seemed acceptable. It reached an outcome through offices, roads, weapons, guards, and decisions. The route from alarm to violence was built through those arrangements.

Legitimacy worked in the same way. The crowd at the Bastille did not simply reject authority; it claimed to act for Paris and the nation against a royal power that no longer seemed entitled to command. The September killers treated themselves, however falsely, as defenders of the Revolution against internal betrayal. Dessalines presented independence as the only legitimate protection for people whom France had enslaved. Political violence became organised when a group could name its target as an enemy of a recognised community and persuade enough participants that ordinary restraints no longer applied.

Restraint survived when another legitimacy remained credible. At the Bastille, negotiation continued because some representatives believed surrender could secure powder and prevent slaughter, while the garrison still had reason to bargain. During the September crisis, restraint weakened because officials could not persuade Parisians that prisoners would be contained or that the frontier would hold. In Haiti, the memory of restored slavery made French promises appear dangerous rather than reassuring. The same population could negotiate in one moment and kill in another because the expected consequences of restraint had changed.

As armies and governments became stronger, the crowd became one participant among others. The Bastille episode shows a crowd forcing a political opening. The September killings show officials failing to use the institutions at their disposal and thereby allowing popular violence to enter prison administration. Dessalines shows a revolutionary leader converting military struggle into a founding act that defined belonging through exclusion. Across these cases, participation is visible in streets, prisons, and armies; causation lies in the decisions that connect participation to outcomes.

A remaining question concerns the point at which fear becomes an ideology rather than an emergency response. At the Bastille, fear of royal force and the search for powder produced a limited set of immediate objectives. By September, fear of invasion fused with categories of internal enemy and made preventive execution appear defensible to some. Dessalines’s declaration carried fear into the future, presenting the return of French power as a permanent danger that independence alone could prevent. Does ideology organise fear, or does repeated danger make an ideology seem true?

The records offer parts of an answer. The Archives parlementaires preserve petitions and proceedings; prison registers and municipal evidence show custody and administrative choice; Cobb and Jones trace the revolutionary armies and the failures surrounding them; Dessalines’s declaration gives political language to a society emerging from slavery. These sources let us follow decisions more closely than motives. They show where a crowd gathered, what officials controlled, which alternatives existed, and how violence acquired a name. They leave an uncertainty that cannot be removed by adding more examples. A negotiator may restrain a crowd because he believes compromise is possible, or because he fears losing control if he refuses. A prison official may delay intervention from sympathy, confusion, or calculation. A leader may invoke national survival to protect a population or to close the political space in which rivals could speak. Fear, legitimacy, and ideology do not operate as separate causes waiting to be measured. They alter one another through decisions made under pressure.

When a crowd approaches a fortress, who can still offer a concession that will be believed? When prisoners are named as enemies, who can move them beyond the reach of rumour? When independence is declared, what forms of exclusion will be treated as security rather than revenge? The answers depend on the institutions available, the alternatives officials choose, and the authority participants recognise. At the Bastille, restraint survived while negotiation retained value; in the prisons, it weakened when custody no longer promised protection; in Haiti, independence became the safeguard against a feared return to slavery. Participation alone did not produce these outcomes. Decisions, institutions, and available alternatives carried crowds from fear to organised violence—or left room for them to stop.

## Chapter 11: Colonial Rule and Resistance

In the Congo Free State, rubber extraction rested on a legal fiction: private force appeared as public administration. Leopold II claimed the territory as his personal state after the Berlin Conference of 1884–1885. Decrees issued in the 1890s declared large areas of the Congo domain land. Within those zones, local people had to provide products gathered from the forest, while concession companies received rights to exploit rubber, ivory, and other resources. The Congo Free State decree on rubber assigned obligations to villages and gave officials the means to compel them, defining the arrangement through obligations and coercion rather than a bargain between a company and a worker. The Force Publique, formally a state army, supplied the armed reach. Leopold’s office received reports, and concession companies expected deliveries. The legal claim, the commercial demand, and the military arm belonged to different offices, but they worked as one extraction system.

Distance made the system workable. A European administrator might sit at a post beside a river, a company agent might calculate the quantity of rubber arriving each month, and a Force Publique officer might send African soldiers into villages to enforce the demand. The people who collected the latex had to travel through forest, cut vines, smoke the rubber, and carry it back while maintaining farms and households. Rubber could be gathered only where suitable vines grew. As nearby supplies were exhausted, collectors travelled farther for smaller returns. A quota written at a post thus became a journey imposed on particular bodies. Its numerical form concealed the time, terrain, hunger, and risk required to meet it.

Failure brought armed enforcement into the household. Women, children, and village leaders were detained in some districts until men returned with rubber. The practice made a family absorb the cost of a concession’s demand. A man who stayed to cultivate food could lose a relative; a village that resisted could face a military expedition. The hostage was not merely someone punished after production had failed. Her detention belonged to production itself, because it converted an uncertain obligation into pressure that reached beyond the collector. The Force Publique made the concession’s claim credible even where no European agent could supervise the forest directly.

African intermediaries carried that pressure downward. The Force Publique recruited soldiers from different parts of Central Africa, often placing them under European officers and sending them far from their own communities. They carried firearms, guarded posts, and conducted punitive expeditions. Their service could bring wages, food, status, or protection in a colonial order that had displaced older forms of political security. It also exposed them to discipline from their officers and to demands for results. Violence moved downward while responsibility was recorded upward. An African noncommissioned officer and a European commander did not occupy the same place in the chain, but each had a reason to transmit coercion rather than absorb the cost of failing to use it.

The incentives of reporting sharpened the chain. A concession company measured success through rubber delivered. An official needed evidence that his post was productive. Leopold’s administration wanted reports that demonstrated effective sovereignty to foreign observers. A report could turn a raid into a punishment for noncompliance, a missing delivery into evidence of local hostility, and an increase in rubber into proof of administrative success. The paper helped determine whether violence would be rewarded, beyond merely describing it. E. D. Morel’s *King Leopold’s Rule in Africa* drew attention to the relationship between records of extraction and abuses concealed within them. Later testimony and investigations showed how the official language of order could coexist with hostage-taking, killings, and mutilation.

The severed hand belonged to this accounting world. Force Publique soldiers were required to account for cartridges, and human hands were sometimes presented as proof that ammunition had been used against people rather than wasted. The practice did not arise from one written instruction governing every operation. It appeared within a system in which officers demanded results, soldiers faced pressure to justify ammunition, and punitive expeditions could claim success through the bodies of villagers. A hand could be counted where a person had been killed, while the report transformed the circumstance into an administrative figure. The record linked battlefield evidence to bureaucratic verification.

Villagers nevertheless altered the system’s reach. They fled into less accessible areas, withheld labour, concealed rubber, negotiated through chiefs, and moved between concession zones. Some local leaders collaborated because the new authority could be used against rivals or because submission protected their communities from immediate destruction. Others complied selectively, sending rubber when armed patrols were near and reducing production when the patrols withdrew. A concession that received less rubber could answer with a patrol, but a patrol consumed supplies and exposed the administration’s dependence on local knowledge, carriers, interpreters, and soldiers.

The Congo decree joined territorial law, commercial privilege, military force, hostage-taking, and reports that rewarded extraction. The concessionaire did not need to own every forest or supervise every collector. The state supplied the claim, the company supplied the target, the Force Publique supplied the threat, and the report supplied a measure by which superiors judged the result. People at the bottom experienced an armed intrusion; those above could describe the same event as revenue, order, or evidence of sovereignty.

Lothar von Trotha’s order of October 1904 used a different language. It addressed the Herero as a population to be expelled from German South-West Africa, presenting destruction as a policy directed toward that end. After the Herero uprising began in January 1904, German forces fought a war in which land, cattle, labour, and political control were inseparable. At Waterberg in August, German troops under von Trotha surrounded Herero forces and families. The order that followed stated, in substance, that every Herero found within the German frontier, armed or unarmed, with or without cattle, was to be shot; women and children were to be driven away or fired upon. Von Trotha’s words made belonging itself the enemy.

The document’s force lies in the categories it refused to separate. It treated a combatant and a household member who had followed the retreat alike, while surrender offered no route back into German jurisdiction. The Herero presence within a claimed frontier was treated as intolerable, and German troops became the instrument by which that frontier would be cleared. The army was being used to make the territory available by removing the people who lived and grazed there, not simply to defeat a force in the field.

Military movement gave the order its practical setting. Herero forces and civilians were driven toward the Omaheke, the arid region east of Waterberg, where access to water was limited. German units guarded routes and water sources, while patrols prevented return toward areas under German control. Von Trotha’s headquarters treated the desert as part of the operation. The order’s language of expulsion acquired meaning through the direction of movement: away from German settlements and water, toward a region in which survival became increasingly difficult. The destruction did not require every death to occur in a firing line. Military concentration and control of water shaped the campaign’s geography.

Settlers’ demands for land and cattle also gave the order its purpose. Herero resistance had emerged amid German seizure of territory, debt relations, settler expansion, and attacks on political authority. German victory would open land to German settlement and weaken Herero leaders’ ability to reclaim herds or negotiate from strength, transforming the administrative order instead of restoring an earlier one. Isabel Hull’s analysis in *Absolute Destruction* places von Trotha’s order within a military culture that accepted annihilating measures when an opponent was defined as outside the protections of ordinary war. The language was extreme, but its effects depended on the prior transformation of land into a prize and of an African population into an obstacle.

The order was later modified and contradicted in practice. German authorities closed some concentration sites, and surviving Herero and Nama were taken into camps and subjected to forced labour. This shifted control from expulsion and shooting to confinement, registration, labour, and the appropriation of land, leaving their political position unrecovered. German records from 1904–1908 show military destruction and colonial administration operating as a single campaign. The rifle cleared territory, the camp held survivors, and the labour register converted them into a controlled workforce.

The text makes intention unusually plain, but the text alone did not kill anyone. Troops, routes, water policy, and later camp administration made its terms real. Von Trotha’s order was issued by a commander operating within German conquest, settler land hunger, and a chain of military orders. Its consequences cannot be reduced to spontaneous hatred between two peoples, nor can they be understood without the institutions that governed those who survived.

In German East Africa, coercion began from another demand. The colonial government needed revenue, labour, and agricultural production across a territory where German officials were few and local political conditions varied. The hut tax, imposed in different forms during the 1890s and early twentieth century, required households to obtain cash or goods through work connected to the colonial economy. Cotton schemes added compulsory labour and supervision. A tax could be collected at a post, but the money to pay it had to come from wages, crop sales, porterage, or the sale of food and livestock. The demand entered villages through roads, plantations, district officers, and African agents.

In the Rufiji and Kilwa districts, cotton cultivation became a direct point of conflict. German officials and planters wanted African producers to grow cotton for export, and local people had to work fields under conditions they did not control. The crop competed with food production and tied village labour to commercial expectations set at the coast. A man who refused could face a fine or beating; a village that did not produce could be marked as disobedient. The policy was not identical everywhere, but its pressure was clear: the colonial state converted land and time into taxable resources.

The rising known as Maji Maji began in 1905. Its first centres lay in the south, particularly around the coastal and inland districts where tax collection and cotton labour had sharpened resentment. Kinjikitile Ngwale, a spirit medium at Ngarambe, became associated with a message that water treated with sacred medicine would protect fighters from German bullets. The name Maji Maji came from this water. German reports often rendered the movement as superstition or fanaticism, but the belief performed political work. It offered people from different communities a language through which they could reject the separate demands imposed by district officials, headmen, planters, and missionaries.

The appeal did not erase local differences. Ngoni, Matumbi, Bena, Pogoro, Zaramo, and other communities entered the rising at different times and for different reasons. Some leaders joined because German officials had undermined their authority or because labour demands threatened their households. Others weighed the danger of reprisals against the possibility that a broad alliance could expel the colonial presence. The movement crossed older political boundaries without becoming a single centralised army. Local groups attacked posts and plantations, burned cotton, and refused work. Their actions targeted the points through which tax and labour entered daily life.

German officials initially underestimated the rising’s breadth. A policy dependent on scattered posts and African auxiliaries became vulnerable when local knowledge shifted to the rebels. Messengers carried warnings, villages denied food, and fighters disappeared into wooded country. Belief in protective water helped create confidence in collective action, but it also encouraged attacks on armed positions that rifles made dangerous. The movement drew strength from alliances and suffered from the same decentralisation. Communities could coordinate an assault without agreeing on the political order that would follow a German withdrawal.

By late 1905, German forces began assembling a larger response. Reinforcements, police, soldiers, and African auxiliaries moved from the coast and major stations toward the southern districts. German reports described expeditions against rebel settlements, while John Iliffe’s *A Modern History of Tanganyika* traces the campaign’s expansion from defending posts to destroying the food base on which the rebellion depended. Villages were burned, crops destroyed, granaries emptied, and people driven from areas where they could plant or harvest. German commanders increasingly treated civilian support as the rebellion’s supply system. To defeat fighters, they attacked households’ ability to feed them.

The shift mattered because the German army could not capture every participant. A patrol might occupy a settlement and then depart; a district might return to resistance when it moved on. Destroying crops extended military force across time. It made the agricultural cycle part of the war. A burned field removed food not only from a fighter but from children, elders, and households that had not taken up arms. German East Africa consequently resembled the Congo in its use of indirect pressure, but the immediate target was different. The army attacked the social capacity to sustain organised refusal rather than enforcing a rubber quota.

German officials also depended on people who did not join the rising. Some African leaders supplied carriers, information, recruits, or labour. Others evaded demands, concealed food, shifted allegiance, or waited for the result. The colonial category of “the natives” concealed these choices. A headman might cooperate to preserve a village from burning while using his position to protect relatives. A porter might carry supplies because refusal was dangerous rather than because he supported German rule. A community might reject the rebels’ demands as firmly as it resisted German taxation. Collaboration and resistance could occupy the same household over different weeks.

Felix von Luschan’s observations, later used in discussions of the war and its human consequences, belong to a wider European record that registered suffering without granting equal weight to African political calculations. German medical and administrative reports recorded disease, hunger, displacement, and mortality. They establish the consequences of scorched-earth policy and the conditions in which people died. Iliffe places those consequences within the rebellion’s chronology: the expansion of resistance, the German decision to deny food, and the years during which famine spread after organised fighting had declined. Officials often wrote to defend policy or explain costs, but their details reveal how military action reached fields, grain stores, and movement between villages.

Famine did not arrive as an accidental shadow after the fighting. Crops had been destroyed or left unplanted. People fled into forests or more distant areas, abandoning fields and tools. Cattle and other food sources were seized or lost. Disease moved through displaced populations, while the campaign restricted return and made ordinary exchange dangerous. The war lasted from 1905 into 1907, but hunger continued after German forces reported military success. A rebellion could be declared defeated while the population remained subject to its consequences.

The medicine associated with Maji Maji helped create an alliance under conditions in which communities faced a well-armed state, although it also encouraged frontal attacks against rifles; participants therefore acted within a mobilizing belief rather than as passive victims of deception. German victory cannot be explained by the medicine’s failure alone. German forces possessed firearms, trained units, transport, reinforcements, and the authority to burn the food supply. They also learned from the rebellion. The colonial state adapted its policing, recruitment, and administration after the war, while local people adapted through avoidance, migration, selective compliance, and later political organisation.

The rebellion’s outcome varied by actor. Some fighters sought to remove German rule. Some local authorities tried to preserve their standing. Some communities joined because cotton labour and taxation had become intolerable; others stayed out because reprisals threatened survival. Merchants, cultivators, porters, spirit mediums, chiefs, and soldiers encountered the colonial state from different positions. Their actions could reinforce one another briefly and diverge later. Maji Maji names a large movement, not a single intention shared by the colonised population.

The Congo’s concessionary order gave private companies a strong interest in increasing rubber deliveries while the territorial state supplied legal claims and armed enforcement. Hostages and patrols made production possible in forests that European agents could not control directly. Reports travelled upward as measures of success, and profit depended on a commodity gathered under compulsion. Violence was dispersed among agents who could each describe their task as enforcement, accounting, or administration.

German South-West Africa rested on a more openly territorial project. Land and cattle were central to settler expansion, and the Herero and Nama were not simply required to deliver a crop to a concession. Their continued presence threatened the colonial distribution of land and political authority. Von Trotha’s order therefore sought removal rather than regularised production. Military concentration, closed routes, and control of water turned the landscape into a weapon. Later camps and labour controls retained survivors within a regime that had already made the destruction of a population a condition of settlement.

German East Africa stood between these forms without becoming either one. Taxation and cotton labour required the population to remain available for work and production. The German state needed cultivators, carriers, intermediaries, and consumers; it could not simply empty the territory of its inhabitants. When Maji Maji resistance threatened tax collection and agricultural schemes, however, the army attacked the food systems sustaining fighters and noncombatants. The campaign became a punitive war against the conditions of social reproduction. Destruction was used to restore labour and revenue, even when it damaged the population from which both were expected.

The difference appears in the place of the hostage, the water source, and the field. In the Congo, a detained woman or child pressed a rubber collector to meet a quota. In German South-West Africa, water routes and military cordons drove a population from land desired by settlers. In German East Africa, the field and granary became targets because the army wanted to break an alliance that could not be defeated by attacking fighters alone. Each object joined a local setting to an administrative aim. The institution gave it a coercive use.

The cases also show different relationships between collaboration and resistance. Congo concession companies depended on African soldiers, porters, chiefs, and intermediaries, many of whom acted under constraint but could still bargain, evade, or redirect demands. German South-West African commanders worked with African auxiliaries and later confined survivors, yet the central policy treated major Herero and Nama populations as obstacles to be removed rather than partners in a labour regime. German East African officials relied heavily on local authorities to collect tax and organise work, and Maji Maji revealed how quickly those links could fail when intermediaries were identified with an unpopular system. African participation did not make colonial rule locally owned.

Numbers and reports carried different political effects in each case. A rubber delivery allowed a company to claim productivity. A military report from the Omaheke could present movement and enemy destruction as operational success. A German East African district report could list taxes, cotton acreage, burned villages, or rebel deaths. Such documents were instruments within violence, telling superiors whether a subordinate had enforced policy, whether a district required reinforcement, and whether a method should be repeated. The record of coercion also distributed praise, blame, and resources.

Local actors exploited gaps between those levels. In the Congo, flight and concealment raised the cost of collection. In German South-West Africa, Herero and Nama resistance forced Germany to commit substantial military resources, even though the campaign brought catastrophic loss. In East Africa, the breadth of Maji Maji delayed colonial production and compelled a response whose famine exposed the consequences of German policy. None of these actions produced an equal contest. They changed the duration, geography, expense, and aftermath of colonial violence. A system could dominate without controlling every choice made within it.

“Colonial violence” is consequently too broad when it erases the institutions that gave violence its direction. The Congo’s concessionary order made a commercial target the centre of coercion. Von Trotha’s campaign made territorial expulsion and racialised military destruction central. Maji Maji grew from taxation and forced labour but became a war over political alliance and the survival of food-producing communities. All three regimes used law, armed force, and administrative reporting, but they used them for different ends.

Colonial aggression became routine when an institution converted a distant objective into a repeated demand at a village, field, road, or post. In the Congo that conversion ran through concession rights and quotas. In German South-West Africa it ran through military orders, land seizure, control of water, and confinement. In German East Africa it ran through taxation, cotton labour, local intermediaries, and the destruction of food during war. The people who encountered these demands collaborated, evaded, negotiated, fought, migrated, and adapted according to the danger before them and the protection they hoped to preserve, responding as communities with differing circumstances rather than as a single population.

A report could call the result order. A company ledger could call it output. A military commander could call it victory. The village that lost its labourers, its field, or its route to water experienced the connection between those descriptions. Institutions built that connection, but the decisions of people placed beneath them continually altered its reach and its consequences.

## Chapter 12: Industrial War and the Civilian Population

> “The British have entered Péronne; the Canadians have captured Vimy Ridge. But the full extent of German frightfulness has never been so clearly displayed as in their retreat. Here, for once, the German account of their own doings is true.”
>
> — Mr. Punch's correspondent, *Mr. Punch's History of the Great War/Mr. Punch's History of the Great War*

On 1 July 1916, the British Fourth Army and French Sixth Army opened the Somme offensive against German positions in northern France. For the week before the assault, the British artillery preparation had been measured in guns, shells, positions, and hours. More than a thousand British guns fired along a front running from near Gommecourt in the north to the villages above the Somme in the south, while French artillery supported the attack farther down the line. The bombardment began on 24 June. Its immediate purpose was to break the wire and trenches, reach the dugouts, and weaken the German field army’s ability to resist. Its reach was already wider than the front. Ammunition columns moved along roads toward the batteries; horses, ambulances, engineers, signal units, and battalions converged on assembly areas. The army approaching the battle was not a mass of men alone but a system for feeding fire into a timetable.

The *Official History of the Great War: Military Operations, France and Belgium, 1916* records that preparation in tables. Guns appear by calibre and position, ammunition by round, objectives by battery, and the lifting or movement of fire by time. Each entry depended on another part of the chain. Shells had to arrive, fuses had to be supplied, guns maintained, and communication kept open between observation and command. Horses or tractors hauled the material over roads damaged by traffic. The artillery plan linked the railway siding to the ammunition dump, the observation post to the field telephone, and the infantry timetable to the moment when fire would move forward. A missing delivery or broken connection could appear at the far end as uncut wire or an exposed advance.

For a week the guns fired, and British commanders expected their weight to destroy the German defensive system. German dugouts, often deep beneath the chalk, preserved men and machine guns from much of the bombardment. Wire remained in places where British reports had assumed it would be cut. When the assault began, infantry crossed open ground in formations governed by the timetable, carrying rifles, ammunition, water, tools, and equipment for a battle expected to move forward. The plan required the artillery to form a moving shield: after the fire lifted, infantry would occupy the German front line; after the infantry advanced, guns would be brought forward and directed against the next position. At Beaumont-Hamel, La Boisselle, Fricourt, and elsewhere, the attack turned on whether that sequence held.

Some units reached or passed the German front line. Others met uncut wire, surviving machine guns, and defenders who had emerged from dugouts after the bombardment had moved onward. The *Official History* follows the progress by battalion, trench, and hour, measuring gains in yards and losses against the units that made them. A company could disappear from the account as an effective formation while the larger operation continued. Later, replacement drafts appeared in returns from depots and reserve battalions. Administrative language made continuity possible even when the men who had entered the attack had not survived.

The opening day produced about 57,000 British casualties, including roughly 19,000 dead, the heaviest one-day loss in the history of the British Army. German and French losses were also severe, though exact totals vary according to the period and method of calculation. A casualty entered the army’s records through several possible routes. Men wounded near the front passed through regimental aid posts, field ambulances, casualty clearing stations, and hospitals farther behind the line. Men listed as missing could remain unresolved for months. Prisoners appeared through another channel, while the dead were registered through burial reports or later investigation. The number on a return was thus provisional, but headquarters still had to use it.

A battalion commander who reported two hundred men missing could not immediately know how many had been killed, captured, wounded, or separated during the attack. Headquarters nevertheless had to decide whether the battalion could hold its ground, whether another unit should relieve it, and how many trained men were needed to restore it. Replacement drafts travelled through the same military geography that carried shells and food. They came from depots in Britain, from reserve battalions, and from men who had survived earlier battles, training, or sickness. Their arrival filled a number on a return without recreating the cohesion of the formation that had entered the assault. The casualty system converted uncertainty into successive administrative decisions.

Transport extended the front beyond the trenches. Railways brought men and material toward the theatre; light railways and motor transport carried them nearer the firing line; roads bore the repeated passage of vehicles until guns, weather, and traffic damaged them. The same connection appeared in reverse during the German withdrawal to the Hindenburg Line in early 1917. *Mr. Punch’s History of the Great War* printed a passage from the *Lokal-Anzeiger* describing a strip ten to thirteen kilometres wide in which villages and farms had been destroyed, roads made impassable, railway tracks and embankments removed, woods reduced to stumps, and wells blown up. The German account called the strip an “Empire of Death.” The quotation was German, its publication British, and its setting the occupied French countryside. It shows how a retreat could attack the routes and resources on which both armies and civilians depended.

The Somme villages lay among railways, roads, farms, fields, and water points that military planners read as infrastructure. A road carrying ammunition could also carry refugees. A railway bringing replacement drafts could be damaged by retreating troops. A village that had supplied labour and food could be evacuated, occupied, or destroyed. Civilian exposure therefore arose before a shell struck a house: a region became part of the war when armies treated its movement, shelter, labour, and water as conditions of military action.

Industrial production increased the supply of shells, railways extended the distance over which men and material could move, and tables allowed losses to be converted quickly into requests. None of those capacities selected the Somme as an objective. Commanders chose where guns would fire, when infantry would advance, which villages would be evacuated, and whether a retreat would destroy roads and wells. The artillery plan and the casualty return belonged to the same institutional world because both turned people and places into quantities that could be moved, replaced, or withdrawn.

The Armenian deportation followed a different route from the Somme, but its parts can also be traced through orders and returns. In 1915, during the First World War, Ottoman officials issued instructions for the relocation of Armenians from areas regarded as vulnerable to Russian advance and internal rebellion. Talat Pasha, minister of the interior, stood at the centre of this policy. Its stated security purpose was to remove a population from strategic zones, place it on routes toward the Syrian provinces, and prevent its return. Instructions moved from the central government through governors, district officials, police, gendarmes, and local notables. A population became an object of state action before any convoy reached the road.

At the local stage, Armenians were registered, summoned, arrested, or assembled. Men were often separated from women and children, and many were killed before deportation columns had travelled far. Property followed a second administrative route. Houses, workshops, livestock, fields, and shops could be inventoried or appropriated. The Ottoman government issued regulations concerning abandoned property, while local officials created committees to manage or redistribute it. The deportation order therefore moved people and possessions through related but distinct procedures: one route sent families toward the Syrian provinces, and the other transferred what they had left behind.

Routes ran through roads, railway connections, and staging points toward Aleppo and the Syrian desert. Some deportees travelled by rail for part of the journey; others continued on foot. Gendarmes were assigned to guard the columns, but protection was irregular and local attacks were common. United States diplomatic reports described killings, starvation, robbery, and exposure along the routes. Ambassador Henry Morgenthau’s reports from Constantinople conveyed information from consuls and missionaries in Erzurum, Trebizond, Harput, and Aleppo. They followed the consequences through roads, officials, convoys, camps, and the condition of people removed from their homes.

Survival depended on the route, the officials encountered, the resources a family still possessed, and the possibility of concealment or protection. Foreign missionaries, Arab communities, Kurdish individuals, and some Ottoman officials offered assistance in particular places. Some Armenians converted, changed identity, hid children, escaped from columns, or remained in cities under altered status. Each option was narrow and dangerous. A governor could delay an order; an officer could release a family; a gendarme could rob a convoy; a committee could seize a house. Central policy became mass death through the repeated local decisions that determined whether people were guarded, abandoned, attacked, or allowed to disappear from the convoy.

Telegraphy, paperwork, and railways allowed instructions to move quickly, officials to report compliance, and people and property to be sorted over great distances. The political aim came first; administrative routines supplied the route. By 1916, the combination had killed a large part of the Ottoman Armenian population and scattered survivors across the Middle East and beyond. A policy described as relocation became, through deliberate direction and repeated local violence, a route toward destruction.

Anne Frank entered the hiding place in the rear annex of Otto Frank’s business at Prinsengracht 263 in Amsterdam on 6 July 1942. The move followed the call-up of her sister Margot for what German authorities described as labour service. Anne was thirteen. She had begun her diary on 12 June, and after the family went into hiding the diary recorded the altered life of the annex: quarrels, study, fear, rationing, political news, bodily changes, and the narrow social world of eight people who could not leave the building without exposing themselves.

War governed the hours of the rear annex, whose occupants depended on helpers in the offices below and on a supply network that brought food, books, clothing, news, and necessities. Miep Gies, Bep Voskuijl, Johannes Kleiman, Victor Kugler, and Johannes Kleiman’s colleagues helped sustain them; Jan Gies and others also contributed. Anne wrote about the danger of making noise during working hours and about the need to limit movement. The hiding place survived through the labour of people who were themselves subject to occupation rules, shortages, surveillance, and punishment. Its protection was an organised human effort rather than a space outside administration.

Rationing entered the diary at the scale of the household. Potatoes, beans, bread, clothing, and soap appear as scarce things whose absence altered relations among the people upstairs. Meals depended on what helpers could obtain through official channels and the black market. The annex residents argued about portions and cooking, complained about monotonous food, and worried about health when ordinary medical care was unavailable. Anne’s account connects those domestic calculations to the larger Dutch economy. German occupation had redirected production and restricted consumption, making food access depend on ration cards, official distribution, informal exchange, and personal connections. A ration was therefore both a household measure and an administrative relation with the occupied state.

Dutch registration had preceded the hiding. Jewish residents in the Netherlands were required to register, and occupation authorities used municipal records, identity documents, population registers, and police information to identify them. Amsterdam possessed an extensive civil administration that recorded addresses, family relationships, nationality, and changes of residence. The form became dangerous when German orders and Dutch implementation turned registration into a means of selection, summons, property control, and deportation. An address that once established residence could be used to locate a person.

Anne encountered that pressure through notices and through stories brought by helpers and radio reports. Jewish people were called up, arrested, or sent away. Bicycles and other property could be confiscated, and businesses faced restrictions. The decision to enter hiding was not a response to one isolated event but to a tightening field of administrative demands. A summons could arrive at a registered address; a police visit could follow a list; a neighbour could report a concealed person. A false identity offered protection only when documents, confidence, and someone willing to maintain the deception were available.

The outside world reached the annex through sound and rumour. Air raids, gunfire, German measures, and Dutch news were discussed by people who could not see the streets. The inhabitants listened to radio broadcasts, especially the BBC, and measured military events against the hope of liberation. Allied advances in Italy in 1943, and later the invasion of Normandy on 6 June 1944, changed the atmosphere in the annex without removing its immediate danger. The war seemed to be moving toward an end, but German police still possessed the authority to arrest the hidden families.

Anne’s diary gives that pressure an individual chronology. She wrote about her mother and Peter, about becoming a writer, about adulthood, and about the future she hoped to reach. Those entries show a young person trying to form a self while the state had reduced her legal existence to a category. Her life in hiding made the machinery of persecution visible through ordinary acts: eating, studying, waiting, listening, and deciding whether a sound outside meant danger.

On 4 August 1944, German and Dutch police officers entered the building and arrested the people in hiding. The exact sequence by which the annex was betrayed remains disputed, but the arrest joined local police action to the wider system of persecution. The occupants were taken to Westerbork transit camp and then deported to Auschwitz in September. Anne and Margot Frank were later transferred to Bergen-Belsen, where they died in early 1945. Their mother, Edith, died at Auschwitz. Otto Frank survived and returned to Amsterdam, where Miep Gies gave him Anne’s papers.

The diary does not describe Dutch registration, the Amsterdam police, or the railway timetable as an official report would. It shows what those systems became inside a household. Registration became an address that could turn into a target. Rationing became a daily calculation over food and health. Police authority became a knock at the door. Deportation began with a summons, continued through transport, and ended in the loss of a name within a system that moved people through camps and trains. Anne’s entries preserve the interval in which those processes were feared but had not yet reached the annex.

Local records did not produce uniform obedience. Some Dutch officials complied; others resisted, delayed, falsified, warned, or assisted. The Amsterdam municipal administration maintained records that the occupier could use, while individual workers and citizens sometimes obstructed those uses. The helpers around the annex acted within that conflict. Their decisions did not dismantle German rule, but they kept the hidden families alive for two years.

The arrest showed the other side of the same arrangement. German political decisions defined Jews as an enemy population and ordered their removal and murder. Dutch agencies supplied registration, policing, addresses, and transport assistance at varying levels. Typewriters, telephones, railways, printing presses, radio, and filing systems increased the reach of information and people, but none of them chose Jews for deportation. The railway carried deportees because officials made deportation an organised task.

The Somme, the Armenian deportations, and Amsterdam registration connected distant decisions to local lives through different chains. At the Somme, artillery and transport sustained immense concentrations of men and material. The principal objects of administration were units, shells, roads, hospitals, and replacement drafts, while civilians encountered the system through occupation, evacuation, destruction, and the seizure of infrastructure. The offensive consumed soldiers at a rate that required the army to count, replace, and reorganise them.

The Armenian case began with a population defined as a security problem. The order travelled through governors and police into streets, houses, roads, and desert routes. Property seizure accompanied human removal, and local decisions determined whether a family was protected, robbed, dispersed, or killed. The decision to move Armenians was political, carried out through administration and violence in wartime amid fighting around the eastern frontier.

Amsterdam occupied a different position. The Netherlands was under occupation, and the people in the annex were not moved because a battle line passed through Prinsengracht. They were targeted because German racial policy classified them as an enemy population, while Dutch records and police practices helped identify and arrest them. Registration enabled the policy without being identical to deportation. The chain included definitions, orders, lists, police action, transit camps, and rail transport.

The similarity lies in the conversion of persons and places into objects of planning. Somme commanders treated French roads, railway lines, farms, and villages as elements in the movement of armies. Ottoman officials treated Armenian households, property, and routes as matters to be ordered and redistributed. German and Dutch authorities treated Jewish addresses, identity papers, and household status as information that could locate people. Once an administrative category existed, action could be repeated: a commander could request shells by calibre, an official could issue deportation instructions, and a police unit could work from a register.

The differences keep “industrial” from becoming a complete explanation. The Somme depended on factories, railways, artillery, telephones, casualty returns, and mass conscription, but its immediate purpose was the destruction or displacement of an opposing army. Armenian deportation used railways and telegraphy where available, yet its lethal force often appeared through marches, starvation, local attacks, and the deliberate abandonment of deportees. In the Netherlands, modern records and trains served a political objective of racial annihilation rather than the removal of a hostile population from a military zone.

Administrative precision did not guarantee complete control. British casualty tables could not immediately distinguish the dead from the missing. Ottoman orders could be delayed, softened, ignored, or intensified by provincial officials. Dutch registration could identify a family, but hiding, forged papers, warning, and assistance could interrupt the chain. Each system depended on people who interpreted orders, carried them out, resisted them, or exploited them. Forms and railways widened the field of action; political decisions gave that field its direction.

The civilian population became vulnerable when governments treated ordinary life as a resource or a risk. Food could sustain an army or be denied to an occupied region. Housing could shelter troops or conceal an enemy. A railway could carry shells, replacement soldiers, or deportees. A name in a register could secure a ration card, expose a family, or mark a person for arrest. Counting, classifying, routing, and inspecting were administrative habits whose consequences depended on the purpose attached to them.

At the Somme, British commanders selected objectives and accepted losses in pursuit of an offensive. Ottoman leaders ordered removal and permitted or directed a process that produced mass death. German authorities defined Jews as a population to be eliminated and required Dutch agencies to help locate and transport them. Industrial technology made these decisions more extensive, faster, and more durable. It did not make them inevitable.

A shell table, a deportation order, and a diary entry preserve different kinds of evidence. The table shows how an army counted its force and supplied its attack. Talat Pasha’s orders and the United States diplomatic reports disclose a policy moving through officials, routes, seizures, and reports of death. Anne Frank’s pages show those processes entering a household through rationing, registration, fear of police, and the fragile protection of concealment. The civilian population became a logistical object of modern war when governments made homes, roads, names, food, and movement answerable to military and political decisions.

## Chapter 13: Genocide, Total War, and Ideological Violence

> “Nor did these special units operate completely independently of the German Armed Forces. There is clear evidence that leaders of the Einsatzgruppen obtained the co-operation of Army commanders. In one case the relations between an Einsatzgruppe and the military authorities was described at the time as being "very close, almost cordial";”
>

Institutions do not turn an exclusionary ideology into extermination by declaration alone. On 20 January 1942, Reinhard Heydrich convened fifteen men at a villa beside the Großer Wannsee in Berlin. The surviving minutes of that meeting, known as the Wannsee Protocol, are neither a battlefield report nor a proclamation of extermination. They are a memorandum prepared for circulation among offices whose work had to be aligned: the Reich Security Main Office, the Foreign Office, the Interior Ministry, the Justice Ministry, the General Government, the occupied territories, the Four Year Plan, the Party Chancellery, and the SS administration. The list of attendees is part of the evidence. Wilhelm Stuckart came from the Interior Ministry, Roland Freisler from the Justice Ministry, Martin Luther from the Foreign Office, Josef Bühler from Hans Frank’s administration in occupied Poland, and Adolf Eichmann from Heydrich’s office. The murder of Europe’s Jews had entered the business of departments that registered nationality, interpreted law, managed occupied territory, negotiated with foreign governments, and arranged transport.

The protocol’s language is spare because its work is administrative. Heydrich presented figures for Jews in Germany and across Europe, including countries over which the German state exercised different degrees of control. A population was made countable across borders, then sorted according to categories created by German racial law. The minutes describe Jews who were to be evacuated east, those who might be used for labour, and those who would be treated through a process in which “natural causes” would eliminate the weaker. The document describes killing sites through a vocabulary that turns deportation into a route, labour into a temporary condition, and death into an anticipated consequence of treatment. Euphemism allowed several offices to participate without each having to write the whole intention in its own language.

Heydrich claimed authority from Hermann Göring’s 31 July 1941 authorisation to prepare a comprehensive solution to what the Nazi state called the Jewish question. Yet the meeting discussed the treatment of Jews in mixed marriages and of people classified as Mischlinge under the Nuremberg Laws, leaving practical matters unresolved. Should a person with Jewish and non-Jewish grandparents be deported, sterilised, or exempted? What should happen to a spouse in a mixed marriage, or to children from such a marriage? The protocol records proposals and anticipated decisions rather than a finished code. Stuckart pressed for legal clarification; Heydrich’s office sought a procedure that would reduce exceptions. The state had decided whom it wanted to destroy, but it still had to make legal definitions, family classifications, and territorial powers serve that purpose.

Because the SS required more than a declaration to deport millions of people, the attendees’ competences mattered, and the Foreign Office had to address Jews in allied or neutral countries. The Reich Ministry for the Occupied Eastern Territories dealt with lands where German officials, local administrations, and military commands overlapped. The General Government contained the largest Jewish population under German rule and possessed its own apparatus of ghettos, forced labour, deportation, and killing. The Interior Ministry controlled identity categories and police authority. Eichmann’s section could coordinate lists and transports, but it depended on information produced elsewhere. A census did not load a train, and a legal definition did not guard a collection point. Every stage required someone to translate one administrative form into another.

By January 1942, Jews had already been shot in large numbers behind the German advance into the Soviet Union, and killing centres at Chełmno and elsewhere were operating or being prepared. The meeting did not begin the Holocaust. It organised the relationship between an exterminatory project already under way and the ministries whose cooperation would make it continental. Wannsee fixed a moment when policy, implementation, and jurisdiction were being brought into alignment, even while some procedures remained unsettled.

The judgment of the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg later stated that “the plan for exterminating the Jews was developed shortly after the attack on the Soviet Union.” The judgment linked Hitler’s earlier threats, Eichmann’s section in the Gestapo, and the Einsatzgruppen’s operations in the East. Its account was retrospective and judicial, formed after the regime’s defeat, but it directs attention away from a single meeting as the origin of the crime. Wannsee was a coordination point within an expanding process. The decision to kill, the decision to make killing continental, and the decisions required to carry it into towns, villages, ministries, and railway systems did not occur at one instant.

The protocol’s omissions are as revealing as its formal language. It does not specify every place of killing, every transport route, or every official who would act. Nor does it explain how the occupied Soviet territories, the General Government, Slovakia, France, or Hungary would be handled in identical terms. A policy can be clear in destination while incomplete in procedure. That incompleteness created room for initiative, delay, bargaining, and escalation. Heydrich’s office could press a governor; a governor could claim a local difficulty; a ministry could demand a legal adjustment; an army command could facilitate or obstruct access to a district. The protocol shows a state moving towards uniform destruction through offices that did not possess identical powers. The same movement appears in the reports produced by the Einsatzgruppen between 1941 and 1943. Their figures do not offer a single count of an event called the Holocaust. They record operations of mobile killing units attached to the Security Police and the SD as German forces invaded and occupied territory in the Soviet Union. The reports identify places, dates, unit designations, numbers of people killed, and changing categories of victims. They allow the historian to follow violence geographically and organisationally while showing how murder became a recurring form of official reporting.

The first reports followed the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941. Einsatzgruppe A moved through the Baltic region with Army Group North; Einsatzgruppe B operated farther south and east behind Army Group Centre; Einsatzgruppe C followed Army Group South into Ukraine; Einsatzgruppe D operated in the southern zone, including parts of Ukraine and the Crimea. Each group contained smaller Einsatzkommandos or Sonderkommandos able to operate in several localities. The units relied on German police and SS personnel, military cooperation, local police, municipal officials, translators, and sometimes auxiliaries recruited in the occupied territories. Their map of killing was also a map of invasion, occupation, roads, railways, intelligence, and local access.

At Ponary, outside Vilnius, German security forces and Lithuanian auxiliaries killed Jews and other prisoners in pits near the city. At Babyn Yar in Kyiv, a German police and security operation assisted by local personnel killed more than 33,000 Jews on 29 and 30 September 1941, according to the report later used in the Nuremberg proceedings. At Rumbula near Riga, German and local forces killed tens of thousands of Jews in November and December 1941. Each place required orders, identification, concentration, transport or marching, guarding, a killing site, and a record transmitted through a chain of command.

The reports changed their categories as the war changed. At first, they described the killing of Jewish men, especially those presented as political officials, Communist activists, or suspected partisans. Women and children were increasingly included during the summer and autumn of 1941. The category of the enemy widened from armed resistance to Jews as a population. “Jewish Bolshevism” joined racial and political enemies, but the operations soon made age, sex, and military activity less important than Jewish identity itself. A report could list executions of Communists, saboteurs, partisans, and Jews in adjacent lines, yet the numbers of Jewish women and children killed demonstrate that the operation was not simply counter-insurgency.

The Nuremberg judgment reported that “Einsatzgruppen operating in the occupied Baltic States killed over 135,000 Jews in three months.” It also recorded that Heydrich could report Estonia had been “cleared of Jews” and that Riga’s Jewish population had been reduced from 29,500 to 2,500. These statements show how local killing entered a central administrative vocabulary. A town became a place measured by the number of Jews remaining.

The reports must nevertheless be read as operational documents, not neutral ledgers. Their totals served commanders, superiors, and the wider security apparatus. Numbers could display diligence, claim success, or justify requests for men and supplies. They also varied in precision and category. Some totals included prisoners killed during anti-partisan operations; others separated Jews, Communists, and local officials. A number is evidence of how an office counted as well as evidence of people killed. The distinction is visible in the report itself.

The units operated through cooperation with the German Armed Forces. The Nuremberg judgment found “clear evidence that leaders of the Einsatzgruppen obtained the co-operation of Army commanders.” In one case, relations between an Einsatzgruppe and the military authorities were “very close, almost cordial”; in another, the smooth operation of an Einsatzkommando was attributed to the Army’s “understanding for this procedure.” The mobile units depended on the Army for movement, intelligence, cordons, prisoners, supplies, and access to occupied places. Cooperation made extermination possible through the relationship between military occupation and police killing, while the Army’s role differed by command and locality and individual soldiers did not all become murderers.

On 13 May 1941, Wilhelm Keitel signed an order permitting civilians suspected of offences against German troops to be shot without trial and stating that German soldiers need not be prosecuted for offences against civilians. On 16 September, after attacks on German soldiers, he ordered the execution of fifty to one hundred Communists for each German soldier killed. The Nuremberg judgment preserved his annotation to a memorandum about holding relatives responsible for sabotage: “Yes, that is the best.” These orders altered the range of actions that commanders could treat as authorised, defensible, or useful, without mechanically causing every later shooting. They removed restraints that might otherwise have interrupted the killing.

There were still choices.

Police commanders chose whether to assemble a village, whom to classify as Jewish, whether to recruit local auxiliaries, and how to report an action. Army officers chose whether to share vehicles, troops, and intelligence. Local officials chose whether to provide lists, warn residents, hide records, or delay compliance. Pressure narrowed the field, but it did not abolish it. Christopher Browning’s account of Reserve Police Battalion 101, though based on a different formation from the Einsatzgruppen, makes the same institutional fact visible: men could be assigned to killing, could sometimes step aside, and could continue participating through conformity, ambition, fear, or acceptance of the racial order. The machinery had human operators.

At Nyamata in Rwanda, the chain looked different and moved through a denser local world. In April 1994, after the assassination of President Juvénal Habyarimana, Tutsi civilians gathered in churches and other places that had seemed to offer protection. Nyamata, in Bugesera, became one of the sites where people were concentrated and killed. The genocide lasted roughly one hundred days. Its perpetrators included soldiers, gendarmes, Interahamwe militiamen, local officials, and civilian men mobilised through meetings, roadblocks, threats, promises, and the authority of administrative superiors. The weapons were often machetes, clubs, firearms, and grenades. A local official could summon people to a meeting, a neighbour could identify a family, and a group of killers could walk to a church.

Jean Hatzfeld’s Machete Season, published in 2005, is built from interviews with imprisoned perpetrators from the Nyamata area. The men describe workdays organised around killing, movement between places, searches for people in marshes and houses, and the division of spoils after attacks. Their testimony is not a transparent transcript of 1994. It was given years later, in prison, through an interviewer who selected and shaped the accounts, and it carries the pressures of memory, self-justification, shame, and shared language. It nevertheless places ordinary participation under examination. The killers speak of orders, group expectations, fear of punishment, access to property, and the gradual normalisation of actions that had first required a political summons.

Survivor testimony supplies a different angle on the same geography. People who fled to Nyamata church, hid in marshes, or moved between communities described the collapse of distinctions between soldier and civilian, official and neighbour, public meeting and death trap. The church could be named as a refuge and then become a place of attack. The identity card, which recorded ethnic classification, could expose a Tutsi at a roadblock. A local official could order people to assemble while the men who killed them came from nearby hills. The genocide’s reach depended on information already held in communities and on institutions that made that information actionable.

Orders mattered, but so did incentives. Some participants feared being denounced as accomplices of the Rwandan Patriotic Front or as insufficiently committed to Hutu Power. Some sought cattle, household goods, land, or the possessions of people who had fled. Some acted with ideological conviction, having absorbed propaganda that described Tutsis as enemies and “cockroaches.” Others joined because neighbours were joining, because local leaders directed them, or because refusal made them vulnerable. These motives cannot be arranged on a single scale from belief to greed. A man could accept the ideology, fear the authorities, follow his companions, and take property in one afternoon.

Hatzfeld’s interviewees also describe rhythm: morning assemblies, journeys to search for those in hiding, periods of killing, and returns home. The comparison with agricultural labour appears in their accounts because the genocide entered an already organised rural calendar. Its significance is analytical. Killing became a task that could be scheduled, distributed, and evaluated. A local leader could expect attendance. A group could be criticised for failing to search thoroughly. Property could be allocated after a house was emptied. Violence acquired a worklike structure without ceasing to be ideological.

The evidence preserves refusal and evasion. Some people hid Tutsi neighbours, accepted payment to let someone pass, gave warnings, or declined to participate. Others joined late, participated selectively, or stopped when immediate pressure weakened. Refusal could carry danger, and testimony from prison may exaggerate independence after the fact. Even so, non-participation is documented across Rwanda. It marks points where the political order had not entirely replaced personal and local obligations.

The church at Nyamata now contains remains and memorial evidence belonging to a later period of commemoration, while Hatzfeld’s interviews belong to a postwar encounter with imprisoned perpetrators. Neither source stands alone. Survivor testimony records danger, flight, recognition, and loss from a position shaped by immediate vulnerability. Perpetrator testimony records participation through memory, self-presentation, and the knowledge of punishment. Their differences do not cancel one another. They identify separate links in the same chain: the victim’s exposure to organised local violence and the perpetrator’s movement through orders, companions, opportunities, and fear.

Intent asks what a political leadership sought to do to a targeted population. Policy asks how that purpose was stated, authorised, or translated into rules. Implementation asks which offices, commanders, officials, and civilians carried it out. Consequence asks what happened to people on the ground, including deaths produced by actions officials described in narrower terms. Opportunity for refusal asks where participants could delay, evade, warn, disobey, or protect. These questions overlap, but an answer to one cannot substitute for an answer to another.

The Nazi leadership’s antisemitic programme, Hitler’s threats, Heydrich’s coordination, the Wannsee Protocol, and the Einsatzgruppen reports establish an intention to destroy Europe’s Jews. In Rwanda, propaganda, administrative mobilisation, militia organisation, speeches, and local orders establish an intention to destroy Tutsi civilians as a group and to eliminate Hutu opponents identified with them. Ideology is therefore important but insufficient. Antisemitism existed before 1933; anti-Tutsi discrimination had a longer history before 1994. Neither earlier ideology automatically produced extermination. Political decisions connected hostile categories to war, state crisis, fear of defeat, and a programme of action.

Policy is more concrete than intention but less immediate than a killing. It includes orders, decrees, plans, personnel assignments, legal categories, and administrative instructions. The German invasion of the Soviet Union was accompanied by orders that removed ordinary legal protections and encouraged mass retaliation. Wannsee brought ministries into the Final Solution’s administrative orbit. In Rwanda, national and local authorities used meetings, radio broadcasts, identity classifications, roadblocks, and militia structures to make Tutsi civilians available for attack. Policies can be contradictory. An order may call for deportation while a local commander kills those designated for transport. A government may urge participation while leaving local officials to decide methods. Policy is an authorised field of action, not a script recording every act in advance.

Implementation is where the chain acquires bodies and places. Einsatzgruppen commanders had to assemble personnel, obtain vehicles, secure sites, coordinate with the Army, and submit reports. German military authorities had to decide whether to assist. In Rwanda, bourgmestres and préfets, soldiers, militia leaders, and civilian groups had to identify victims, gather attackers, search houses, guard roads, and distribute property. A policy requiring thousands of participants generates practical dependencies and points where a person can reduce, redirect, or intensify violence. Local officials could announce a search instead of an execution, delay a list, or allow a family to escape. German officers could withhold transport or supply it. Such alternatives did not guarantee safety, but they reveal why implementation cannot be treated as automatic.

Consequence must remain separate because outcomes can exceed or diverge from stated policy. A commander who orders deportation may produce death through hunger, exposure, forced marches, or shooting. A government that calls for destruction of an enemy may allow local actors to kill beyond the named category. German policy towards Jews moved through ghettos, forced labour, shootings, gas chambers, and deportations, with different combinations in different territories. In Rwanda, the killing of Tutsi civilians coincided with the destruction of homes, seizure of property, sexual violence, displacement, and the killing of moderate Hutu. Consequences were produced by routes, shortages, local rivalries, weapons, and decisions made during execution. This separation resolves a problem in reading the Wannsee Protocol. If the document is treated as the complete origin of the Holocaust, the chronology becomes false: mass shootings had already taken place, and killing centres were operating. If it is treated as harmless bureaucracy because its language avoids explicit murder, its administrative function disappears. It is evidence of policy coordination during an exterminatory process. The document establishes the participation of ministries and the effort to standardise treatment; other records establish earlier decisions, local actions, and the scale of consequence.

The Einsatzgruppen reports create a related problem. Their numbers can tempt the reader to see murder as a military operation whose logic is exhausted by totals. Yet the categories shift, the units depend on other commands, and each number conceals a locality with its own officials and witnesses. The reports establish scale and administrative recognition. They do not by themselves explain why one village was surrounded, why an auxiliary joined, why an army commander assisted, or why another person refused. Those explanations require orders, correspondence, demographic records, testimony, and archaeology.

Rwandan testimony creates the opposite temptation: to explain genocide through the motives of individual killers. The interviews make greed, fear, camaraderie, and habituation visible, but they occurred after the genocide and within a prison setting. They cannot make every perpetrator’s account typical. Nor should their emphasis on local men obscure the state’s role in making killing legitimate, organising meetings, broadcasting threats, and protecting participants from punishment. The testimony is most useful beside survivor accounts, court records, local administrative documents, and the chronology of the genocide. It shows how a national project reached a particular hillside and how national purpose was altered by local action.

The comparison changes the meaning of bureaucracy. In Germany, ministries and police offices connected a racial classification to deportation across a continent. In Rwanda, local administration and political mobilisation connected ethnic identification to attacks carried out in neighbourhoods, churches, fields, and roads. One system depended heavily on railways, police files, occupied territories, and specialised killing units; the other drew on face-to-face recognition, local government, radio, militia, and forced civilian participation. Both involved institutions, but the institutions did not perform identical work. To call both bureaucratic violence is useful only if the word retains differences in scale, technology, office, and social proximity.

Ideology also operated differently. Nazi racial antisemitism presented Jews as a biological and conspiratorial threat that could not be removed through ordinary political compromise. Hutu Power propaganda portrayed Tutsis as an internal enemy and framed the civil war as an existential struggle. In both cases, the target group was made into a danger before it was made into a corpse. Yet ideology required a political opening. War, invasion, state collapse, fear of defeat, and struggles over power transformed hostile categories into instructions. Classification could precede organisation, while organisation could intensify ideology by producing new evidence of enemy danger.

At Wannsee, officials could have rejected cooperation, challenged legal categories, or refused administrative assistance; most did not. In the occupied East, military commanders could have opposed retaliatory orders or denied support to mobile killing units; some resisted particular measures, but the Army as a whole cooperated extensively. In Rwanda, officials could have protected people at churches and roadblocks, militia members could have declined to join searches, and neighbours could have hidden those marked for death. Some did. The danger of refusal was real, and its cost varied by rank, place, and moment. A history that treats every act as compelled erases agency; a history that treats every participant as equally free ignores the coercive structure that made refusal dangerous.

The evidence of refusal is thinner than the evidence of obedience. Orders, reports, and official minutes preserve what authorities wanted recorded. Survivors remember assistance as well as attack, but not every act was documented, and the dead cannot testify. Perpetrators may claim that they resisted more than they did. Courts may privilege acts that fit legal categories. The historian must distinguish a documented opportunity from a presumed one. A commander with troops and rank possessed a different choice from a farmer summoned to a roadblock. A ministry official who could decline to process a file occupied a different position from a child whose identity had been exposed.

The Genocide Convention, adopted by the United Nations in 1948, defines genocide through acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. That legal requirement asks whether perpetrators aimed at the group’s destruction. Historical explanation asks additional questions: how was the aim formed, how was it transmitted, how did local actors interpret it, and what consequences followed from actions only partly controlled? Legal proof may depend on documents, patterns, public statements, and the scale of destruction. Historical explanation must also account for ambiguity, improvisation, and institutional conflict.

The Nuremberg judgment demonstrates the value and limit of retrospective synthesis. It joined Hitler’s threats, Eichmann’s office, the Einsatzgruppen, Army cooperation, and Keitel’s orders into a coherent account of criminal policy. Its findings are indispensable, but the judgment compresses a long sequence into a narrative of responsibility suitable for a tribunal. The Wannsee Protocol, the operational reports, and local testimonies must be returned to their own times and purposes. A court asks whether responsibility has been proven; a historian asks how a decision acquired reach. The answers can support one another without being identical.

The Holocaust involved the systematic murder of approximately six million Jews across German-occupied Europe, alongside the persecution and murder of Roma and Sinti, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, Polish civilians, and others. The Rwandan genocide killed roughly 800,000 people, most of them Tutsi, in about one hundred days, with moderate Hutu also targeted. Numbers convey scale and speed without providing a moral ranking. They identify different relations between population, time, territory, and killing. The German case extended across borders through a mature state and wartime empire. The Rwandan case moved with extraordinary speed through local administration and civilian participation during state crisis and civil war.

Can the two cases be compared without making one the standard for the other? Their differences are too large for a single model, while their similarities are too important to ignore. In both, exclusionary political language identified a group as a threat; authorities used existing classifications and organisations; participants received incentives or protection; and killing required choices by people situated between central purpose and local action. The comparison becomes misleading if the Wannsee Protocol and a perpetrator’s interview are treated as equivalent documents, or if a railway timetable and a village meeting are assumed to represent the same administrative form.

The unresolved question is how far comparison can go before it turns distinct histories into a common moral scenery. Nazi extermination, carried through racial law, police power, occupation, deportation, and killing centres, cannot be reduced to the pattern of local participation visible in Nyamata. Rwanda cannot be treated as a smaller or less developed version of Germany’s genocide, because its political history, social relations, technologies, and tempo were different. Still, each case makes the other more legible. Wannsee shows how offices could make destruction portable across jurisdictions. Nyamata shows how an exterminatory policy could become intimate enough to depend on the knowledge of neighbours and the pressure of peers.

The records leave one question open. When an institution provides the category, the order, the weapon, and the protection, at what point does an individual decision cease to be a contribution and become the act itself? No single document answers it. The protocol shows a room of officials; the Einsatzgruppen reports show units and totals; Hatzfeld’s interviews show men remembering days of killing. Between them lies the history of conversion: an idea made official, an office made responsible, a population made visible, and a person deciding whether to carry the command into another person’s life.

## Chapter 14: After 1945: Deterrence, Insurgency, and Uneven Decline

On 16 October 1962, John F. Kennedy was shown photographs taken by a U-2 reconnaissance aircraft over Cuba. The images, made on 14 October, revealed Soviet construction sites near San Cristóbal and objects that American analysts identified as missile launchers. The President was not told at once that Nikita Khrushchev had placed nuclear-capable missiles on the island. He saw the photographs in the White House with a small group of advisers, and the meeting began a week in which information was divided, decisions were delayed, and the consequences of delay were weighed against the consequences of action.

The photographs did not speak by themselves. They showed roads, cleared areas, buildings, and equipment, but their significance depended on intelligence officers who compared the sites with known Soviet missile installations. By 16 October, the Central Intelligence Agency judged that medium-range and intermediate-range ballistic missiles were being installed in Cuba. A missile placed there could reach much of the United States. The Soviet Union already possessed missiles capable of reaching American territory from its own soil, but the Cuban sites altered the time available for warning and response. They also brought the nuclear rivalry close to the political and military boundary that Washington had drawn around the Caribbean after the Bay of Pigs invasion failed in April 1961.

Kennedy’s advisers entered the room amid competing policies: the ExComm transcripts record arguments for an air strike, an invasion, a naval blockade, diplomatic pressure, and waiting. General Curtis LeMay and other military officers treated the missiles as a direct challenge that required removal. An air strike might destroy many launchers, but it could not guarantee that every missile would be found before firing. It might also kill Soviet personnel, leaving their government under pressure to retaliate. An invasion promised control of the island, yet it could become a prolonged operation against Cuban and Soviet forces while American commanders remained uncertain about the location and readiness of nuclear weapons. A blockade, renamed a quarantine because the latter word carried a narrower legal meaning, would prevent additional offensive weapons from reaching Cuba while leaving the missiles already there.

The domestic pressure was real, though it did not dictate a single outcome. Kennedy had inherited the political consequences of the failed Bay of Pigs operation, and the midterm elections were approaching in November 1962. Republican senators, including Kenneth Keating, had publicly warned that offensive missiles were being installed in Cuba. If the administration appeared to tolerate a Soviet nuclear base ninety miles from Florida, critics could accuse the President of weakness. If it attacked and produced a wider war, the same political system would judge the decision by the destruction that followed. Kennedy had to act under public expectations while keeping the central facts secret. An immediate announcement could have narrowed the space for negotiation and allowed Moscow to present an American attack as unprovoked.

The transcripts show officials trying to turn uncertainty into sequence. The United States would announce the discovery and establish the quarantine. American naval forces would stop ships carrying offensive weapons, while reconnaissance flights continued. The Soviet government would be given a choice: remove the missiles or face a mounting confrontation. The plan turned time into an instrument while leaving the missiles dangerous. Each day without an air strike allowed more diplomacy, but each day also allowed Soviet technicians to complete sites and make the launchers harder to destroy.

Kennedy’s televised address on 22 October told the American public that the Soviet Union had placed offensive missile sites in Cuba and that the United States would impose a quarantine on military equipment bound for the island. He described the weapons as a threat to the Western Hemisphere and demanded their removal. While withholding every military estimate and the extent of the advisers’ divisions, the speech used controlled language to present a decision already made as American ships moved toward a line beyond which Soviet vessels might be stopped.

The signals then multiplied. The quarantine was announced publicly, but military preparations were visible to Moscow through reconnaissance and diplomatic channels. Soviet ships approached the quarantine line. Some slowed or turned back; others continued. Kennedy and Khrushchev communicated through public statements and private letters, while ambassadors and intermediaries carried messages whose authority was not always clear. On 26 October, Khrushchev sent a personal letter that presented the crisis as a Soviet effort to protect Cuba and suggested that the missiles could be removed if the United States pledged not to invade the island. On 27 October, a second message demanded more: the removal of American Jupiter missiles from Turkey.

The two letters created a question about which Soviet position governed the exit. The public message of 27 October was harder and raised the prospect of an exchange between missiles in Cuba and missiles in Turkey. The Turkish weapons were aging, but they were deployed under American control through the North Atlantic alliance, and their removal could appear to reward Soviet pressure. Kennedy’s advisers discussed answering the first letter publicly. Robert Kennedy would tell Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin privately that the Turkish missiles would be removed later, provided the concession remained secret and was not presented as a formal condition.

A U-2 aircraft was shot down over Cuba on 27 October, killing Major Rudolf Anderson. The incident intensified the danger because it could have been treated as an act of war. Kennedy did not order the immediate attack that military officers had urged in earlier discussions. American aircraft continued reconnaissance, ships maintained the quarantine, and diplomacy remained active. The United States also learned that Soviet forces in Cuba possessed tactical nuclear weapons, a fact not fully understood in Washington during the confrontation and one that made an invasion more dangerous than the President’s advisers realised at the time.

Khrushchev agreed to remove the Soviet missiles from Cuba under United Nations observation, though Fidel Castro disputed the inspection arrangement and it never operated in the form Washington first desired. The United States publicly promised not to invade Cuba. Through Dobrynin, it privately indicated that the Jupiter missiles in Turkey would be withdrawn within several months. The quarantine ended after the Soviet missiles were removed, and the Turkish missiles were dismantled in 1963. A direct telephone link between Washington and Moscow followed, along with the Limited Test Ban Treaty of 1963, though neither development erased the continuing arms race.

The crisis is often used to demonstrate the stabilising force of nuclear deterrence. That description captures something important: Kennedy and Khrushchev understood that military success could become political catastrophe, and both retained options short of immediate attack. The documents show deterrence working through more than fear of destruction. Secrecy protected room for manoeuvre. Reconnaissance reduced some uncertainty while leaving other dangers hidden. Domestic politics made withdrawal costly. Military plans narrowed the future even as civilian leaders resisted them. Personal letters allowed each leader to address the other while public speeches addressed allies and opponents. Restraint emerged from pressure, not from the disappearance of aggression.

A different postwar logic appeared in Algiers. Paul Teitgen, secretary-general of the prefecture of Algiers, was not a battlefield commander. He was a senior civil servant responsible for the machinery of French administration during the Algerian War, which began in 1954 and entered a decisive urban phase in 1956 and 1957. His position placed him near the paperwork through which emergency authority became action: arrests, detention, searches, intelligence reports, and the movement of prisoners. When French military authorities expanded their role in policing Algiers, Teitgen saw that the official vocabulary of order could conceal a practice of disappearance.

The mechanism began with legal delegation. In 1956, the French government granted special powers to civil and military authorities in Algeria. The army received broader authority to search homes, arrest suspects, hold people without ordinary judicial safeguards, and interrogate them. In January 1957, General Jacques Massu’s 10th Parachute Division entered Algiers with the task of breaking the urban campaign of the Front de Libération Nationale. The FLN had organised bombings, assassinations, strikes, and clandestine networks in the city. French commanders treated the population not simply as civilians caught between armies but as a field in which hidden support for the insurgency had to be located.

Intelligence was the hinge. A bombing created the need to identify a courier or a safe house. Arrests supplied names. Interrogations produced further names, sometimes through information, sometimes through coercion, and sometimes through answers shaped to satisfy the interrogator’s expectation. The information could be entered into files and plotted across the city. Intelligence officers mapped relationships between districts, families, cafés, transport routes, and suspected political organisers. Each arrest promised an individual result and a route into the wider network.

Torture entered this process because the search for information rewarded speed and made delay appear dangerous. French forces used electric shocks, beatings, simulated drowning, and other forms of coercion, although the practices were denied or concealed in official language. The stated purpose was extraction: a prisoner would reveal the location of a bomb, the name of an accomplice, or the time of an attack. Coercion also changed the quality of the information. A person in pain might provide accurate details, guessed details, or whatever answer seemed likely to end the interrogation. The system could not reliably distinguish among them.

The FLN’s strategy exploited the same urban density that made French intelligence possible. Its operatives used cells and false identities; women carried bombs through checkpoints, and networks connected the Casbah to European districts. Bomb attacks required concealment, timing, and enough political effect to demonstrate that French security could not protect ordinary life, even without control of the city. The French response made every neighbourhood a potential source of intelligence and every arrest a possible route into the network. Insurgent violence and counterinsurgent repression strengthened one another: attacks justified wider powers, while wider powers supplied grievances and fear on which recruitment could feed.

The Battle of Algiers was not a conventional contest between two armies. French forces controlled streets, checkpoints, prisons, aircraft, communications, and the formal government. The FLN controlled neither a regular army in the city nor the legal order, but it possessed secrecy and a political objective that military searches could not remove. The army could dismantle one cell and still fail to end the movement. It could obtain short-term intelligence by methods that damaged the authority it claimed to defend.

Teitgen’s protest was directed at the conversion of emergency into routine. In accounts later discussed by Martin Evans in *The Memory of Resistance*, Teitgen described signing resignation documents after confronting the use of torture and arbitrary detention. He had himself been tortured by the Gestapo during the Second World War. That experience gave his objection a precise political meaning: French officials were reproducing practices associated with the enemy while claiming that the exceptional danger of Algeria made them necessary. His intervention did not stop the system. It recorded the moment when a civil servant recognised that legal forms were being used to pass people into a space where violence could be inflicted without judicial accountability.

French government reports and later inquiries preserved a divided record. Official statements emphasised the FLN’s bombs, murders, and intimidation, all of which were real. They also described arrests and the restoration of order in language that concealed how information had been obtained. Counterinsurgency required secrecy because public acknowledgment of torture could weaken French legitimacy, provoke criticism in metropolitan France, and give the FLN evidence for international audiences. The practice became most useful to its operators when it remained officially deniable.

The city fell quiet after the French campaign dismantled much of the FLN’s urban organisation in 1957, but the war did not end. Rural guerrilla warfare, political mobilisation, repression, and international pressure continued until the Evian Accords and Algerian independence in 1962. Tactical success in Algiers did not settle the political question. It showed that an occupying or governing power could disrupt a clandestine network through emergency powers and intelligence-led arrests while also showing why such success could fail to produce consent. The hidden violence that made information available made French rule harder to defend.

The Cuban confrontation and the Algerian War belonged to the same postwar world, but they turned danger into different calculations. In October 1962, the United States and the Soviet Union faced the possibility that a local deployment could trigger war between nuclear powers. In Algiers, French authorities faced an insurgency within a colonial territory they claimed to be part of France. Both crises involved secrecy, uncertain information, military pressure, and leaders who feared that retreat would invite further challenge. Their different results arose from the scale and location of the political stakes and from the weapons available.

Kennedy and Khrushchev each had reasons to preserve the other’s room for retreat. A public humiliation could destabilise an ally, damage a leader at home, and make later concessions impossible. The American President therefore accepted a private arrangement on Turkey that could not be advertised as a trade. Khrushchev accepted the removal of Soviet missiles from Cuba while obtaining a non-invasion pledge and, privately, the withdrawal of American missiles. The settlement worked because both governments could claim something and neither had to acknowledge complete defeat.

French officials possessed no equivalent reason to treat the FLN as a government whose security required preservation. The insurgents were classified as rebels, terrorists, or criminals rather than as a rival sovereign whose political claims had to be incorporated into a settlement. The army’s local success could be presented as proof that more pressure would work. Emergency powers had an internal constituency: settlers in Algiers demanded protection, while military commanders and police officials punished signs of concession. The costs of repression fell largely on Algerian Muslims, who lacked equal political power within the colonial order.

The two cases also differed in the visibility of threatened harm. Nuclear war made the scale of failure imaginable even when its precise sequence was not. A bomber, missile, or invasion might kill millions and destroy the governments that ordered it. Torture in a prison cell could be kept outside public view. Its victims were individually exposed, while its wider consequences were dispersed through fear, silence, recruitment, exile, and damaged legitimacy. The absence of spectacle did not make the violence small; it allowed officials to administer it with less immediate political cost.

Deterrence worked in Cuba because each side could threaten unacceptable retaliation and because both leaders recognised that the threat might escape their control. The Soviet Union did not remove the missiles simply because Washington possessed greater force. It removed them after naval pressure, diplomatic communication, the non-invasion pledge, and the private Turkish concession produced a tolerable exit. Military strength created the danger; political bargaining prevented that danger from becoming war. The arrangement also required leaders to restrain subordinates, including commanders who might respond to an aircraft shot down or a ship challenged at sea.

French counterinsurgency pursued another kind of deterrence. Arrests, torture, curfews, collective suspicion, and public displays of force were intended to make participation in the FLN too costly. The target was not only the person who carried a bomb but the neighbourhood that might shelter him and the family that might know his name. This strategy could interrupt attacks, yet its coercion also communicated that legal protection depended on political identity. It discouraged some acts while teaching others that the colonial state regarded them as enemies regardless of individual conduct.

Cuba had a bounded geopolitical objective. Washington wanted offensive missiles removed, while the Soviet Union wanted its ally protected without a direct superpower war. Neither side sought the destruction of the other’s political system during those thirteen days. Algeria involved a sovereignty question that the French government was unwilling to treat as negotiable for much of the war. The FLN sought independence; French authorities sought to preserve the colony, though they described it as an integral part of France. Because the political end was refused, the military means expanded.

Cuban restraint was not a simple product of enlightened leadership. Kennedy had approved the Bay of Pigs invasion and authorised covert pressure against Fidel Castro. Khrushchev had secretly installed missiles in Cuba partly to protect the revolution and alter the strategic balance. The crisis became restrained only after earlier coercive policies had created the setting in which restraint was needed. Nor does Algerian repression prove that democratic governments inevitably torture under pressure. French officials such as Teitgen opposed it, journalists exposed it, lawyers challenged it, and public controversy helped weaken the claim that emergency could be unlimited. The difference lay in which voices carried power and which population could be sacrificed without equal representation.

One case placed violence near the centre of political responsibility; the other pushed it into offices, prisons, and classified reports. Kennedy and Khrushchev had to manage an international audience that included allies and adversaries capable of understanding the danger of nuclear exchange. French commanders could describe disappearances as administrative necessities and leave the victim’s family searching through police stations and detention lists. A reduction in one form of organised killing could coexist with the refinement of another form of coercion.

The United Nations reports on chemical attacks in the Iran-Iraq War show how international rules encountered this uneven field. Between 1983 and 1988, investigators sent by the United Nations examined allegations that Iraq had used chemical weapons against Iranian forces and civilians. Their reports described symptoms, contaminated areas, delivery methods, and munitions. The language was cautious but cumulative. Investigators found evidence consistent with the use of mustard gas and, in later attacks, nerve agents. The reports did not treat chemical weapons as an abstract violation; they followed the substances into bodies and places.

Mustard gas blistered skin, damaged eyes, and injured the lungs. Nerve agents disrupted the nervous system and could kill rapidly. Iranian soldiers were exposed in trenches and on open ground, while civilians suffered in towns and villages affected by attacks. The reports described bombs, artillery shells, and aircraft as delivery systems. These objects mattered because the violence depended on a chain: chemical agents had to be produced or obtained, loaded into munitions, transported, delivered, and used where people could not easily escape.

The investigators reported evidence from Iran after visits in 1984, 1985, 1986, and 1987. The Security Council condemned chemical-weapons use in a series of statements and resolutions. Yet enforcement remained weak. The United Nations could send investigators and publish findings, but it did not possess an independent force capable of stopping Iraqi attacks. Great-power politics shaped the limits of action. Iraq received support from states that opposed the Iranian revolution, while the conflict occupied a strategic position in the Persian Gulf. Condemnation established a record without reliably imposing an immediate cost.

The reports expose a measurement problem. A battle-death count might register a soldier who died from a blast, but it could treat a survivor with damaged lungs, blindness, infertility, or chronic neurological injury as merely wounded. Families caring for those survivors bore years of medical and economic consequences. Villages exposed to gas carried fear into later battles and displacement. The figures available to international investigators were already narrower than the harm produced by the attacks.

A United Nations report could identify the weapon and describe its effects, but its authority depended on states willing to enforce the rule. The Iran-Iraq War joined two postwar developments that did not move at the same speed: the definition of prohibited violence became more precise, while the capacity to punish a powerful violator remained conditional. Norms could name the act. They could not by themselves protect the next trench or village.

The central uncertainty is how much decline a battle-death series can bear. Interstate wars between major powers became less common after 1945, and nuclear weapons helped make direct confrontation dangerous. Yet wars continued in Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan, Algeria, Iran, Iraq, and many other places, often with foreign governments supplying arms, intelligence, money, or air power. Civilians were killed, displaced, imprisoned, tortured, starved, and injured in ways that did not enter the same column as soldiers killed in battle.

If fewer people died on battlefields while more violence moved through prisons, militias, sieges, forced migration, and damaged health, should the period be called less violent? The answer changes according to whether the unit is the state, the army, the household, or the person. The Cuban crisis shows that deterrence could restrain leaders facing catastrophic escalation. Algiers shows that emergency power could make coercion less visible without making it less organised. The United Nations reports show that a rule could become clearer even while enforcement remained uncertain.

The postwar decline is real in one register and unsettled in another. Interstate battle deaths fell, but coercion persisted in civil wars, colonial emergencies, proxy conflicts, prisons, and the damaged bodies left outside the battle count. The question is not whether violence disappeared. It is who was exposed to it, which institutions could conceal it, and which forms of harm the available measures were prepared to see.

The army that defeated Charles I carried more than weapons into the church at Putney. It carried a question about who had the right to give orders after the fighting ended. Thomas Fairfax stood at its head as commander in chief, while elected representatives and agitators gathered in October and November 1647 to argue over the political future that victory had opened. At Andersonville, by contrast, military power governed men who had already been defeated. The question was whether command would preserve their lives, neglect them, or allow their suffering to become part of war’s accepted cost. In South Africa, Desmond Tutu presided over an institution that inherited the consequences of organized violence without possessing the power to punish every offender. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission had to decide what kind of knowledge might stand where a complete reckoning was impossible. These cases do not form a ladder from violence to enlightenment. They show institutions choosing under different pressures. The New Model Army could coerce the country while containing arguments that challenged property-based political power. Confederate officials at Andersonville worked inside a system of imprisonment and military scarcity, yet the deaths of prisoners depended on decisions about supply, water, shelter, and responsibility. The TRC created a public procedure in which testimony and conditional amnesty could redirect some demands for revenge toward an official record, while leaving anger and every injury unresolved.

At Putney, the dispute concerned the reach of the vote. Thomas Rainborough argued that every man living under a government ought to consent to it. Henry Ireton insisted that political rights belonged to those with a “permanent fixed interest” in the kingdom, as recorded in the proceedings of the Putney Debates. The army’s coercive strength made the dispute dangerous to men who wanted the restored political order to remain narrow. Soldiers had fought, suffered, and organized; their representatives could speak with more than abstract indignation. Yet the army did not become a democratic instrument merely because its ranks contained Levellers. Fairfax and other commanders could restrain radical proposals, and military authority could impose a settlement as readily as widen participation.

The political alternatives at Putney were inseparable from the army’s organization. Its soldiers operated through regiments, officers, petitions, agents, and procedures through which arguments could be made. The Agreement of the People, associated with the Leveller movement, proposed a more representative political order, while army grandees sought a settlement that would preserve command and prevent further disorder. Fairfax’s authority could hold the army together while negotiations continued, but it did not make him identical with the soldiers’ most expansive demands. The army’s internal divisions gave the debate its unusual pressure.

Rainborough’s statement begins with residence under government: “every man that is to live under a Government, ought first by his owne consent to putt himself under that Government.” The proposition moves from exposure to coercion toward participation. A person subjected to law becomes a person entitled to consent. Ireton began elsewhere. He tied political power to a fixed stake in the kingdom, especially property, because he feared that people without estates would use political participation to alter the social order. His “permanent fixed interest” was a qualification and a boundary around the people authorized to decide.

A military organization can produce political speech because it gathers people, trains them to obey commands, and gives them a shared experience of risk. The same organization can suppress political speech because commissions and habits of obedience place decisions in the hands of officers. Putney unsettles the claim that armies necessarily create domination. The New Model Army possessed concentrated coercive power, yet that power opened a space in which ordinary soldiers and their representatives challenged the authority of property holders. It also constrained the consequences of that challenge.

The printed and manuscript records preserve a contest over consent, property, and conquest, but they do not reveal the complete motives of every soldier. They show something more precise: coercive power can be internally divided over its political use. The army enabled the argument and limited the settlement.

At Andersonville, the record moves from argument to bodies counted by the day. Camp Sumter, established near Andersonville in southwestern Georgia in 1864, was intended to hold prisoners transferred from other Confederate camps as Union advances made older sites vulnerable. The stockade expanded, but the number of prisoners continued to rise. Stockade Branch served as a water source and became contaminated by the camp’s waste. The crowded enclosure left prisoners exposed to weather, disease, hunger, and violence. Mortality rose as overcrowding, poor water, inadequate food, exposure, and the collapse of exchange reinforced one another.

The camp command changed during its existence. Captain Richard B. Winder became associated with its administration, while Henry Wirz served as commandant. Above them stood Confederate departments responsible for prisoners, supply, transport, and exchange. Their responsibilities did not carry identical weight, and the deaths cannot be assigned only to the man who signed orders at the stockade gate. Conditions emerged from decisions made at several levels: the suspension of prisoner exchange, the allocation of food and building materials, the movement of captives into the camp, and the failure to provide adequate medical care.

The prison’s most lethal period came in the summer of 1864, when thousands of prisoners were confined behind the stockade. The dead were buried outside the camp at what became Andersonville National Cemetery. The cemetery’s rows later made mortality visible as a landscape, but the deaths occurred through administrative failures repeated over time. Rations were insufficient and often nutritionally poor. Wood for shelter was scarce. The stream could not safely serve a population of that size. Prisoners built shelters from scraps, dug for water, and formed groups for protection and trade. Those responses reduced some dangers without changing the conditions that produced them.

The United States Sanitary Commission’s *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the United States Army* treated military health as a matter of administration rather than fate. Its evidence belongs beside the February 1865 report from Fort Pulaski, where C. Grover described Confederate prisoners as suffering from insufficient food and clothing and from scurvy. Grover endorsed the medical director’s recommendation that they receive full prison rations and clothing from friends, and asked for authority to relieve their sickness and suffering. He did not propose that relief wait for the war to end. An active conflict still permitted an administrative duty of care. That document exposes the limits of scarcity as an explanation. The Confederacy faced blockade, military defeat, transport disruption, and diminishing resources. Those conditions made Andersonville difficult to supply. They did not dictate the decision to concentrate prisoners where water and shelter were inadequate, nor did they make exchange impossible in every moment. Better provisioning, dispersal, improved sanitation, medical supplies, and limits on further transfers remained imaginable policies.

Robert E. Lee and Jefferson Davis, according to the account preserved in *The Immortal Six Hundred*, protested the violation of the exchange cartel and offered to transfer sick and wounded Union prisoners if the United States would provide transports. Lee’s reported appeal was direct: “Come and get all your prisoners of war, we cannot feed them, nor get medicines to keep them in health.” The proposal carried military risks. Exchanges could return soldiers to combat, and both governments feared strengthening the opponent. Disagreements over black Union troops made the exchange system especially contentious. Those risks narrowed the field of action; they did not erase every alternative within it.

The numbers show the scale of death, but they do not distribute blame among Wirz, the Confederate administration, military policy, and wartime conditions. That distribution requires orders, reports, testimony, supply records, and comparison with other camps. A commander’s authority was real, but it did not make him omnipotent. Andersonville became a case of organized neglect because suffering was made predictable, visible, and administratively tolerable.

The comparison with Putney turns on the people being governed. The soldiers at Putney were organized actors with arms, elected agents, and a claim upon the victorious state. The prisoners at Andersonville were disarmed enemies whose political value had been reduced by captivity. A system that recognized the army’s demands could neglect prisoners because their suffering carried little immediate cost for those directing the camp. Scarcity intensified the danger, but it did not choose the policy.

South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission worked within a different institutional field. After apartheid, a negotiated transition could not simply reproduce the criminal courts of a victorious power. No side possessed an uncontested mandate to punish every member of the former security state and liberation movements. The Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act of 1995 created the commission, whose hearings began in 1996. Desmond Tutu and the commissioners inherited police files, military secrets, destroyed documents, partisan narratives, and private grief. Their task was to establish a record in a country where no single authority could command agreement about the past.

The commission’s public form gave testimony a political location. Victims and survivors spoke before commissioners and audiences; interpreters carried testimony across South Africa’s languages; names, dates, places, and organizations entered the record. Tutu’s presence made the hearings recognizably religious to many observers, but the mandate was legal and historical as well as moral. The commission investigated gross violations of human rights committed between 1960 and 1994, including killing, abduction, torture, and severe ill-treatment. Those categories were broad enough to collect different forms of injury while specific enough to distinguish individual acts from general accusations.

Amnesty was the hinge of the arrangement. It was not a blanket pardon issued before evidence was heard. Applicants had to make full disclosure and show that their acts were associated with a political objective and committed in the prescribed period. The commission assessed whether an applicant had told the truth, whether the act was proportionate to the stated objective, whether the applicant had acted under the direction of a political organization, and whether the account was supported by other evidence. A person who received amnesty avoided prosecution for the disclosed act. A person who concealed material facts could not claim that protection simply because the act had occurred during the conflict.

The procedure altered the incentive attached to speech. A perpetrator had reason to describe a killing, disappearance, or torture in detail, including the names of superiors and colleagues, because silence could leave the person exposed to prosecution. That incentive did not guarantee honesty. Some applicants minimized their roles, shifted blame, or supplied accounts that conflicted with other testimony. Investigators relied on corroboration, documentary evidence, forensic work, police records, medical reports, and comparison among witnesses. Public testimony became one part of an evidentiary structure, subject to that structure’s standards.

The evidence standard distinguished the TRC from a criminal court without freeing it from proof. A court asks whether guilt has been established according to a defined legal standard. The commission pursued a broader historical account, seeking to establish responsibility and patterns when prosecution was unavailable. Its work could illuminate command structures and political objectives that a narrow trial might leave outside an indictment. A broad inquiry also risked allowing political conflict to absorb personal motives, opportunism, or ordinary criminality. The requirement of a political objective was meant to prevent that expansion, though applying it was difficult.

For victims, the procedure changed the status of injury. A family whose son had disappeared could place his name before an institution that acknowledged the disappearance as a matter of public concern. A survivor could describe detention or torture without reducing the experience to an anonymous statistic. The hearing did not restore the dead, return years taken from the imprisoned, or repair the economic damage imposed by apartheid. It could move an injury from private grief or partisan accusation into the country’s official history.

Tutu repeatedly emphasized the human cost of political violence, but the commission did not rest on his moral authority alone. Its report named perpetrators, organizations, state agencies, and patterns of action. It also recorded failures. Not every victim appeared. Not every perpetrator sought amnesty. Some evidence had been destroyed, withheld, or distorted. The commission could identify a pattern without proving every individual act within it, and it could establish that an organization had pursued a policy without showing that every member knew its full consequences. Its limits were built into its design. Conditional amnesty could feel like impunity to victims who wanted punishment. Reparations recommendations depended on later political action, and disclosure did not ensure equal economic recovery. The commission’s language of reconciliation could be heard differently by a former detainee, a widow, a policeman, and a politician. Tutu could create a setting in which these voices met, but he could not make their interests identical.

The report’s historical value lay in its combination of scale and particularity. It could describe patterns of state violence while retaining individual names. It could place an act within a national conflict while asking what happened at a specific house, road, police station, or farm. That combination resisted two opposite errors: treating violence as a series of isolated crimes with no political structure, or treating political structure as so powerful that individual decisions disappear.

Apartheid had classified South Africans through racial law, residence, movement controls, labour systems, and political exclusion. Organizations mobilized people through African nationalism, Afrikaner nationalism, socialism, Christianity, anti-colonial politics, and local loyalties. These identities shaped who could be targeted and how violence was justified. They did not determine each act. A command still had to be issued, a police unit still had to arrive, a weapon still had to be used, and a bystander still had to decide whether to assist, refuse, or remain silent.

Leadership appeared at several levels. Tutu gave the commission a public voice, but commissioners, investigators, translators, lawyers, clerks, and witnesses made the process work. Political leaders chose negotiation rather than an unrestricted victor’s tribunal. Applicants chose whether to disclose. Victims chose whether to testify. The commission’s authority emerged from these linked decisions, none of which guaranteed the final outcome. A procedure can redirect violence only when people make it consequential.

The TRC also shows why repair cannot be measured by disclosure alone. Public testimony created recognition, but recognition did not equal punishment or material recovery. A family might hear an account of a disappearance and still reject amnesty for the person responsible. A perpetrator might reveal a chain of command while avoiding prison. The commission redirected some claims from clandestine retaliation toward an official record, but the record could not settle every claim made against the past.

The cases permit comparison because each reveals a different point at which conditions became decisions. Ecology could make a prison unhealthy, but it did not select contaminated water as an acceptable condition. Inequality could define who counted as a political participant, but it did not determine whether soldiers would challenge that definition. Technology could enlarge a weapon’s range or accelerate administration, but it did not choose the target. Identity could divide a population into enemies and citizens, but it did not independently issue an order, falsify a report, or grant amnesty.

Leadership mattered where a person or small group could convert circumstances into policy. Fairfax’s authority, Confederate prison administration, and Tutu’s public stewardship each demonstrate that power. Leadership exercised its reach through offices, records, money, transport, armed personnel, legal rules, and followers prepared to carry decisions out. A leader’s choice became historical force only through an institution capable of transmitting it.

Contingency entered when alternatives remained open: a different settlement at Putney, a different policy toward prisoners, a different bargain between punishment and disclosure in South Africa. These alternatives differed in likelihood, yet remained available to be argued over, proposed, or rejected. Comparison can identify that field without pretending to know the full result of every road not taken.

The chapter’s method answers explanations that reduce violence to one master cause. Ecology can create pressure without selecting neglect. Inequality can structure exclusion without determining revolt. Technology can increase capacity without fixing purpose. Identity can define an enemy without issuing every command. Leadership can redirect institutions, but only within the resources and rules that make action possible. Contingency names the openings through which a different decision might enter.

Historical comparison can show how violence becomes organized when people, resources, information, and command are joined. It can trace the institutional choices that make prisoners more or less likely to survive, that widen or restrict political participation, or that exchange punishment for disclosure. It cannot calculate what any alternative would have produced in full. It cannot recover every motive, measure every unrecorded injury, or establish that a different decision would have prevented every later death.

At Putney, the army’s power opened a political argument and narrowed its settlement. At Andersonville, war created scarcity while officials selected policies that made prisoners more or less likely to survive it. In South Africa, testimony and conditional amnesty turned part of a violent past into a public record without transforming that record into complete justice. The comparison identifies conditions that enabled violence and decisions that selected restraint, neglect, or repair; it does not supply a final measure for the lives that another decision might have saved.
